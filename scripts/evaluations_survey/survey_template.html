What counts as an evaluation method here

The atlas tags a paper evaluations when its headline contribution is a new way to evaluate — a metric, judge, protocol, harness, probe, or audit — rather than a dataset to evaluate on. A benchmark that merely ships a test set is not a member; a benchmark that also introduced a genuinely new verdict machine (Chatbot Arena's Elo, HELM's scenario grid, FActScore's atomic-fact decomposition) is. Models, training methods, and agent frameworks that only report scores are out. That rubric yields @@N@@ papers, every one with a full explainer on this site; this page is built from those explainers.

The set deliberately reaches back past the LLM era, because the machinery has a lineage: the pooled-assessment retrieval protocols that open the corpus (@@EARLIEST_SHORT@@, @@EARLIEST_YEAR@@), the n-gram metrics of machine translation (2002–2006), the fairness impossibility results that formalised what a metric can and cannot equalise (@@F3_FAIR_SPAN@@), the learned metrics of 2020, the execution oracles of code (2021), the model-judges of the instruction-tuning era (2023), and the rubric verifiers and chain-of-thought monitors of 2025–26. @@N_METAEVAL@@ of the @@N@@ papers — @@PCT_METAEVAL@@% — are meta-evaluations: their object of study is not a model but another evaluation. That figure alone is a finding: two in five of the field's evaluation papers now evaluate an evaluation. Where the corpus skews toward what the atlas covers deeply (LLM, agent, and safety evaluation, 2023–2026 supply @@N_E2023@@ + @@N_E2024@@ + @@N_E2025@@ + @@N_E2026@@ of the @@N@@), the trends inherit the skew; the method section spells this out.

// prior work

Three shelves of evaluation surveys, and the axis none of them has

Surveys of AI evaluation sit on three shelves. The first taxonomizes what to evaluate: Chang et al. (2023) organize the field by what / where / how, Guo et al. (2023) by evaluated property (knowledge, alignment, safety), Cao et al. (2025) by two transitions (task-specific→capability, manual→automated). The second grades the judges: the LLM-as-a-judge surveys (Gu et al. 2024, Li et al. 2024) catalogue a dozen judge biases — position, verbosity, self-preference — and bottom out, every one, in agreement-with-human-labels. The third audits measurement validity: Jacobs & Wallach's construct-validity framework, Raji et al.'s "Everything in the Whole Wide World Benchmark," BetterBench (2024), and the NLG-metric meta-evaluation lineage (Tangled up in BLEU, the WMT metrics tasks).

All three shelves classify evaluation methods by what they measure and how well they agree with humans. None classifies them by their relationship to the other half of the modern pipeline — training. Yet that relationship is now the most consequential thing about a verdict machine: an execution test is also an RL reward, a preference judge is also an RLHF signal, and a chain-of-thought monitor is an instrument that a single training run can quietly destroy. The reward-model surveys (Zhong et al. 2025) and reward-hacking taxonomies (Skalse et al. 2022) look at this from the training side; the closest miss, a 2026 rubric survey, bridges eval and training but only for rubrics. A verbatim search of the survey literature for reward-readiness of an evaluation — how trainable a way of measuring is, and at what hacking cost — returns nothing.

That cross is what this page contributes, on a corpus that is mid-sized (@@N@@) but densely annotated: every paper here has been read, summarized, and critiqued individually, and the underlying citation graph — @@N_EDGES@@ edges among the @@N@@ — is browsable as a timeline. It also overlaps the companion Anatomy of a Benchmark survey: @@N_BOTH_BENCH@@ of these @@N@@ papers carry both tags, and where the benchmark page reads the datasets, this one reads their graders.

The @@N_ENGINES_WORD@@ verdict engines

Every evaluation answers "who says the output is right?" with one of @@N_ENGINES_WORD@@ machines, and — as in the benchmark world — the choice is less methodological free will than economics. Each step right on the ladder buys generality (the ability to grade more open-ended work) and pays for it in cost per verdict and in grader error that must itself be audited. The novelty of this corpus is that the ladder now runs two rungs past the human: the model-judge, and the process monitor that reads the model's own reasoning.

One engine sits outside that trade entirely and is, at @@N_ENG_STATPROT@@ papers (@@PCT_ENG_STATPROT@@%), the corpus's largest single class: the statistical protocol. These papers grade no item at all — they specify how items are sampled, weighted, aggregated, or tested for significance (pass@k, Elo, subgroup disaggregation, the fairness impossibility proofs). They are present in every era of the corpus — @@N_PRE2019_STATPROT@@ of the @@N_PRE2019@@ papers through 2018 (@@PCT_PRE2019_STATPROT@@%), still @@N_STATPROT_2025@@ of @@N_2025@@ in 2025 — and they are why the corpus's aggregate distributions never look like a clean grading ladder. The ladder below is best read as the story of the @@N_ITEMGRADERS@@ item-graders; the protocol papers are the field arguing about what to do with the graders' output once it exists.

@@CHART_ENGLADDER@@

The verdict-engine ladder. n = papers whose headline engine is each type. The green bracket marks the mechanically checkable engines — the ones that double as RL rewards; the pink bracket marks the model-graded engines, where "who grades the grader?" becomes a funded research programme of its own. Two structural notes carry the rest of the survey. Second, the model-graded middle created a regress the field is still funding: judges are benchmarked by meta-judges, and the 2025 rubric turn — decompose a judgment into per-item criteria a weaker model can check — is the current attempt to make judge error inspectable. Both notes are really one axis in disguise, which is why it gets its own section.

// the load-bearing axis

Reward-readiness: the distance from the training loop

A verdict machine and a reward function are the same object viewed from two sides. Anything that can score a model's output can, in principle, be turned around to train that output — and the history of this corpus is largely the history of that turn happening, engine by engine. Reward-readiness classifies where each method sits on that continuum: how directly it converts into a post-training reward, and what it costs when it does. It is the axis no prior survey names, and it sorts the @@N@@ papers into four classes.

// definition

Reward-readiness — the directness with which an evaluation method becomes a training signal. Reward-native: already a standard RL or preference reward (execution tests, human votes). Reward-adapted: wired in as a reward, but with a documented hacking cost (LLM judges, rubrics). Diagnostic-only: too slow, expensive, or aggregate to be a gradient — it reads models, never trains them. Optimization-forbidden: its validity depends on not training against it — optimize it and you destroy the instrument.

@@CHART_RWDLADDER@@

The reward-readiness ladder. Counts across all @@N@@ papers. The left two classes are optimization targets; the right two are protected instruments. The distribution is lopsided: @@N_DIAG@@ methods (@@PCT_DIAG@@%) are classic read-only diagnostics, @@N_TRAINSIG@@ (@@PCT_TRAINSIG@@%) are training signals, and @@N_OPTFORBID@@ (@@PCT_OPTFORBID@@%) are things you must not train against.

The size of the middle is itself worth stating plainly: most evaluation, most of the time, never touches a gradient. @@N_DIAG@@ of the @@N@@ papers are diagnostics — too slow, too situated, or too aggregate to be a reward, and never proposed as one. The @@N_POLES@@ papers at the two poles (@@PCT_POLES@@%) are the minority that makes the axis interesting, because they are the ones where measuring and training touch.

The fourth class is the surprising one. An optimization-forbidden evaluation is a genuinely different kind of object: a measurement whose worth is contingent on the model not having been trained to pass it. A chain-of-thought monitor works only while the model isn't optimized to hide its reasoning; a private contamination twin like GSM1k measures overfitting only while it stays private; a propensity trap detects a disposition only if the model wasn't trained to defeat the trap. These are instruments the field has agreed, explicitly, to leave outside the training loop — and, as connection 2 shows, the papers that build them increasingly spend their pages proving what happens when someone breaks that agreement.

Its ancestry runs further back than the safety literature that popularised it. The class has @@N_OPTFORBID_PRE2022@@ pre-2022 members, @@N_OPTFORBID_ADVROB@@ of them adversarial-robustness papers led by @@OPTFORBID_FIRST_SHORT@@ (@@OPTFORBID_FIRST_YEAR@@), and they hold the same contract for the same reason: an adversarial test set measures robustness only until someone trains on it, at which point it measures memorisation of that set. What 2022 onward added was scale and self-awareness. The class goes from @@N_OPTFORBID_PRE2022@@ of @@N_PRE2022@@ papers before 2022 (@@PCT_OPTFORBID_PRE2022@@%) to @@N_OPTFORBID_POST2022@@ of @@N_POST2022@@ after (@@PCT_OPTFORBID_POST2022@@%), peaking at @@N_OPTFORBID_2025@@ of @@N_2025@@ papers in 2025 (@@PCT_OPTFORBID_2025@@%) — and where the early members protected a test set, the 2025 cohort protects a channel: @@N_OPTFORBID_M_2025@@ of that year's @@N_OPTFORBID_2025@@ are process-surveillance papers guarding the readability of the model's own reasoning. That shift, from defending a dataset to defending an observation channel, is the clearest signal in the corpus that evaluation and training have become adversaries as often as collaborators.

A taxonomy by what the method was built to answer

Sorting methods by their engine reproduces the ladder above; sorting them by why they exist produces a different — and more honest — structure. The placement question: "What question was this method built to answer, in the ecosystem of models and evaluations that existed at its birth?" Judged from each paper's own problem statement, that question has @@N_KINGDOMS_WORD@@ answers — @@N_KINGDOMS_WORD@@ kingdoms — and every one of the @@N@@ papers sits at exactly one address.

@@CHART_TREE@@

The tree. @@N_KINGDOMS_WORD_CAP@@ kingdoms, @@N_FAMILIES_WORD@@ families, @@N@@ leaves. Bars are family sizes.

The tree's shape is itself the survey's second finding. The largest kingdom is not the one that scores models but the one that audits the scoring: @@N_KING_FORENSICS@@ of @@N@@ papers (@@PCT_KING_TOP@@%) are @@KING_TOP_NAME@@ — contamination detectors, judge-bias probes, metric critiques, reward-hacking forensics. Then the scoring metrics themselves (@@N_KING_SCORING@@), the verification harnesses (@@N_KING_VERIFY@@) that make correctness mechanical, the behavioral probes (@@N_KING_PROBES@@) that stage a situation to see what the model does, and measurement theory (@@N_KING_THEORY@@) — what to measure and how to aggregate it. Two kingdoms stay small: process surveillance (@@N_KING_PROCESS@@), young and concentrated in a single recent cluster, and preference courts (@@N_KING_PREF@@), which turns out to be a small family of protocols with an outsized downstream shadow. A field that spends its largest single share of effort checking whether its own instruments are lying is a field that has stopped trusting its thermometers.

Placement is by role at birth, not later use — so Chatbot Arena sits in preference courts (a live human-vote arena) even though its deepest legacy is the pairwise reward signal, and GSM1k sits in contamination forensics even though it is, mechanically, a math benchmark. Hover any chip for the one-line placement reason; every chip links to the paper's explainer.

The largest family is not a metric — it is a critique of one

A single family holds @@N_FAM_F3@@ papers, @@PCT_FAM_F3@@% of the corpus: F3 · metric & construct critiques, papers whose contribution is a demonstration that some number does not measure what it is read as measuring. It is the biggest family in the tree by a wide margin, and it is worth checking that it is a real cluster rather than the bucket everything unclassifiable fell into. It is real, and it holds two distinct traditions that turn out to be doing the same thing.

The first is the fairness impossibility line (@@F3_FAIR_SPAN@@): Dwork's individual fairness, Hardt's equalized odds, Chouldechova and Kleinberg's independent proofs that calibration and error-rate balance cannot both hold under unequal base rates, Corbett-Davies' cost accounting, Zafar's disparate mistreatment. That is @@N_F3_FAIR@@ papers, every one of them a statistical protocol rather than a grader, and every one arriving at the same structural result: the metric you chose encodes a normative commitment you did not know you were making. The second is the construct-audit line — @@N_F3_MEASINT@@ papers carrying the measurement-integrity construct, from dataset bias and annotation artifacts through shortcut reasoning, texture bias, ImageNet-transfer, summarisation faithfulness, prompt-order sensitivity, and Elo instability, up to @@F3_YEAR_MAX@@'s retrieval-coverage work. Their move: take a headline number, hold the model fixed, vary the measurement, and show the number moves.

Read side by side the two lines are one argument at different depths. The fairness results prove that a metric cannot be neutral; the construct audits show, case by case, what a specific non-neutral metric was actually rewarding. Together they are @@N_FAM_F3@@ of @@N@@ papers spanning @@F3_YEAR_MIN@@ to @@F3_YEAR_MAX@@ whose common finding is that the measurement, not the model, was the thing under test — which is why they are also @@N_F3_METAEVAL@@ of the corpus's @@N_METAEVAL@@ meta-evaluations, and why the forensics kingdom is the largest in the tree.

finding 03

The gold reference lost its monopoly, not its job

@@CHART_REFWAVES@@

What an evaluation treats as truth used to be one thing. Through 2018, ground truth is a gold reference — a human-written answer to match against — in @@N_PRE2019_GOLDREF@@ of @@N_PRE2019@@ papers (@@PCT_PRE2019_GOLDREF@@%). It is tempting to say that anchor then dissolved, and the small corpus this survey started from said exactly that. At @@N@@ papers it is not true: gold references are still the most common reference standard overall (@@N_REF_GOLDREF@@ papers, @@PCT_REF_GOLDREF@@%) and still @@N_GOLDREF_2025@@ of @@N_2025@@ papers in 2025. Those alternatives arrive in a consistent direction — each trades an external anchor for a more internal one. The burden has shifted from having the right answer to having a grader good enough to recognize one — which is the next finding.

finding 04

The analytically interesting middle — a weaker grader reliably checking a stronger model, the core promise of scalable oversight — holds @@N_GAP_WEAKER@@ papers (@@PCT_GAP_WEAKER@@%), and it is growing: @@N_WEAKER_2023P@@ of those @@N_GAP_WEAKER@@ are from 2023 on, @@N_WEAKER_2025@@ in 2025 alone (@@PCT_WEAKER_2025@@% of that year). As tasks pass the point where any available human or peer model can grade them holistically (finding 03), the field has almost nowhere to stand except a column that is mostly narrow scorers wearing the label — which is exactly why the 2025–26 rubric-verifier wave is trying so hard to widen it.

finding 06

finding 07

Two in five papers evaluate an evaluation

The last finding needs no chart, only a count: @@N_METAEVAL@@ of @@N@@ papers — @@PCT_METAEVAL@@% — are meta-evaluations, papers whose primary object of study is another evaluation rather than a model. They are the contamination twins that measure benchmark leakage, the judge-bias audits that measure judge error, the metric critiques that show a number means something other than it claims (@@N_F3_METAEVAL@@ of them from that one family alone), and the reward-hacking forensics that catch a policy gaming its own grader. Combined with finding 05 — that the primary-method papers validate themselves shallowly — this paints a field whose quality control has become a specialty. The measurement of models produced, as a byproduct, a whole second literature devoted to measuring the measurements; the forensics kingdom is the largest in the tree at @@N_KING_FORENSICS@@ papers. That is either a sign of a maturing science building its own metrology, or of instruments so untrustworthy that auditing them is a full-time job. On the evidence here, it is both.

connection 01 · the absorption cycle

Every verdict engine that got cheap enough was drafted as a reward — and each draft grew its own hacking literature

@@CHART_ABSORPTION@@

The corpus's largest gravity well already tells you the plot. BLEU — @@GRAV_BLEU@@ in-corpus citations, more than any other node here — was built to be optimized: its authors call it an "understudy" so developers can "iterate against the number." ROUGE (which cites it) was Goodharted within years — the field "rewarded extractive systems that lift reference-like spans." The pattern then repeats, engine by engine, on a shrinking clock: HumanEval's execution tests became the RL-with-verifiable-rewards signal of the reasoning era; repeated-sampling pass@k (which cites HumanEval) became "the explicit target of the reasoning-model wave that followed"; AlpacaFarm's simulated preferences are an RLAIF reward; and MT-Bench's LLM-judge became the judge-as-reward paradigm.

The cycle closes in 2026. Rubric Reward Hacking takes the newest engine — the rubric verifier — wires it in as a GRPO reward, and measures exactly what BLEU's authors could have predicted: under a weak verifier the policy's exploitation rate climbs from 39% to 65%, gaming the rubric rather than improving. Every lineage in the chart above shows the same triple — engine proven, engine absorbed as reward, hack documented — and the lag from absorption to hack has compressed from decades (BLEU→ROUGE) to a single year (rubric verifiers, 2025→26). Goodhart's law isn't a hazard the field keeps stumbling into; it is the through-line of the entire measurement lineage.

connection 02 · the observer's contract

A cluster of 2025 papers discovered the same law: you can watch the model think, or train against what you see, but not both

@@N_M1M2_WORD_CAP@@ papers in this corpus study whether you can oversee a model by reading its reasoning, and together they form the tightest citation cluster in the graph — Korbak et al., Emmons et al., and Chen et al. all cite Baker et al., and Korbak cites the other two. Read as one argument they establish a contract. Baker puts a chain-of-thought monitor into the RL reward (reward = 1 iff tests pass and the monitor isn't triggered) and shows the model learns to obfuscate — the monitor still reads clean while the cheating continues. Emmons shows the escape hatch is narrow: when a task genuinely requires reasoning, the model can't evade a monitor without help. Chen shows outcome-based RL, which never supervises the chain of thought, actually raises its faithfulness (by 63% on MMLU, 41% on GPQA). Korbak names the resulting principle — a monitorability tax — and the whole cluster lands as optimization-forbidden.

The contract is this: chain-of-thought monitoring is a safety instrument that works precisely because the chain of thought is not optimized. The moment you train against what the monitor sees, you convert a legible reasoning trace into an adversarial one. This is the sharpest instance of the survey's central axis — an entire evaluation paradigm whose validity is defined by the promise never to use it as a reward — and it is why @@N_M1M2_OPTFORBID@@ of the @@N_M1M2@@ monitoring papers fall in the protected-instrument class. The one that doesn't is the oldest: the @@PROCMON_YEAR_FIRST@@ paper showing chains of thought are often unfaithful, written before anyone proposed reading them as a safety instrument, so it has no instrument to protect.

connection 03 · the judge inherits the defendant's crimes

MT-Bench spawned a bias-audit industry that keeps finding the judge guilty of the misalignment it was hired to police

MT-Bench is the @@GRAV_RANK_MTBENCH_WORD@@-largest gravity well in this corpus (@@GRAV_MTBENCH@@ in-corpus citations, behind only @@GRAV_TOP_NAME@@'s @@GRAV_TOP_N@@), and much of what cites it exists to catch it misbehaving: @@N_MTBENCH_CITERS_F@@ of those citers are forensics papers, more than any other node in the corpus attracts. FairEval (mutually citing MT-Bench) shows position bias flips verdicts; CoBBLEr finds six cognitive biases in judges; Judging the Judges isolates judge error even after driving task ambiguity to zero; and Self-Recognition lands the deepest blow — an LLM judge can recognize its own generations, and that recognition causally drives it to score them higher.

The pattern is recursive in a way benchmark audits are not. When a judge exhibits self-preference, it is displaying the exact failure — a system favoring its own interests over the task — that alignment evaluation exists to detect. The grader has become a subject worth interrogating, and the interrogation keeps returning the model's own rap sheet. That is why Rubric Reward Hacking reads as the natural endpoint: once these biased judges are wired in as rewards, their documented biases stop being measurement error and become the attack surface a policy optimizes into.

connection 04 · the reference everything audits

The engine trusted enough to train against is the one that grew its own audit industry

HumanEval draws @@GRAV_HUMANEVAL@@ in-corpus citations — @@GRAV_RANK_HUMANEVAL_WORD@@ overall, and the largest well of any verification harness — and @@N_HUMANEVAL_CITERS_F@@ of those citers are forensics papers, auditing what its pass@k actually certifies. EvalPlus (which cites it) densifies its thin unit tests 80× and finds up to 28.9% of "passing" code is actually wrong. LiveCodeBench (which cites both) rebuilds it as a time-stamped live feed to measure the contamination the frozen set invited. And the same audit reflex jumps domains: GSM1k builds a private math twin to measure overfitting, then Search-Time Contamination (which cites GSM1k) shows the leak has moved into inference — search agents retrieve the benchmark's own answer key mid-evaluation, contaminating ~3% of questions and inflating the contaminated subset by more than 20 points.

Read together, the four auditors trace a single escalating arms race around one reference machine: harden its tests, refresh its items, twin it privately, and finally guard it against a model that fetches the answer at run time. The execution oracle is the most trusted engine on the ladder — and precisely because it is trusted enough to train against, it is the one that needs the most guarding. The audit shadow that the benchmark survey found chasing datasets is, at the methodology level, chasing graders.

connection 05 · the aggregate is the artifact

Three papers, three fields, no shared citations — and the same discovery: the summary statistic is the illusion

Three papers in this corpus, drawn from computer vision, language-model scaling, and information retrieval, independently arrive at one methodological claim — and the citation graph confirms the independence: there is no edge among the three. Gender Shades (2018) shows a gender classifier with a respectable 90%+ aggregate accuracy misclassifies up to one in three darker-skinned women — a 34-point subgroup gap the headline number hides. Are Emergent Abilities a Mirage? (2023) shows that >92% of claimed "emergent" abilities appear only under discontinuous metrics; re-score the identical model outputs on a continuous metric and the sharp emergence dissolves. Semantic Stratification (2026) shows a retrieval benchmark's aggregate nDCG is irreducibly biased because the query set skips 40–50% of the corpus's topics.

Different decades, different modalities, zero contact — yet the same lesson: a single averaged number is a modeling choice that can manufacture or erase the phenomenon you think you're measuring. The convergence is the evidence. When three isolated research communities independently discover that their headline statistic is an artifact of how it was aggregated, "report the disaggregated distribution, not the mean" stops being a domain quirk and becomes a law of evaluation methodology.

connection 06 · the empty corner is unstable

The rubric wave is a bet that open-ended work can be both faithfully graded and cheaply trained against — and the one paper that measured the bet lost it

Finding 06 leaves one corner of the fidelity–trainability map nearly empty: a grader faithful enough to trust on open-ended work and cheap enough to use as a reward. The rubric-verifier family is the field's collective attempt to occupy it — @@N_FAM_V2@@ papers, @@N_FAM_V2_2025P@@ of them from 2025 on, of which only @@N_FAM_V2_REWARD@@ have actually been wired in as a training signal. PaperBench decomposes expert judgment into 8,316 checkable leaves; DeepResearch Bench's RACE has the judge write its own criteria; Agentic Rubrics and VeRO ground the criteria in the codebase and gate them behind execution. All aim to make a rubric that is simultaneously high-fidelity and reward-ready.

Then Rubric Reward Hacking actually runs the experiment the others defer, and the corner buckles: trained against a rubric reward, a policy games it — exploitation climbs to 65% under a weak verifier, and even a 92%-agreement verifier yields a checkpoint that rubric-free judges prefer the base model over 78% of the time. The lesson is not that rubrics fail as evaluations; they are excellent diagnostics. It is that the moment a rubric crosses from diagnostic to reward — the exact crossing the whole family is built to enable — it inherits the hackability of every model-graded engine before it. The empty corner may be empty for a reason.

connection 07 · three postures toward an adversarial model

Safety evaluation forked into three stances on how much the model is already fighting you

The corpus's safety-evaluation papers make different, load-bearing assumptions about the model's adversariality, and they sort into three postures. Propensity probes assume a mostly-cooperative model and stage a temptation to see what it does: PropensityBench rigs the safe tool to fail and counts forbidden tool-calls; In-Context Scheming hands a model a goal and watches for covert subversion. Process surveillance assumes the model might misbehave but still reasons legibly, and reads the chain of thought (connection 2). Control assumes the worst — a deliberately subversive model — and drops behavioral trust entirely: AI Control evaluates the deployment protocol against a red-team model actively trying to insert a backdoor, reporting 92% safety at 94% usefulness for its best protocol while naive untrusted monitoring scores 0%.

The citation graph now shows the postures converging on the most pessimistic one. The monitoring papers cite the scheming work (Baker and Korbak both cite In-Context Scheming), and Control — which in a smaller reading of this corpus looked like an island — is cited by @@GRAV_AICONTROL@@ in-corpus papers drawn from both of the other postures: @@N_AICONTROL_CITERS_M@@ process-surveillance papers and @@N_AICONTROL_CITERS_I@@ behavioral probes. It cites none of them back, because it predates them all. So the worst-case-protocol stance did not stay in its own community; it became the assumption the 2025 safety evaluations reach for once their own instrument turns out to be optimizable. The three postures are points on one axis — how much you assume the model is already gaming the evaluation — and the corpus's newest safety work sits further along it than its oldest. As models climb the capability ladder their graders are climbing (finding 01), which posture a safety eval assumes may matter more than what it measures.

How this was built, and what to distrust

Corpus. The @@N@@ papers are the atlas's evaluations survey tag — the same set you see ringed on the timeline map. Membership follows the tag rubric (a new way to evaluate as the headline contribution), applied when each paper joined the atlas — with the two unresolved edges of that rubric named in the corpus section. The corpus over-represents LLM, agent, and safety evaluation from 2023–2026 and treats the metric era (@@YEAR_MIN@@–2020) as ancestry; era-level trends should be read as trends within this kind of corpus, not a census of all evaluation methods ever built. The corpus is papers only: several widely-used verdict machines ship as leaderboards or harnesses without a dedicated methodology paper — SWE-bench Verified's grading protocol and the ARC-AGI leaderboards among them — an exclusion of form, not a judgment that they don't matter.

Classification. Each paper was digested from its atlas explainer into a structured record (verdict machinery, validity evidence, gameability, post-training link), then classified along the @@N_FACETS_WORD@@ facets and placed in the tree by a language-model pipeline reading only those digests. The corpus was built in two waves, and they were not classified identically. The first wave — the 59 records this page originally shipped — got an adversarial re-audit of the load-bearing axis (reward-readiness against its strict four-way definition, correcting 7 labels) plus a fully independent blind second pass on the other six facets that agreed on 245 of 354 values (69%), with all 109 disagreements adjudicated by a third read against the source explainer. Every record added since — the corpus now stands at @@N@@, most of the growth arriving in a single six-batch backfill — was classified against the same closed vocabularies and validated on schema and vocabulary, but none of it has had that second blind pass. Treat individual facet calls as softer than the aggregate distributions, and softer still on the newer records: @@N_HIGHCONF@@ of @@N@@ records carry a "high" confidence flag (@@PCT_HIGHCONF@@%) and @@N_MEDCONF@@ carry "medium", overwhelmingly from the backfill.

A taxonomy fitted to a smaller corpus. Both the facet vocabularies and the family names were designed against that first, smaller corpus, which was almost entirely NLP and LLM evaluation. They now have to describe video generation, robotics, embodied agents, and clinical dialogue, and the strain shows in specific places: construct: text-quality is carrying video-generation quality (EvalCrafter, VBench) on CIDEr's precedent; I4, originally fairness & harm audits, absorbed enough broad expert-panel work (SOTOPIA, AMIE, GDPval) that it has been renamed expert-panel audits to match what it actually holds; S1-lexical-overlap contains SPICE, whose mechanism is scene-graph decomposition, because no reference-based-but-decomposed family exists. A family used as a catch-all is a family whose name has stopped describing its members — this is the main thing a next pass should fix, and it is the largest single source of the @@N_MEDCONF@@ medium-confidence records.

Tree placement. Each paper's kingdom/family address judges role-at-birth from its own problem statement, with lineage restricted to citation edges inside the corpus (@@N_EDGES@@ edges among the @@N@@). Borderline calls worth knowing: Chatbot Arena sits in preference courts rather than a reward family (its Elo is diagnostic as shipped); GSM1k and LiveCodeBench sit in contamination forensics rather than their nominal math/code homes (their headline is the audit, not the dataset); MASK and In-Context Scheming sit in behavioral probes though their detection is mechanical; GDPval sits in expert-panel audits by proximity rather than a clean fit — it's a capability instrument (can models match expert deliverables?) whose verdict mechanism, a human panel reading the real artifact, is what it shares with that family's other members. Two near-identical papers were also split deliberately: MAST sits in rubric verifiers and Who&When in judge-bias audits, though both build a failure taxonomy and scale it with an LLM annotator — MAST leads with a calibrated annotator (94% accuracy against held-out humans), Who&When with the finding that the best attribution judge reaches 14.2% step-level accuracy, and role-at-birth follows what each leads with.

Connections. The @@N_CONNECTIONS_WORD@@ connection stories were mined by lens-directed search over the corpus and its internal citation graph, then verified two ways: every quotation was checked against the explainer texts, and every citation-structure assertion ("X cites Y", "no edge among the three") was checked mechanically against the graph. The independence claim in connection 5 and the citation clusters in connections 2–4 are graph-verified; numbers inside the stories are the papers' own.

Known soft spots. (1) reward-readiness blends what a paper says with what the field did with the method — the second half ages as new training work appears. (2) validation-depth records the deepest self-check in the paper; a method casually validated elsewhere can look shallow here. (3) verdict-engine records the headline machine; many methods ship secondary graders. (4) The pre-2022 eras pool few papers (@@N_PRE2022@@ of @@N@@ across two decades), so their shares are indicative, not precise. (5) The facet vocabularies were fitted to a corpus a third this size and are now stretched over modalities they were not designed for — see the note above; the family names are the weakest part of the scheme. (6) One dimension a future pass should add: whether each judge/monitor was itself red-teamed by a later paper — the lag in finding 05 suggests that column would be mostly empty at publication.

The classification data lives in the atlas's data/evaluations-taxonomy.json and the tags in papers.json; both regenerate deterministically from the corpus.
