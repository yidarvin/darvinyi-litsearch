The Anatomy of a Benchmark

Every benchmark on the atlas — 369 evaluation papers spanning TREC-8 QA Track (2000) to SWE Atlas (2026) — classified along eight facets, then placed in a single taxonomy tree by the question each was built to answer, with lineage mined from the citation graph itself. Read one paper and you learn a benchmark; put all of them in one structure and you can see what no single paper shows — including nine connections that only appear across papers.

// the corpus

What counts as a benchmark here

The atlas tags a paper benchmarks when a named benchmark, evaluation dataset, or evaluation environment is its headline contribution — the thing a survey would cite it for. Models, training methods, and agent frameworks that merely report benchmark numbers don't qualify. That rubric yields 369 papers, every one of which has a full explainer on this site; this page is built from those explainers.

The corpus is curated, not exhaustive — but it is not an LLM-era corpus. 86 of the 369 papers (23%) were published before 2021, and they are not there only as NLU ancestry. They include the question-answering and dialogue generations that predate the LLM era (TREC-8 QA Track in 2000, PASCAL RTE, DailyDialog, PersonaChat), the vision canon (ImageNet, CIFAR-10/100, LFW, MS COCO, VQA), a deep-RL and robotics block (ALE in 2013, OpenAI Gym, Meta-World, Hanabi, RLBench and robosuite), multi-agent game environments, speech, and the text-to-image and text-to-video evaluation cluster. That older block prefigures the shape of today's agent benchmarks mostly without a citation path to them — see finding 04. Anything this page says about “eras” is therefore a statement about a quarter-century of measurement practice, not about the last three years of it.

86 of the 369 also carry the evaluations tag — papers that shipped a new way to evaluate along with their dataset (Chatbot Arena's Elo protocol, HELM's scenario grid, FActScore's atomic-fact decomposition). Where the corpus skews, the findings inherit the skew; the method section spells this out.

Four papers in it are, on inspection, not benchmark papers by the rubric above, and saying so is more useful than quietly dropping them. MLPerf Training measures hardware and software throughput to a fixed accuracy gate — its own explainer says “this isn't a capability eval.” LCCC and MedDialog are pretraining corpora whose only evaluation runs on someone else's benchmark. AgentSkillOS's 30-task benchmark is never named anywhere in the paper and is graded by models from the same family that produced the answers.

The tell was mechanical, and it generalizes: the classifier reported that for MLPerf every facet was a forced choice. When every facet of a record is a forced choice, the schema is telling you the paper isn't the kind of object the schema describes. They stay tagged (retagging is a graph-wide operation, not a page-level one) and stay in every count on this page; where one of them is the reason a cell is non-empty, this page says so.

// prior work

Two shelves of benchmark surveys, and the gap between them

Existing surveys of AI evaluation sit on two shelves that barely touch. The first shelf taxonomizes what benchmarks measure: Chang et al. (2023) organize the field by what / where / how to evaluate, Guo et al. (2023) by evaluated property (knowledge, alignment, safety, domain), and HELM (2022) — the most rigorous of the family — by a scenario × metric grid it then deliberately fills. The second shelf grades how well benchmarks are built: BetterBench (2024) scores 24 benchmarks on 46 lifecycle criteria, McIntosh et al. (2024) find 22 of 23 prominent benchmarks vulnerable on the same integrity grounds, Bean et al. (2025) audit construct validity across 445 benchmarks, and the Agentic Benchmark Checklist (2025) shows that 7 of 10 audited agentic benchmarks had unsound graders.

The agent-evaluation surveys that appeared in 2025 — Yehudai et al., Kapoor et al.'s AI Agents That Matter — added structural vocabulary (dataset vs. environment, offline vs. online, tool access) but each survey coins its own terms, and none quantifies the axes it names. Meanwhile the dimension that turns out to matter most for where benchmarks are going — how long the task is — was only made measurable by METR's time-horizon work (2025), and has never been applied to a benchmark corpus.

What no prior survey does is cross the shelves at paper level: place every benchmark in a capability map and record its grading machinery, task horizon, tool surface, provenance, and contamination posture — then look at the joint distribution over time. That cross is what this page contributes, on a corpus of 369 papers that is densely annotated rather than merely enumerated: every paper here has been read, summarized, and critiqued individually, and the underlying citation graph is browsable as a timeline. Two further anchors for what follows: Biderman et al.'s reproducibility lessons (2024) and the benchmark-saturation study (2026), which finds roughly half of all benchmarks saturated. This corpus's own pre-2021 cohort is saturated at a far higher rate — 55% (47 of 86) against 19% corpus-wide — and the gap is exactly what finding 07 predicts: saturation concentrates in short-horizon work, and all but 2 of those 86 pre-2021 papers pose a task an expert finishes inside half an hour (the exceptions are TAPE (2019) and MLPerf Training (2020)).

One honest limit of facet 1. The domain vocabulary was designed for a corpus of LLM and agent evaluation, and the corpus outgrew it: three domains it now contains cannot be named. Robotics and manipulation are recorded as multimodal, multi-agent RL and games as reasoning, theory of mind as social-moral — every affected record says so in its notes, and the papers are real members of the corpus, not strays (the embodied-operation family alone holds 40 of them). 16 of the vocabulary's 17 values are actually used, and the filter below offers only those. Read the domain chips as “nearest available label,” and read any domain-conditioned claim on this page as conditioned on that.

Defining “complexity”: the task horizon

“Complexity” is the word everyone reaches for and nobody defines. The literature operationalizes it at least five incommensurable ways — reasoning steps, compositional depth, context length, search-space size, IRT difficulty — none of which lets you compare a multiple-choice exam to a week of freelance software work. The definition used here is the one that does:

// definition

Task horizon — the wall-clock time a competent human domain-expert would need to complete one typical task instance, reading and artifact production included. It is the only complexity currency that spans the whole corpus, and it is the axis METR showed frontier-model ability climbs along at a measurable rate (a ~7-month doubling time). Five buckets: L1 <1 minute · L2 1–30 minutes · L3 30 minutes–1 day · L4 1 day–2 weeks · L5 >2 weeks.

The horizon ladder. Counts and canonical examples per rung, across all 369 papers. Only a handful of papers (GDPval, RE-Bench, the METR suites) measure human time; everywhere else the rung is estimated from the paper's own task descriptions — see method.

The six grading families

Every benchmark answers “who says the output is right?” with one of six mechanisms, and the choice is less methodological free will than economics: each step right on the ladder below buys generality — the ability to grade open-ended work — and pays for it in cost per item and in grader error that must itself be audited.

The grading-cost ladder. Ordered by what it costs to grade one item. The reliability annotations are from the papers themselves: MT-Bench measured GPT-4-judge/human agreement at 85% (humans agree with each other 81%); PaperBench's judge reaches F1 0.83 against expert grading; the ABC audit found unsound graders in 7 of 10 agentic benchmarks it checked.

Two structural notes. First, the left end of the ladder is what the RL-with-verifiable-rewards era trains on — a benchmark whose grader is mechanical is also a reward function, which means the most verifiable benchmarks are precisely the ones models are being optimized against (Goodhart exposure is a property of the grading family, not the dataset). Second, the model-graded middle created a regress the field is still funding: judges are now benchmarked by meta-benchmarks (JudgeBench, RewardBench), and rubric-based grading is the current attempt to make judge error inspectable by decomposing it into checkable criteria.

// the tree

A taxonomy by evolutionary role

Sorting benchmarks by what they test reproduces the domain facet; sorting them by why they exist produces a different — and more honest — structure. The placement question used here: “What question was this benchmark built to answer, in the ecosystem of models and benchmarks that existed at its birth?” Judged from each paper's own problem statement, that question has seven answers — seven kingdoms — and every one of the 369 papers sits at exactly one address.

The tree. Seven kingdoms, twenty-five families, 369 leaves. Bars are family sizes.

The tree's shape is itself a finding, and the shape is lopsided and tilting. 202 of 369 papers (55%) are capability probes — the thing "benchmark" is usually assumed to mean — but that bare majority is an artifact of the corpus's history rather than its present: capability probes are 74% of pre-2021 entries, 47% of 2024–25 entries, and 41% of 2025 alone, where they are a clear minority. The other 167 papers (45%) do something more reflexive: audit other measurements (45), rehearse deployments (43), probe hazards (26), build walls designed not to fall (15), manufacture training signal (8), or work out how to evaluate at all (30). Benchmarking has become an ecosystem with predators, parasites, and decomposers — not a row of thermometers.

The families are lopsided too, and in a way that dates the corpus. The largest is A1-perception-parsing at 57 papers — recognize, parse, entail, retrieve — which is the pre-LLM vision-and-NLU stratum sitting under everything else; the second largest, A5-embodied-operation at 40, is the corpus's other big block and spans two generations that share a form more than a bibliography (deep-RL and robotics environments from 2013 onward, then LLM agent environments from 2022 — see finding 04). At the other end, 5 families hold fewer than five papers each, the smallest being B1-contamination-twins at 2. Small families are not noise: rebuilding a benchmark's test set from scratch to measure overfitting is expensive work few teams do, so the size of B1-contamination-twins is a fact about cost, not about whether the question is settled.

Placement is by role at birth, not later use — so HellaSwag sits in de-saturation sequels (it was built to resurrect a solved benchmark, and its adversarial-filtering construction matters more than its commonsense content — see connection 3), SWE-bench sits in program synthesis (the agents came later), and GSM8K sits in closed-form reasoning even though its deepest legacy is the verifier paradigm (connection 7). // findings

What the cross-tabs say

Seven findings, each read off a chart below. All numbers are shares of the 369-paper corpus by publication era; per-era counts are small enough that you should read slopes, not decimals.

finding 01

The grading waves — and the 2025 rubric turn

Headline grading mechanism by era. Eras pool small years (≤2018 n=39, 2019–20 n=47, 2021–22 n=49); single years from 2023 (n=55, 85, 80, 14).

Grading moves in waves, and each wave displaces the one before it rather than replacing it outright. Proxy metrics (BLEU, ROUGE, perplexity) gave way to exact match, which owned the early corpus — 54% of papers through 2018, 48 of 86 up to 2020 — and has fallen every era since 2020, to 28% in 2025. Execution-based programmatic grading arrived with the code and environment benchmarks (19 of 49 in 2021–22, 39%) and has held about a third of every cohort since: the largest grading family for new benchmarks in 2025 is programmatic, at 29 of 80 (36%). Holistic LLM-judging peaked in 2024 (15 papers, 18%) and has receded since.

The genuinely new wave is rubric-scored judging, and its slope is the story rather than its size: 4 papers total before 2025 (FActScore (2023), SOTOPIA (2023), TIFA (2023) and Agent-as-a-Judge (2024)), then 15 of 80 (19%) in a single year — HealthBench, MultiChallenge, PaperBench and ToolEyes among them — and 3 of 14 in 2026 (HiL-Bench, MCP-Atlas and SWE Atlas). Set against 29 new programmatic benchmarks in the same year, that is a turn, not a takeover — the earlier, 139-paper version of this page called it the largest family of 2025, and the fuller corpus says otherwise.

The wave structure has a single driver: each new capability regime produced outputs the previous grader couldn't score. Exact match broke on generation; unit tests broke on anything that isn't code; holistic judges broke on expert domains where “sounds good” diverges from “is correct.” The rubric turn is the field's current answer — decompose expert judgment into criteria a model can check — and it is also a quiet confession that the thing we now want to measure no longer has an answer key. That the mechanical families still grade most new benchmarks says the field has not abandoned verifiability; it has partitioned, sending checkable work to harnesses and open-ended work to rubrics.

finding 02

The verifiability frontier

Task horizon × grading family, all 369 papers. Hollow cells are empty. The mechanical families thin out with horizon but do not die: 7 exact-match and 24 programmatic papers survive at L3, and L4 is split 2 human · 1 rubric · 1 programmatic.

Read the grid diagonally and a boundary appears: cheap grading gets rarer as horizons lengthen, and it survives only where the answer stays short. Exact match owns L1 (54 of 88 papers) and thins to 7 of 41 at L3 — TAPE, LongICLBench, RULER, AgentDebug, BrowseComp, TRAIL and Who&When — none of which is a general-purpose answer key. They are short keys bolted onto long work: needle retrieval inside a very long context, search questions inverted until the answer is a verifiable string, and error localization in a recorded agent trace against a fixed label set. Programmatic grading peaks at L2–L3 (24 papers: unit tests, environment end-states) and nearly disappears at L4, where it survives in 1 paper — a game environment whose end state is still machine-readable. Nobody has built a test harness for “produce a competitive research paper.”

This is the survey's central claim: a benchmark can only pose tasks as long as it can afford to grade. Verification asymmetry — tasks that are hard to do but cheap to check — is the scarce resource of benchmark design, and the L4 row shows what happens when it runs out: GDPval pays experts for >1 hour of pairwise grading per item, PaperBench amortizes 33 person-weeks of rubric authoring. The grading-cost ladder from the taxonomy section is not a menu; at high horizons it is a budget constraint.

finding 03

The horizon climb — three rungs in 26 years, top rung empty

Share of new benchmark papers per era posing L3+ tasks (≥30 expert-minutes per instance).

Benchmarks are climbing the ladder their subjects climb. In the corpus's first two decades only 2 papers posed a task above L2 (TAPE (2019) and MLPerf Training (2020) — and one of those, MLPerf, is a throughput harness rather than a capability eval). The L3+ share was 4–12% through 2023–24, then jumped to 28% in 2025 and 7 of 14 papers in 2026 (50%). The mean rung moved from 1.3 (≤2018) to 2.5 (2026). That tracks the capability curve METR measured — as models' 50%-success horizon doubles every ~7 months, benchmarks below the frontier stop discriminating and the field re-builds one rung up.

But the climb is running out of ladder. L4 — day-to-week projects — contains 4 papers (BALROG, GDPval, PaperBench and RLI), all from 2025, and L5 holds 0: no benchmark in this corpus poses work beyond two expert-weeks. The binding constraints are grading cost at that altitude (finding 02) and item economics — a 1,000-item benchmark of week-long tasks is a thousand expert-weeks of ground truth. Whoever solves cheap verification of long-horizon work gets to write the first L5 benchmark, and probably the reward signal for the next training era with it.

finding 04

The interactive turn is a return — and multi-turn dialogue is the thing that got dropped

Task shape by era. Interactive environments: the model acts, the environment responds, repeat. Multi-turn: dialogue without an environment.

Interactive environments are not new here, and reading the recent climb as an invention is the mistake a smaller corpus invites. The first one is ALE (2013), and the 2019–20 cohort is already 19% interactive (9 papers, Meta-World, Hanabi, RLBench and robosuite among them) — but that block evaluates policies, in robotics simulators and multi-agent games. The two generations do touch — 31 in-corpus citations run between them — but through robotics and games (LIBERO and ManiSkill2 citing RLBench and Meta-World, BALROG citing ALE), not through the web-and-tool environments that drive the recent climb, which descend from WebShop rather than from Gym. The language-model era did not inherit it: the interactive share sits at 16% in 2021–22 and 16% in 2023, still mostly simulators and games, with WebShop the language-agent beachhead in 2022 — and only then does it climb, on a different lineage: 31% in 2024, 41% in 2025 (33 of 80), 64% in 2026 (9 of 14). The static single-turn dataset was 74% of the corpus through 2020 and is 36% of 2026 — already the minority format. The migration the agent surveys narrate is real; what the full timeline adds is that the field re-derived a form it had abandoned, from different ancestors.

The anomaly is what got dropped: multi-turn dialogue is 35 papers out of 369, and its high-water mark is the oldest era in the corpus (2018 and earlier, 15%), when dialogue systems were the product and 11 pre-2021 papers evaluated conversation directly (DailyDialog, SMN, CMU DoG and PersonaChat). No era since has matched that share: 8% in 2024, 8% in 2025, 0 papers in 2026. The field did not skip multi-turn dialogue — it built the dialogue corpora, then walked away from them, jumping from one-shot prompts to autonomous tool loops and leaving the thing users actually do with these systems (talk, clarify, correct, change their minds mid-task) to a handful of instruments like MT-Bench, MultiChallenge and AdvancedIF. τ-bench-style simulated users live inside a few agentic environments, but conversation as the object of evaluation is a shrinking share of the field, not a growing one.

finding 05

Benchmarks stopped being scraped and started being commissioned

Provenance of task items by era. “Expert-authored” means items written or curated for the benchmark by recruited domain experts; “real-work-mined” means harvested from artifacts of real work (GitHub issues, freelance jobs, filed contracts).

Through 2018, benchmarks were built from what could be collected cheaply: crowdworkers and scraped exams (20 of 39 papers, 51%). By 2025 crowdsourcing has collapsed to 2% and 48% of new benchmarks are expert-authored — physicians writing HealthBench criteria, IMO medalists writing FrontierMath-class problems, industry professionals with 14-year average experience writing GDPval deliverables — and another 10% are mined from real work products (SWE-bench's GitHub issues, SWE-Lancer's paid freelance jobs, RLI's real projects). The era of the annotation farm is over at the frontier.

The driver is the same as in finding 01: models passed the crowd. When the evaluated system outperforms a Mechanical Turk worker at the task, crowd-sourced ground truth stops being ground truth, and item-writing becomes skilled labor — which is why benchmark construction industrialized (Scale, Mercor, Surge are all in this corpus's author lists) and why item counts fall as horizons rise. The economics of expertise, not dataset fashion, is what moved this chart.

finding 06

The contamination cycle: the field forgot the private test set, then re-learned it

Strongest shipped contamination defense by era. “Private holdout” includes hidden test sets and evaluation servers; “live refresh” means post-cutoff or continuously regenerated items by design.

The instructive row is one of the oldest. The corpus’s earliest era already had the answer: 10 of 39 papers through 2018 (26%) shipped a hidden test set or an evaluation server — MS COCO, SQuAD, GLUE and HotpotQA among them — because a benchmark you could only query through a leaderboard was the norm when the test set was the scarce asset. The web-scrape training era then let it lapse: 41 of 49 papers in 2021–22 published everything (Codex, GSM8K, MATH and WebShop), leaving only 16% with any defense at all, and the contamination crisis of 2023–24 (GSM1k measured up to 8-point inflation on GSM8k) was the bill for that. From 2024 the defenses return and diversify: private holdouts (22 of 80 in 2025, 28%), live refresh (LiveCodeBench, SciPredict's post-cutoff sourcing, Arena's user traffic), procedural generation (Reasoning Gym), canaries.

The recovery is real and still partial. The share of new benchmarks shipping some defense runs 28% (through 2018) → 19% → 16% → 16% → 25% → 39% → 43% (6 of 14 in 2026): a dip and a return, with the 2020s cohorts finally passing the era that invented the practice — but no era in 26 years has ever crossed half, peaking at 43%. The default benchmark, in every era of this corpus, ships public and undefended.

The defenses also work differently well. Of the 12 live-refresh benchmarks, 0 are saturated; private holdouts saturate at 23%, slightly above the corpus base rate of 19% — a hidden test set stops leakage but not progress. Refresh-by-design is the only defense that also buys longevity, which is why the newest entries (SciPredict's “no paper before March 2025,” UpBench's live freelance feed) build refresh into the sampling frame rather than bolting privacy onto a frozen set.

finding 07

What saturates: horizon is destiny

Saturation status by task horizon, judged from the frontier scores each explainer quotes (mid-2026). Counts inside bars.

Saturation is almost perfectly ordered by the ladder: 44% of L1 benchmarks are saturated, 12% of L2, and 2% of L3 and above. Of the corpus's 69 saturated benchmarks, 1 poses a task longer than half an expert-hour: TAPE (2019), a protein-modelling suite whose long tasks are graded by a numeric answer key — the one shape of long-horizon benchmark that can saturate, because it never had to pay for open-ended grading. Domain matters less than you'd think and defense matters less than refresh (finding 06); what predicts whether a benchmark is still discriminating in 2026 is simply how much expert time its tasks embody.

That gives benchmark builders a clean actuarial rule — expected lifespan is bought in human-minutes per item — and it explains the corpus's own shape: the L1 rows are the field's history, the L2 rows its present workhorses (17% of them already closing), and the L3–L4 rows, 73% of which are still open, are where evaluation currently lives. The ladder from finding 03 isn't just being climbed; it's being consumed from the bottom.

// connections

Nine things no single paper shows

These were mined by tracing the corpus's internal citation graph (2013 edges among the 369, plus 682 typed lineage relations — descends-from, audits, de-saturates and the like — recorded per paper during classification) against the full text of every explainer, then checking each claim's quotes and citation-structure assertions mechanically. Each is a connection you cannot see from inside any one paper.

connection 01 · the twin transplant

GSM1k is ImageNetV2 re-run on LLM math — and the method's flaw transplanted with it, sign-flipped

GSM1k (2024) is Recht et al.'s 2019 CV experiment — rebuild a benchmark's test set by replicating its creation process, measure the drop — transplanted to LLM arithmetic five years later; its explainer says the move is “borrowed straight” from the CIFAR/ImageNet rebuilds. What transplanted along with the method is its central hazard: a reconstructed twin never lands exactly on the original distribution. But the bias flipped sign in transit. ImageNetV2's construction mismatch inflated its headline drop — Engstrom et al. later showed roughly two-thirds of it was artifact — while GSM1k's twin came out slightly easier than GSM8k, so its measured overfitting is plausibly understated.

Same method, same flaw, opposite error bars — and, strikingly, the same meta-verdict about two different eras: rankings mostly transfer, progress is mostly real, and inflation concentrates in specific model families rather than the frontier.

connection 02 · the audit shadow

Wells get corrected or they get extended — and only 12 of the 20 biggest got corrected

Reading the lineage graph for corrective descent only — a child that audits its parent, de-saturates it, or hardens its grader, as opposed to one that merely extends or responds to it — turns the audit shadow from a slogan into a count. 48 such edges exist among the 369 papers, reaching 32 distinct targets, and the longest unaudited stretch in the corpus is ImageNet → ImageNet-C/P at 10 years. The wait has been shortening: the longest first-correction lag is 10 years for targets published through 2018, 5 for 2019–20 targets, 3 for 2021–22, 2 for 2023 and later (medians 3 · 4 · 2.5 · 1). Part of that is real and part of it is arithmetic — a 2024 benchmark cannot have a five-year-old audit yet — so the honest version of the claim is the one the early rows carry on their own: the pre-2019 cohort contains both a one-year correction (SQuAD) and a ten-year one, and the recent cohorts contain no long waits at all.

The bigger correction to the older version of this story is that the shadow is not universal. Of the corpus's 20 most-cited benchmarks, 12 have a corrective descendant here; the rest — SuperGLUE, WebShop, AgentBench and Gorilla among them — have plenty of descendants, but they extend rather than correct: new domains, new modalities, harder instances of the same format. Correction and extension are different evolutionary pressures, and the agent-era wells have so far attracted mostly the second. That is worth watching, because extension inherits a benchmark's construct without ever testing it.

Where the shadow does fall, its mechanism has changed: early corrections were third-party forensics (Berkeley auditing ImageNet), but by 2024–25 they moved in-house and turned pre-emptive — OpenAI hardened its own SimpleQA into BrowseComp, LMSYS distilled its own Arena into Arena-Hard, Scale extended its own MultiChallenge into audio. Quality control became contemporaneous with publication.

connection 03 · the filter-model trap

HellaSwag's construction step became the 2024–25 standard — its documented failure mode got re-derived from scratch each time

Filter-by-model-failure — keep only items a reference model gets wrong — is HellaSwag's 2019 adversarial-filtering recipe, and it is the load-bearing construction step of three flagship evals in three domains: MMLU-Pro drops questions that 5+ of an 8-model panel answer correctly, HLE admits an item only if frontier models already fail it (70,000+ logged attempts), and Arena-Hard's BenchBuilder keeps only prompts a GPT-4 annotator scores ≥6. The lineage is contiguous in the citation graph — MMLU-Pro and Arena-Hard cite HellaSwag, HLE cites MMLU-Pro — yet none of the three names adversarial filtering. The fuller corpus adds a fourth case that makes the point sharper by not being lineage at all: WinoGrande's AFLITE (2020) filters the same way and traces back to Zellers et al.'s earlier SWAG filter rather than to HellaSwag, which its text does not cite.

The method propagated while its caveat got rediscovered independently each time: difficulty defined against a reference model is not intrinsic difficulty, so each benchmark is guaranteed to be climbed by anything stronger than its filter. HellaSwag's own ablation said so in 2019, and each descendant aged exactly as predicted — HellaSwag's “100,000 GPU-years to human level” fell in four years; HLE's sub-10% launch scores were tripled within months.

connection 04 · the pendulum forks

Web-agent evaluation swings between realism and gradability — and on the third swing it split in two

Each paper in the web lineage names the previous pole's grading failure as its motivation. Mind2Web (2023) buys exact-match gradability with static snapshots and pays in false negatives; WebVoyager (2024) swings to the live web with a GPT-4V judge and pays in irreproducibility and judge error. Then the retreat to verifiability happens twice, in incompatible directions: OSWorld keeps a live machine but gives up the open web (a controlled VM with per-task execution checks), while BrowseComp keeps the open web but gives up realistic tasks (questions inverted until the answer is a short verifiable string).

The pendulum doesn't return to one point — it forks, each branch sacrificing a different slice of realism. That fork is why GUI-agent evaluation and deep-research evaluation, both descended from a WebVoyager-shaped ancestor, no longer look related.

connection 05 · grader recursion

The same judging machinery scores ~90% at verdicts and 11% at explanations

Agent-as-a-Judge's machinery — hierarchical requirements checked one by one by an agentic judge that reads the workspace — was absorbed as PaperBench's rubric-tree grading infrastructure (which cites it). Then TRAIL (which also cites it) inverted the paradigm: the judge became the benchmarked system, asked not to check requirements but to localize faults inside real agent traces — and the ~90% human-alignment success story collapsed to 11% joint accuracy.

Verdict-checking and fault-explanation look like the same skill from a distance. The citation chain documents the field discovering, one paper at a time, that they are not — with direct implications for how much rubric-graded scores (finding 01) can be trusted when the rubric items get harder than the checkable kind.

connection 06 · the rubric fork

Per-task rubrics were invented twice in three months — and the two branches disagree about what the expensive part buys

2025's rubric wave is usually told as one movement; the citation graph splits it. PaperBench (April) and HealthBench (May) are genuine descent — linked papers, same lab, expert-written per-instance criteria (author-co-written rubric trees; 262 physicians). DeepResearch Bench's RACE (June) cites neither and is cited by neither: an independent convergence on per-task criteria that resolved the cost problem the opposite way — the judge writes its own criteria at evaluation time.

ResearchRubrics then ran the experiment that adjudicates the fork: letting an LLM augment expert rubrics degrades human alignment by 15–20%, while RACE's own ablation concedes its adaptive judge-written criteria buy only ~3 points over static ones. Expert-grounded versus judge-generated rubrics is now a falsifiable methodological fight between rival benchmarks — grader design has become an experimental science with adversaries.

connection 07 · the benchmark is the reward

The training flip was there from the start — and contamination travels through graders, not just test sets

The canonical benchmarks were never eval-only artifacts. GSM8K shipped as verifier-training infrastructure — a 7.5K train split plus a learned verifier, with the launch paper already documenting the verifier being reward-hacked. HumanEval arrived inside the Codex training paper with unit tests doubling as a sample-selection oracle; SWE-bench shipped 19K training instances and its own fine-tunes. When GSM1k audited the ecosystem, its most telling overfit case was contaminated through the grader lineage — a process-reward model leaking reasoning chains — with no test-set leak an n-gram checker could see.

Validity then had to be re-purchased, each time by moving the grader further from the training corpus: EvalPlus densified HumanEval's tests 80×; SWE-bench Pro bought commercial code models could never have seen — and its ~25–30-point public→commercial collapse, same models, same harness, is the field's cleanest single measurement of what training against a public grader family costs. By 2025 the flip is doctrine: MLGym's complaint about prior benchmarks is that they can't be trained on, and Reasoning Gym publishes its reward function. Goodhart doesn't disappear under procedural generation — it migrates into the grader's blind spots.

connection 08 · gravity selects portability

APPS was bigger, denser-tested, and tiered — HumanEval got the 66 citations. Wells get renovated, not abandoned

APPS and HumanEval are same-year, mutually-citing, execution-graded code benchmarks; APPS was 60× larger with twice the tests per problem. HumanEval's paper is the most-cited node in this corpus (66 in-corpus citations, ahead of MMLU's 63, against APPS's 20) because what the field reused was the measurement kit — the unbiased pass@k estimator plus 164 cheap, self-contained tasks scoring in the discriminative range — while APPS's grader vocabulary failed to travel (its lenient metric flattered; its strict metric floored at 0.00% on the whole Competition tier). The clincher: when EvalPlus proved HumanEval's thin tests inflate pass@k by up to ~29%, the field patched HumanEval in place rather than migrate to APPS — which already had denser tests.

The pattern repeats one generation down: SWE-bench Pro's forkable Docker harness is already substrate for third-generation benchmarks, while SWE-Lancer's more rigorous but unforkable grader (triple-verified end-to-end tests on one proprietary repo) has 0 coding descendants — every benchmark that descends from it (GDPval, RLI and UpBench) took the framing, real-dollar difficulty, into the economic-rehearsal family instead, and the coding benchmarks that cite it do so to explain what they did differently. Benchmark genes travel through cheap reproducible harnesses; the ecosystem structurally selects reproducibility over rigor.

connection 09 · the noiseless human

AlpacaFarm proved a simulated human must be noisy to be faithful — the lineage then built noiseless ones

AlpacaFarm's deepest result is that fidelity means reproducing human imperfection: it rejected a single GPT-4 judge with higher raw agreement in favor of 13 disagreeing judges plus deliberate label noise, because only the noisy simulator reproduces reward over-optimization — the phenomenon the sandbox existed to study. The later simulated humans quietly inverted this. MathDial's simulated student errs in GPT-3.5's failure modes, not children's; by HiL-Bench (2026) the simulated collaborator is explicitly an oracle — instant, correct, and free — which its own authors concede makes every reported number a ceiling-free floor.

A fake human that never lies, stalls, or charges cannot surface the failure modes that only human noise reveals — the exact category of blindness AlpacaFarm demonstrated in 2023. The founding fidelity lesson of the simulated-human lineage was traded away, one convenience at a time.

// method & caveats

How this was built, and what to distrust

Corpus. The 369 papers are the atlas's benchmarks survey tag — the same set you see ringed on the timeline map. Membership follows the tag rubric (benchmark as headline contribution), applied when each paper joined the atlas; the four edge cases that don't really meet it are named rather than hidden. The corpus over-represents what the atlas covers deeply (LLM, agent, and safety evaluation, 2023–2026: 55 + 85 + 80 + 14 of the 369) and samples the earlier generations rather than enumerating them, so era-level trends should be read as trends within this kind of corpus, not a census of all benchmarks ever built. The corpus is papers only: the 2026 model-card canon also includes non-paper instruments this page doesn't cover — SWE-bench Verified, Aider Polyglot, AIME-as-eval, the ARC-AGI leaderboards, and needle-in-a-haystack probes among them — an exclusion of form, not a judgment that they don't matter.

Classification. Each paper was classified from its atlas explainer (and the paper itself where the explainer underdetermined a dimension) by a language-model pipeline, in two historical stages. The first 139 papers got one full-schema pass followed by a fully independent second pass re-classifying the six judgment-heavy dimensions with different paper groupings: the two passes agreed on 94.8% of the 750 compared values, and all 39 disagreements (on 32 papers) were adjudicated by a third read of the source text, with saturation the noisiest dimension (16 of 39 conflicts). The remaining 230 were classified in a later backfill, sharded into 12 batches of ~20 against the same schema, each batch's classifier reading the same explainers and reporting its own borderline calls; the batches were merged, then gravity (in-corpus in-degree) was recomputed across the whole corpus and the lineage graph re-enriched, because a shard can only see the papers inside it. Those two stages did not get equal scrutiny, and the record carries the difference: 151 of 369 records are marked confidence: medium. Treat individual facet values — saturation above all — as softer than any aggregate on this page.

Tree placement. Each paper's kingdom/family address was assigned by the same protocol, judging role-at-birth from the paper's own problem statement, with lineage restricted to relations between papers that are both in the corpus (682 of them, over 2013 citation edges among the 369). The independent second placement pass covered 78 papers of the original 139-paper corpus before rate-limiting; it disagreed on 5 placements, each re-adjudicated against the source text. Two known lineage biases: a shard could not link to a parent sitting in a sibling shard (the post-merge enrichment pass exists to repair that, and did, but cannot prove it caught everything), and because a node's date is its arXiv v1 month while its camera-ready cites later work, genuinely forward-pointing relations were dropped rather than recorded — the lineage graph understates connectivity, in both cases in ways that are arbitrary with respect to the subject matter. Borderline calls worth knowing: ResearchRubrics and DeepResearch Bench sit in research execution rather than grader benchmarks (their headline is agent evaluation, their machinery is rubric methodology); MultiNLI sits in de-saturation sequels as SNLI's coverage-restoring successor; HellaSwag's placement is argued in the tree section itself.

Connections. The nine connection stories were mined by lens-directed search over the corpus and its internal citation graph, then verified two ways: every quotation was machine-checked against the explainer texts (all anchor phrases verified; elided quotes marked), and every citation-structure assertion ("X cites Y", "X and Y share no edge") was checked mechanically against the graph. Claims that survived only partially were corrected or dropped. Numbers inside the stories are the papers' own.

Known soft spots. (1) Task-horizon rungs are estimates of the typical instance from the papers' own task descriptions; only a few papers measure human time. (2) Saturation reflects the frontier scores quoted in each explainer at the time it was written (through mid-2026) — it ages. (3) grading_primary records the headline metric only; many benchmarks ship secondary graders. (4) Item counts are approximate. (5) The domain vocabulary cannot name robotics, multi-agent RL, or theory of mind, all of which the corpus now contains — see the facets; every affected record says so in its notes, but any domain-sliced number here inherits the squeeze. (6) One dimension a future pass should add: whether each benchmark's grader was itself adversarially audited — the ABC finding suggests that column would be mostly empty.

The classification data is embedded in this page (view source) and the tags live in the atlas's papers.json; both regenerate deterministically from the corpus.
