The Reversal Curse: LLMs trained on "A is B" fail to learn "B is A"

Finetune GPT-3 or Llama-1 on "Uriah Hawthorne is the composer of Abyssal Melodies" and the model can answer "Who composed Abyssal Melodies?" but not "Who is Uriah Hawthorne?" — reverse-direction accuracy stays near 0% at every model size and family tested, and the model's log-probability for the correct name shows no statistically detectable increase over a random name's, across every seed and size tested. GPT-4 shows the same asymmetry on real celebrities: 79% at recalling a parent, 33% at recalling the matching child.

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

If a person learns "Valentina Tereshkova was the first woman to travel to space," they can immediately answer "Who was the first woman to travel to space?" — the identity relation is symmetric, and a competent learner should treat "A is B" and "B is A" as the same fact seen from two directions. Prior work on LLM factual knowledge had already gathered circumstantial evidence pointing the same way, without running the direct test: knowledge-editing papers (Zhu et al., 2020; Mitchell et al., 2021) insert or edit a fact in one direction with no report of whether the edit generalizes to the reverse, and Meng et al. (2023)'s ROME editing technique goes further, explicitly finding that its edits are not bidirectional — evidence associations may be stored differently depending on direction, but not itself a controlled behavioral test; interpretability work on transformer feed-forward layers (Geva et al., 2021; 2022; 2023) had characterized factual associations as directed key-value lookups, again suggestive but not a direct test; and a cluster of consistency-checking papers (Elazar et al., 2021; Hosseini et al., 2021; Lin et al., 2022's TruthfulQA; Press et al., 2023's compositionality-gap work; Fluri et al., 2023) had shown LLMs give inconsistent answers to logically related questions, but none isolated ordering — the direction a fact was written in during training — as the variable that breaks generalization. As the paper itself frames it: "While these studies provide circumstantial evidence for the Reversal Curse, we provide a direct test."

The gap this paper closes is a direct, controlled behavioral test: train a model on "A is B" and check, at the level of raw next-token log-probability (not just top-1 generation), whether "B is A" becomes even slightly more likely than it would be for an unrelated fact. No prior paper had run that specific experiment, and the answer — a complete, not partial, failure — is surprising enough to be worth naming.

// how it works

The method

The core test (Figure 2) is always the same two-step structure: finetune a base LLM (GPT-3 or Llama-1) on documents written in one order — "<name> is <description>" or the reverse — then prompt the finetuned model with held-out questions in both orders and measure whether it can answer. If the order at test time matches the order seen in training, the model should do well; the Reversal Curse claim is that when the order is flipped, exact-match accuracy collapses to chance and, more strongly, the model's log-probability for the correct name is statistically indistinguishable from a random name's — not just lower, but no different from noise.

Experiment 1 (fictitious celebrities, controlled). The authors generate entirely fictitious name–description pairs with GPT-4 (so the facts cannot already be in the base model's pretraining data) and build a 3,600-document finetuning set: 900 documents in a NameToDescription subset, 900 in a DescriptionToName subset (30 base facts × 30 paraphrases each), and 1,800 in a "Both" subset where each of 30 further facts appears in both orders (30 facts × 30 paraphrases × 2 orders) as auxiliary data meant to help the model learn that facts often appear both ways. They finetune GPT-3 (350M through 175B) via the OpenAI API and Llama-7B, sweeping learning-rate multiplier and batch size, and evaluate with two metrics: exact-match on held-out prompt templates (Table 2 lists twenty of them, several per direction), and, for the NameToDescription subset only, an "Increased Likelihood" test that compares the model's average log-probability for the correct name against a random name from the finetuning set, checked for significance with a paired t-test and a Kolmogorov–Smirnov test.

Experiment 2 (real celebrities, uncontrolled). Since Experiment 1 uses invented facts with a fully controlled corpus, the authors also test whether the effect shows up in a real model's real pretraining-derived knowledge. They take the top 1,000 most popular celebrities from IMDB, query GPT-4 for each one's parent, then test whether models can recover the celebrity given the parent's name — including, to rule out an instruction-tuning refusal confound, base Llama-1 models that never went through instruction-tuning or RLHF, alongside GPT-4 itself and gpt-3.5-turbo. The authors are explicit that, because they don't control what's in the pretraining set, "Experiment 2 is not a direct test of the Reversal Curse and so any conclusions are somewhat tentative" — a caveat this page returns to below.

Experiment 3 (instruction reversal). A third, structurally identical test recasts the same ordering manipulation as instruction-following: models are finetuned on either "Answer <question> with <answer>" (QuestionToAnswer) or "Answer with <answer> when you see <question>" (AnswerToQuestion) instructions, each dataset built from 1,100 question-answer pairs, and tested on the corresponding held-out "Q: <question> A:" completions across Llama-7B/13B/30B.

// what they found

The results

For GPT-3-175B (Table 1), exact-match accuracy is high when the test-time order matches training — 50.0% ± 2.1 for NameToDescription facts, 96.7% ± 1.2 for DescriptionToName facts (the paper partly attributes this same-direction gap to exact-match being an easier metric for short names than for longer descriptions, not to anything about reversal) — and collapses to 0.0% ± 0.0 and 0.1% ± 0.1 respectively when the order is reversed. That pattern — strong same-direction, near-zero reverse-direction — repeats across every configuration tested: a GPT-3-350M hyperparameter sweep over 20 learning-rate/batch-size combinations gives same-order accuracy from 49.0% to 78.0% but reverse-order accuracy ≤0.3% in all 20 of 20 cells; a Llama-7B sweep over 12 combinations tops out at 1.33% reverse accuracy, below the dataset's own 1/30 = 3.3% random-guessing baseline in every cell; and a far larger dataset (100 facts × 100 paraphrases, 40,000 documents versus the original 3,600 — about an 11× increase) still lands at exactly 0.0% reverse accuracy for both subsets (Table 6) — more data does not fix it.

That log-probability result is the paper's strongest evidence, because it rules out a "the model learned a little but exact-match is too strict a bar" explanation: if any partial generalization existed, it should show up as the correct name being at least marginally more likely than a random one, and it doesn't, at any of the four GPT-3 sizes tested (350M, 1.3B, 6.7B, 175B).

Experiment 2's real-celebrity numbers show the same shape, with two different accuracy figures reported for GPT-4's "Child" direction: GPT-4 identifies a celebrity's parent 79% of the time (the query protocol isn't stated in the same detail as the child-recall protocol below), yielding 1,573 usable child–parent pairs; asked to go the other way — name the child given the parent — GPT-4 succeeds 33% of the time counting a success whenever it answers correctly in at least one of 10 sampled attempts (footnote 11, to guard against naming a real but different child, e.g. a sibling), or 28% on Figure 5's "Child" axis, whose aggregation the paper doesn't state. Figure 5 itself plots two different metrics side by side: gpt-3.5-turbo's bars are the mean accuracy over 10 samples at temperature 1, while the three Llama-1 bars (7B/30B/65B) are each model's likelihood of the correct completion. Read that way, gpt-3.5-turbo's mean accuracy at recovering the parent is roughly three times its mean accuracy at recovering the child (mid-30s% vs. low-teens%), and all three Llama-1 sizes show an even starker split — each assigns roughly 8% likelihood to the correct parent but the low single digits or less to the correct child.

Experiment 3's instruction-reversal test lands in the same place by a different route: all three Llama-1 sizes tested exceed 80% accuracy on the held-out question-answer pairs when the direction matches training, and stay below 7% when it's reversed — a residual the paper attributes not to real generalization but to guessing among semantically related, previously-seen answers (footnote 13 notes that 7% is itself higher than pure random output would score, precisely because the held-out answers are semantically related to their questions). A separate in-context control (Table 5) makes the localization sharp: when the same "A is B" statement is placed directly in the prompt (3-shot) instead of trained via a gradient update, reversal succeeds almost perfectly — 100% for 7 of 8 (model size × direction) cells, 96.67% for the eighth (GPT-3-350M, DescriptionToName). The failure is specific to what a gradient update on "A is B" does to the model's weights, not to whatever the same GPT-3 models can represent or reason about in-context (the in-context control, Appendix B.6, was run on GPT-3 only).

// does it hold up?

Is this the training objective, or a corpus artifact? For Experiment 1, the paper has a real answer, not just an assertion: the finetuning corpus is entirely author-constructed and fictitious, with the NameToDescription and DescriptionToName subsets built from disjoint fact sets of matched size (30 facts × 30 paraphrases each), so there's no real-world frequency imbalance to point to — the reverse direction simply never appears as text for a given fact, by design. Combined with the in-context control (near-100% reversal accuracy when the same fact is given via the prompt instead of a gradient update), the localization is specific: it isolates the failure to what SGD does to a "A is B" gradient update, not to tokenization, corpus statistics, or a general representational limit. That's a well-controlled result for Experiment 1 — with one caveat the paper names itself: it finetunes an already-pretrained model, so a directional retrieval prior inherited from pretraining isn't fully excluded ("A limitation of our Experiment 1 is that it uses finetuning (rather than realistic pretraining) and synthetic data").

Does Experiment 2 actually isolate reversal from frequency imbalance? No, and the paper says so directly: "Since we do not know the precise contents of the LLM's training set, Experiment 2 is not a direct test of the Reversal Curse and so any conclusions are somewhat tentative." A celebrity's parent is very plausibly mentioned in more pretraining text, in more sentence orders, than the reverse direction — sentences naming a celebrity's mother are common; sentences naming a private individual's celebrity son are comparatively rare — which would produce exactly the observed asymmetry through ordinary frequency effects with no reversal-specific mechanism required at all. The paper's own Future Work section goes further and concedes the entanglement explicitly: "the distribution of mention counts for entities in training corpora is long-tailed and so some of this information will be rarely expressed in the reverse order" — and proposes, as an unexecuted next step, using entity-linking over the actual pretraining corpus (citing Kandpal et al., 2023) to find genuine one-direction-only facts. That control — matching or measuring forward vs. backward mention frequency for the same facts — does not appear anywhere in this paper, in the main text or any of its seven appendices. Given that, Experiment 2's headline 79%-vs-33% (or 28%) gap should be read as consistent with the Reversal Curse but not yet distinguished from a plain frequency confound, not as independent confirmation of it — the paper itself doesn't claim more than that, but the abstract's framing (leading with the celebrity numbers right after Experiment 1's controlled result) makes it easy for a reader to conflate the two experiments' evidentiary strength.

What would falsify the reversal explanation, and did the paper look? Yes, in two ways that go beyond just reporting accuracy. First, the increased-likelihood test is a genuine attempt to detect a partial effect that exact-match would miss: if the gradient update nudged the correct name's probability up even slightly, a paired t-test or KS test across 30 fact-pairs per seed should catch it, and across 12 (model size × seed) comparisons it never does (minimum p = 0.07). Second, the mitigations aren't a single failed attempt — the paper tries paraphrase augmentation, "Both"-direction auxiliary training data, a reformatted Q&A version of the task (Experiment 3), a 10× larger dataset, and prompt tuning instead of full finetuning, and reports all of them failing to produce reverse-direction generalization. What's still missing is a case where the intervention succeeds at something adjacent — e.g., a control condition (like the in-context test) that succeeds is present, but there's no ablation that partially restores reverse accuracy, which would help calibrate how brittle vs. fundamental the failure is. As it stands, the evidence supports the narrower claim that gradient-based finetuning on one-directional facts robustly fails to transfer to the reverse direction, not the stronger claim that no training-time intervention could ever fix this — a distinction the paper's own Discussion section draws explicitly, framing the whole result as an open question rather than a settled negative: "In this paper, we set out to prove a negative result. Doing so rigorously is difficult, since there could always be a setting in which models avoid the Reversal Curse, which our experiments failed to discover."

baselines

Random-name and matched-direction baselines are used throughout and are the right comparators; the Increased-Likelihood test's random-baseline design (a random name from the finetuning set, not an arbitrary string) is a genuinely careful choice.

contamination

Not the relevant risk here — Experiment 1's facts are deliberately fictitious specifically to avoid pretraining contamination. The open risk runs the other way for Experiment 2: real celebrities and their real, plausibly memorized parent-child relations are exactly what one would want controlled for frequency, and aren't.

what's missed

A frequency-matched or frequency-measured version of Experiment 2 (proposed by the authors themselves as future work, never executed here); a single ablation that partially — not just totally — restores reverse accuracy, which would sharpen how brittle the mechanism explanation is; and a stated protocol for GPT-4's parent query to match the child query's documented 10-sample/temperature-1 procedure (footnote 11) — the paper never says whether the parent query used the same repeated-sampling approach.

would raise confidence

Entity-linked pretraining-corpus analysis (the paper's own suggested follow-up) directly measuring forward vs. reverse mention frequency for the celebrity pairs in Experiment 2; a version of Experiment 1 at pretraining scale rather than finetuning (partially addressed by contemporaneous work the paper cites — Allen-Zhu & Li, 2023, training from scratch, and Grosse et al., 2023's influence-function analysis of pretrained models — but not run by this paper itself).

// bottom line

Experiment 1 is a tightly controlled, convincing demonstration that gradient-based finetuning on "A is B" does not transfer to "B is A," down to the level of raw log-probability, robust across model family, size, dataset size, and several attempted mitigations. Experiment 2's real-world celebrity numbers are the more citable, more viral result, but the paper itself flags them as tentative for exactly the reason a careful reader should worry about — an un-ruled-out frequency confound — and that caveat is easy to lose sight of once the 79%/33% numbers travel on their own.

// what to remember

What to remember

  1. Training a model on "A is B" does not raise the model's probability for "A" when queried with "B" — not just below a usable threshold, but statistically indistinguishable from a random name, across four GPT-3 sizes and three finetuning seeds each: minimum p = 0.07 across the 24 p-values Table 3 prints (a paired t-test and a Kolmogorov–Smirnov test for each of the 12 model-size/seed combinations, which the paper's own Appendix B.5 refers to as "12 statistical tests").
  2. The same fact given in-context instead of trained by gradient descent reverses almost perfectly (100% in 7 of 8 cells), which localizes the failure specifically to what a training update does to the model's weights — not to a general inability to represent bidirectional relations.
  3. The paper's celebrity experiment (GPT-4: 79% parent-recall vs. 33% child-recall) is real-world evidence but not a controlled test of the Reversal Curse specifically — the paper says so itself, and its own Future Work section names the un-run control (frequency-matching via entity-linking) that would settle it.
