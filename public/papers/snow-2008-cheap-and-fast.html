Cheap and Fast – But is it Good? Evaluating Non-Expert Annotations for Natural Language Tasks

The founding empirical study of crowdsourced NLP annotation: pool four to ten anonymous, paid Amazon Mechanical Turk workers, correct for their individual biases, and — across five different tasks — reach at or near single-expert quality, matching or beating it outright on several, for pennies per label.

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

By 2008, NLP had a well-known bottleneck: the field's landmark resources — the Penn Treebank, PropBank, TimeBank, FrameNet, SemCor — each represent years of trained-annotator labor, and the field's own results (Banko and Brill's 2001 finding that more data reliably beats a better algorithm) meant demand for labeled data was only growing faster than expert annotation could supply it. Amazon launched Mechanical Turk in 2005 as a marketplace for exactly this kind of piecework, but by 2008 nobody had run a rigorous, task-general test of whether its anonymous, unvetted, pseudonymous workers could actually substitute for trained linguistic annotators — as opposed to using them for a single task in isolation, the way a handful of prior studies had (hotel-listing entity resolution, Wikipedia article-quality ratings, machine-vision labeling).

The open questions were concrete and, before this paper, unanswered as a set: how many non-expert labels does it take to equal one expert's? Does the answer depend on the task? Can non-expert labels train a machine learning system as well as expert labels can? And when individual annotators are unreliable, is there a cheap way to correct for that without collecting more data? Snow, O'Connor, Jurafsky and Ng answer all four with a single, tightly controlled experimental design run across five distinct NLP tasks — affect recognition, word similarity, recognizing textual entailment (RTE), event temporal ordering, and word sense disambiguation (WSD) — each with a pre-existing expert gold standard to check against.

// how it works

The method

For every task the design is the same: collect ten independent non-expert annotations per item on Mechanical Turk, restricting tasks to multiple-choice or bounded-numeric responses so answers are easy to aggregate. Then run two comparisons against the existing expert gold standard. First, inter-annotator agreement (ITA): for the affect task, six SemEval expert annotators had already double-labeled the same headlines, so the paper computes each expert's correlation with the average of the other five ("E vs. E"), and separately each non-expert's correlation with those same five experts ("NE vs. E"). Second, a pooling experiment: average every possible subset of n of the ten non-expert labels (for n = 1…10) into a single "meta-labeler," and ask at what n = k its agreement with the held-out experts first reaches or beats a single expert's own agreement. For the other four tasks (word similarity, RTE, event ordering, WSD) the same subset-averaging analysis is repeated, but scored against each task's own published gold labels rather than a live expert pool, using majority voting for the categorical tasks and Pearson correlation for the numeric ones.

The paper's second contribution is a bias-correction model for the categorical tasks (RTE and event ordering). Following Dawid and Skene (1979), each worker w's response is modeled as conditionally independent of other workers given the true label, with a per-worker confusion-matrix likelihood P(yw | x) estimated from a sample of gold-labeled training items (Laplace-smoothed, uniform label priors, fit and evaluated via 20-fold cross-validation). Integrating worker votes through Bayes' rule turns this into a weighted-voting rule: each worker's vote is weighted by their own log-likelihood ratio, so workers who are more than 50% accurate vote positively, workers whose labels are pure noise vote near zero, and even anti-correlated (systematically wrong) workers can be turned into a useful negative-weighted signal instead of discarded.

log P(xi=Y | yi1…yiW)P(xi=N | yi1…yiW)  =  Σw log P(yiw | xi=Y)P(yiw | xi=N)  +  log P(xi=Y)P(xi=N) // gold-calibrated weighted voting: each worker's vote weighted by their own log-likelihood ratio

Separately, on the affect task's numeric ratings the same idea is adapted with a per-worker Gaussian noise model, yw | x ~ 𝒩(x + μw, σw), recalibrating each worker's mean offset and variance rather than a confusion matrix. Finally, to test whether the labels are useful for training, not just evaluation, the paper builds a simple unigram bag-of-words classifier for affect scoring — a token's weight for an emotion is just the average emotion score of the headlines it appears in — trained separately on each of six individual experts' labels versus on non-expert label subsets of the same sizes, then compared on a held-out 900-headline test set.

// what they found

The results

On the affect task, pooling non-expert labels reaches expert-level agreement for every emotion except fear, and often does so with very few annotators: two non-experts already match a single expert on anger (0.536 vs. 0.459) and disgust (0.627 vs. 0.583), while surprise needs nine (0.481 vs. 0.464). Averaged across all seven affect/valence sub-tasks, the minimum crossing point is k = 4 — four pooled non-experts reach the same agreement (0.613) as one expert (0.603). At $2.00 for the 7,000 non-expert affect labels collected, and roughly four labels needed per expert-equivalent judgment, that comes out to about 875 expert-equivalent labels per dollar.

The other four tasks each hit their own high-agreement plateau. Word similarity (30 word pairs, replicating Miller & Charles 1991): ten pooled annotators reach 0.952 correlation with the published gold ratings, close to Resnik's (1999) benchmark of 0.958 with ten subjects — and the whole 300-annotation batch was completed by workers in under 11 minutes (1,724 annotations/hour). RTE (recognizing textual entailment, PASCAL RTE-1): on a 100-pair analysis subsample, ten-annotator majority voting reaches 89.7% accuracy, just short of the 91–96% expert-agreement range reported for other subsections of the same corpus — though the paper collected the full 800-pair, 8,000-label RTE-1 set for its cost accounting (Table 3), a larger volume than the subsample used for the accuracy curve. Event temporal ordering (a simplified two-way "strictly before / strictly after" version of TimeBank, 462 verb-pairs, 4,620 labels): ten-annotator voting reaches 0.94 accuracy — but the paper is explicit that no expert baseline exists for this simplified task; the only available reference point is 0.77 ITA on TimeBank's full fourteen-label scheme, which the authors themselves flag as "the more general task," so the two numbers aren't a fair apples-to-apples comparison. WSD (disambiguating "president" in SemEval Task-17 sentences, 177 examples): majority voting plateaus at 0.994 accuracy, above the best automatic system's 0.98 — and inspecting the single remaining disagreement (a 9-to-1 crowd vote against the "gold" label) turned up an actual annotation error in the original SemEval gold standard; correcting it brings non-expert accuracy to 100%. Worth finishing that thought, since it cuts the paper’s way: the “just short” figure is the raw majority vote. Figure 7’s gold-calibrated curve, the paper’s actual proposal, climbs past the 91% lower bound of the expert-agreement band by ten annotators — the dashed reference line in that figure sits at exactly 0.91. So the bias correction is what closes the gap majority voting leaves open.

Across all five tasks, the full data-collection run cost $25.82 for 21,690 labels over 143.9 hours of elapsed wall-clock time (measured from HIT submission to the last worker's assignment, not aggregate worker labor time) — an average of 840 labels per dollar and 150.7 labels per elapsed hour (Table 3).

The bias-correction model recovers further accuracy from the same raw annotations, without collecting a single additional label: gold-calibrated weighted voting adds +4.0% accuracy on RTE and +3.4% on event ordering (both averaged across 2–10 annotators), and the Gaussian-noise variant adds a smaller +0.6% correlation gain on the numeric affect ratings. WSD was left uncorrected — it was already at ceiling.

Finally, non-expert labels were also tested as training data, not just evaluation data. Table 4 reports, per emotion, the minimum number of non-expert annotation sets (k) a classifier needs before it outperforms the average single-expert-trained classifier: a single non-expert annotation set (k = 1) is already enough on five of the paper's seven emotion/valence rows — Anger (0.172 vs. 1-Expert's 0.084), Disgust (0.185 vs. 0.130), Fear (0.176 vs. 0.159), Sadness (0.141 vs. 0.127), and Surprise (0.061 vs. 0.060). Valence needs two pooled non-expert sets before it crosses (k = 2, per the paper's own table), and Joy never crosses even with all ten sets pooled (10-NE 0.125 vs. 1-Expert 0.130). Separately, pooling all ten non-expert annotation sets (the 10-NE column) beats the single-expert-trained classifier on six of the seven rows — every emotion except Joy (Anger 0.233 vs. 0.084, Disgust 0.231 vs. 0.130, Fear 0.247 vs. 0.159, Sadness 0.174 vs. 0.127, Surprise 0.101 vs. 0.060, Valence 0.229 vs. 0.159).

// does it hold up?

The eval read

The core empirical claim — that pooled, cheap, non-expert annotations can approach expert-level quality — is well supported for four of the five tasks tested, and the paper is admirably careful to show its work: Table 1's four ITA columns (E-vs-E, E-vs-All, NE-vs-E, NE-vs-All) let a reader see individual expert-to-expert agreement is genuinely higher than non-expert-to-expert agreement on every single emotion, which is exactly what you'd want a rigorous head-to-head comparison to establish before the paper goes on to show pooling closes that gap. But each task borrows its expert baseline from a different, pre-existing external study — Miller & Charles (1991) for word similarity, published RTE-1 sub-corpus agreement figures, six SemEval annotators for affect, a single automatic system's score for WSD — and for the fifth task, event ordering, there is no expert baseline of the same task at all. The paper's own text calls the closest available number (0.77 ITA on the full fourteen-label TimeBank scheme) "the more general task," and doesn't quantify how much harder that fourteen-way distinction is than the paper's simplified two-way before/after choice. Event ordering's headline 0.94 accuracy is therefore the one result in the paper that isn't actually validated against a like-for-like expert number — it's compared against a different, likely much harder task.

Table 1 does carry a real, checkable arithmetic slip — and it's precise enough to name exactly. Its E-vs-E column prints Avg.All = 0.580, where the true mean of its own seven per-emotion cells is 0.602; and its E-vs-All column prints Avg.Emo = 0.603, where the true mean of its own six emotion cells is 0.580. Each broken cell holds the other row's correct value: 0.580 is actually E-vs-All's Avg.Emo, and 0.603 — which matches the same seven numbers reprinted as the "1-Expert" column of Table 2, whose own reported Avg.All is 0.603 — is actually E-vs-E's Avg.All. The NE-vs-E and NE-vs-All columns have no such problem; both reconcile cleanly with their own cells. It reads as a two-cell transposition between adjacent columns in one table, not a scattered pattern of errors, and it doesn't change any of the paper's conclusions — but it's worth knowing if you're going to cite the exact 0.580/0.603 figures. Table 4 has a smaller slip of the same checkable kind. Its k column is defined as the number of non-expert annotations needed to match one expert, yet Valence lists k = 2 against a 2-annotation score of 0.146 — below the single-expert 0.159 it is supposed to have reached. It is the only row of the seven where the table’s own k does not satisfy its own definition.

Sample sizes throughout are modest by later NLP-benchmark standards — 100 headlines, 30 word pairs, a 100-pair RTE subsample, 462 event pairs, 177 WSD examples — and every headline number is a single point estimate: there are no confidence intervals or variance estimates reported anywhere in the paper, on either the ITA curves or the bias-correction gains. And the entire study predates the phenomenon that would make a literal 2025 rerun measure something different: multiple studies since 2023 have found a meaningful fraction of Mechanical Turk workers now use large language models to generate their responses, which quietly collapses "non-expert human judgment" into "human-relayed LLM judgment" for exactly the kind of task this paper pioneered.

baselines

Four of five tasks compare against a genuine, task-matched expert or published reference (word similarity, affect, RTE, WSD); event ordering has none — its 0.94 accuracy is checked only against a harder, differently-scoped 14-label task, which the paper itself flags as not directly comparable.

contamination

Not applicable in the modern train/test-leakage sense (this predates statistical-LM and neural benchmarks by years) — but the 2008-era premise that AMT workers are "non-expert humans" is itself now a live contamination risk: post-2023 studies report substantial LLM use by crowdworkers on exactly this kind of task, so a modern replication can't assume the same population.

what's missed

No confidence intervals or variance reporting anywhere, on samples as small as 30–462 items per task; "k = minimum non-experts to beat an expert" is defined against an average expert from a small (5–6 person) held-out pool, not against expert variance or the best expert; and Table 1 carries a two-cell transposition between its E-vs-E and E-vs-All columns (0.580/0.603 swapped) that a reader citing exact ITA figures should know about.

would raise confidence

A matched expert baseline for event ordering; per-item variance or bootstrap confidence intervals on every ITA/accuracy curve; a larger held-out replication given sample sizes of a few hundred items per task; and, for anyone using this paper's framework today, an explicit check for LLM-assisted crowd responses.

// bottom line

The methodology this paper introduced — pool a handful of cheap non-expert labels, correct for individual worker bias with a Dawid-Skene-style model, and treat the result as expert-equivalent — became the default assumption behind an entire decade of crowdsourced NLP datasets. That's a real, durable contribution. But the paper's own evidence is more uneven than "crowdsourcing works" summaries of it usually convey: one of five tasks (event ordering) has no real expert baseline, none of the five report any variance, and the crowd-worker population the paper describes — anonymous, unvetted, pre-LLM humans — is no longer the population you'd get by rerunning this exact experiment today.

// takeaways

What to remember

  1. Pooling just four non-expert Mechanical Turk annotations matched single-expert-level agreement on the affect task, and larger pools (up to ten) got very close on word similarity and, on WSD, went past the expert reference outright — though RTE's ten-annotator majority vote (89.7%) landed just under, not inside, the 91–96% expert-agreement range reported for the corpus. All told, about 840 labels per dollar.
  2. A simple gold-calibrated bias-correction model — weighting each worker's vote by their own estimated reliability — recovered further accuracy from the exact same raw labels: +4.0% on RTE and +3.4% on event ordering, with no additional data collection.
  3. Cheap crowd labels were good enough to train classifiers, not just evaluate them: on five of seven emotion/valence rows in Table 4, a classifier trained on just a single non-expert annotation set already beat one trained on a single expert's labels, and pooling all ten non-expert sets pushed that to six of seven (Joy being the only row that never crosses). Crowd disagreement even caught a real labeling error in an existing SemEval gold standard.
