Evaluating Attribution in Dialogue Systems: The BEGIN Benchmark

A three-way taxonomy — fully attributable, not attributable, or generic — applied to 12,288 model-generated dialogue turns, built not to rank dialogue systems but to test whether eight automatic attribution metrics can be trusted at all.

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

Knowledge-grounded dialogue systems built on large language models are fluent — utterances sampled from them score well on human measures like "engagingness" or "human-likeness" — but fluency isn't the property this paper cares about. These systems routinely produce statements their own evidence doesn't support: informally, "hallucinations." Following the Attributable-to-Identifiable-Source (AIS) framework of Rashkin et al. (2021), the authors define an attributable response as one connected to textual evidence that supports the entirety of the response. Several modeling approaches to reduce hallucination in grounded dialogue already existed by the time this paper appeared (Rashkin et al., 2021; Shuster et al., 2021; Dziri et al., 2021, 2022). Progress on the modeling side, the authors argue, is bottlenecked on the measurement side: you can't fix what you can't reliably detect.

The gap is specifically methodological. Prior comparisons between automatic metrics and human attribution judgments relied on small-scale datasets — a few hundred examples — which produces a noisy correlation estimate and no real ability to check performance on infrequent example types. And a metric correlating with human scores on some small study doesn't establish that it's measuring the right thing: a learned metric, like anything else a neural network learns, can rely on spurious correlations that fail to generalize to harder cases. So the paper's actual contribution isn't a leaderboard for dialogue systems — it's a large, structured resource for auditing the metrics researchers already use to judge those systems. As the authors put it directly: "the goal of this benchmark is to determine to what extent current evaluation metrics fulfill their purpose."

// how it works

The method

BEGIN is built in three stages: generate a large, diverse pool of machine responses to knowledge-grounded dialogue prompts; label each response by hand with a three-way attribution taxonomy; then use those labels to interrogate eight existing evaluation metrics and a purpose-built classifier.

A three-way taxonomy, not a binary one

Rather than a simple faithful/unfaithful split, BEGIN labels every response as one of:

  • Fully Attributable — the response conveys only information completely supported by the provided document (what the literature elsewhere calls "faithfulness").
  • Not Attributable — the response contains at least some information that can't be verified against the evidence, whether or not it's true in the real world: hallucinations, direct contradictions of the background text, and off-topic statements are all folded into this one category. A pilot study tried to split these three sub-cases apart, but the authors report the boundaries were too difficult to define and annotate reliably, so they were merged.
  • Generic — bland, context-independent utterances such as "I don't know about that" or "Hello there!" The authors add this category deliberately: a model that hedges or acknowledges ignorance shouldn't be scored as badly as one that invents facts, even though neither conveys grounded information.

This exact taxonomy has a life beyond this paper as a measurement scale, not just an annotation guideline. The same lead author's FaithDial, whose TACL preprint appeared about a year later, doesn't only borrow the three labels to sort Wizard-of-Wikipedia turns before rewriting the unattributable ones — it certifies its own corpus by reporting Fleiss' κ of 0.75 on the BEGIN classes (against this paper's own 0.7) and states its headline result, "94.4% faithful," as a distribution over those same classes, extended to five (adding Partial Hallucination and Uncooperative). That is what turns a taxonomy into a way to evaluate rather than only a way to label: a later paper runs the protocol and reports its own number in the taxonomy's units, rather than only reusing the bucket names.

Generating the responses

Four dialogue systems were trained, one on each of three knowledge-grounded corpora (12 model–corpus pairs): two plain language models, GPT2-base and T5-base, and two systems purpose-built for grounded dialogue, DoHA (a BART-based model with a two-view attention mechanism over document and history) and CTRL-DIALOG (T5-base augmented with control tokens that push generation toward grounded, less subjective content). The three corpora are Wizard of Wikipedia (WoW; Wizard/Apprentice conversations, evidence spans typically one or two sentences, average 27 words), CMU-DoG (conversations about films grounded in Wikipedia sections, average 215 words), and TopicalChat (conversations grounded in Reddit/Wikipedia/news facts, average 271 words). Each model generated at least 1,000 responses per corpus on held-out test data; after filtering toxic outputs with the Google Perspective API, this yielded 12,288 examples in total.

Annotating the responses

Three annotators labeled each of the 12,288 examples with the three-way taxonomy given a knowledge snippet, the prior turn, and the generated response; the majority-vote label is kept, and examples where all three annotators disagreed were dropped. Before the main round, annotators were trained on two pilot batches of about 100 examples each, with manual feedback after each; during the 12k-example main round, quality was spot-checked every 3,000 completed annotations. This procedure produced a reported Krippendorff's α of 0.7 — a single, aggregate figure, with the paper never specifying exactly which judgments and phases it was computed over. BEGIN itself has no training split by design ("BEGIN is intended as a test benchmark"): only a 10% dev / 90% test partition. Among the majority-vote labels retained from this ~12.3k-example pool (a small number of three-way ties were dropped and never labeled), the distribution is 70.1% Not Attributable, 23.5% Fully Attributable, 6.4% Generic.

Eight metrics, four families

BEGIN is used to test: three lexical-overlap metrics (BLEU-4, ROUGE-L, and a word-level F1 between response and knowledge); three semantic-similarity metrics (BERTScore with a DeBERTa-xl-MNLI backbone, BARTScore, BLEURT-20); one question-based metric (Q², which generates a question from the response and checks whether the knowledge source can answer it consistently); and NLI-based classifiers — T5-base and RoBERTa-large fine-tuned on MNLI and the dialogue-inference set DNLI (both mapping entailment/contradiction/neutral onto attributable/unattributable/generic), on the synthetic factuality set AugWoW (which maps its own supported/refuted/not-enough-information labels onto the same three), and on the authors' own adversarial training set (below), which is built natively in BEGIN's three categories and needs no label mapping at all.

BEGIN-ADVERSARIAL

Because gold NLI datasets aren't built for this taxonomy, the authors construct an 8k-example silver training set from WoW: attributable examples are the original human responses (filtered to exclude first-person opinion and require ≥25% word overlap with the evidence); unattributable examples are produced by perturbing the knowledge span before generation (swapping subject/object, replacing verbs, or swapping named entities via a SpaCy NER tagger), by directly negating or antonym-substituting the gold human response, or by sampling an off-topic gold response from a different but same-initial-topic conversation; generic examples come from the WoW-trained GPT2 sampled at a low temperature (0.4).

// what they found

The results

Overall, the four systems generated a substantial share of unattributable responses (70.1% of the benchmark). Broken down by model, CTRL-DIALOG has the lowest share of unattributable responses (30.8%) — the authors read this as evidence that its control-token design is "moderately successful" at its stated goal, since the other three systems' bars all sit well above it. Broken down by corpus, TopicalChat produces the most unattributable responses, followed by CMU-DoG, then WoW — the same ordering, in reverse, as the corpora's average knowledge-source length (271, 215, and 27 words respectively).

For the lexical, semantic-similarity, and Q² metrics, BEGIN doesn't fit a classifier — it plots the raw score distribution per category and reports what's visually true of it. Across BLEU, ROUGE, F1, BERTScore, BARTScore, and BLEURT, the median score for Fully Attributable responses sits above Generic and Not Attributable, as expected, but the three distributions overlap substantially, especially between Generic and Not Attributable — enough that the authors conclude no fixed threshold on any of these metrics cleanly separates the categories. More strikingly, the median score for Generic responses is lower than for Not Attributable ones on every metric plotted, even though a hedging response is normatively preferable to a hallucinated one. Q² behaves the same way here as the lexical and semantic metrics: typically higher for Attributable responses, but unable to separate Generic from Not Attributable.

The one quantitative, table-scored family is the NLI-based classifiers (Table 2, macro-averaged P/R/F1 on the BEGIN test and dev splits). BEGIN-ADVERSARIAL substantially outperforms classifiers trained on MNLI, DNLI, or AugWoW on both backbones despite being the smallest of the four training sets — T5 test macro-F1 of 45.9 versus 37.8 (AugWoW), 34.6 (MNLI), and 25.6 (DNLI); RoBERTa test macro-F1 of 41.1 versus 36.4 (MNLI), 29.7 (AugWoW), and 27.2 (DNLI). Using MNLI as an intermediate fine-tuning step before BEGIN-ADVERSARIAL helps the RoBERTa backbone (41.1 → 45.2 test F1, a gain the paper reports as "↑4.1 on F1") but very slightly hurts the T5 backbone on the same two columns (45.9 → 45.3 test F1, 46.3 → 46.1 dev F1) — a small regression the text doesn't comment on, though both rows are printed in the same table. The best F1 anywhere in the table is 45.9 (T5, test) and 46.3 (T5, dev); the paper's own framing — "none of the models exceed 46% F1" — holds for every test-set cell but not for two dev-set cells: T5 BEGIN-ADVERSARIAL (46.3) and T5 BEGIN-ADVERSARIAL+MNLI (46.1), on adjacent rows of the same table. Against this, the Human row reports 96.4% (test) and 97.2% (dev) — precision only, no recall or F1, since it measures how often an individual annotator's label agrees with the majority-vote label of that same trio of annotators, not agreement against an external ground truth.

A second finding cuts across every metric: performance depends heavily on extractivity — how much of a response's language is copied verbatim from the knowledge, measured with a density metric from Grusky et al. (2018) and split into low/medium/high-density thirds. For the lexical and semantic metrics, attributable responses score much higher when they're extractive than when they're abstractive (paraphrased), and the gap between categories narrows in the abstractive third; the paper reports that unattributable-but-extractive responses score higher on average than attributable-but-abstractive ones across these metrics. The RoBERTa classifiers show a related pattern (Figure 7): Attributable-response F1 rises with density in every one of the three training sets shown, while Unattributable-response F1 falls with density for the DNLI- and MNLI-trained classifiers and stays comparatively flat for the classifier trained on BEGIN-ADVERSARIAL+MNLI. Within this split, the paper singles out one exception to the general blurring of categories in the abstractive third: among low-density (abstractive) responses, Q² alone still keeps a clear Attributable/Not-Attributable separation, where the lexical and semantic metrics lose it.

Finally, the same corpus split that drives the category breakdown above also drives a robustness result: F1 on responses from TopicalChat and CMU-DoG is markedly lower than on WoW, and the text singles out one comparison to quantify it — RoBERTa-MNLI's F1 on Attributable responses falls by about 10 points on TopicalChat versus WoW. The authors connect this directly to knowledge-source length (WoW ≈ 27 words, CMU-DoG ≈ 215, TopicalChat ≈ 271): shorter spans, they conclude, are easier for every metric tried.

// does it hold up?

The eval read

BEGIN is exactly what it says it is: a diagnostic set for auditing metrics, not a leaderboard for dialogue systems, and its central claim — that current metrics conflate word overlap and document length with genuine attribution — survives every check we ran against its own tables and figures. But several parts of the evidence are narrower, or more entangled, than the surrounding prose implies.

The annotation is thinly documented for a paper whose whole foundation is human labels

The reported quality signal is a single Krippendorff's α of 0.7, and the paper never specifies exactly which judgments or which phase (pilot vs. main round) it was computed over — there's no per-category breakdown (agreement on distinguishing "not attributable but extractive" from "attributable," the paper's own key failure mode, could plausibly be much lower than agreement on the easy cases), and while the method section does say three annotators labeled each example, the paper never reports the size of the underlying annotator pool, their location, or their compensation (the acknowledgments thank a named vendor team, not a specific crowd platform). That's a real contrast with this lab's own later work: FaithDial reports a κ of 0.75 specifically on BEGIN's own attribution classes (a different, separate κ of 0.61 covers its own additional VRM speech-act labels) and discloses that its corpus came from just 10 retained AMT workers based in the US or Canada — and even then, FaithDial's own working taxonomy is a five-class extension of this paper's three (adding Partial Hallucination and Uncooperative), not a verbatim reuse. BEGIN gives a reader no way to tell whether its one omnibus 0.7 is hiding a much weaker signal specifically on the categories its headline findings depend on.

Seven of the eight metrics are evaluated only qualitatively

"We then use BEGIN to analyze eight evaluation metrics" reads as one uniform evaluation, but the protocol actually splits in two. The NLI-based classifiers get a real quantitative benchmark score — macro-averaged precision, recall, and F1 against the three-way label (Table 2). The other seven — BLEU, ROUGE, F1, BERTScore, BARTScore, BLEURT, and Q² — are evaluated by plotting their score distribution per category and reporting what's visually apparent (medians shift the right way; the boxes overlap a lot). No correlation coefficient, threshold-based accuracy, or AUC is reported for any of the seven. That's enough to support the paper's qualitative claim (attribution and lexical/semantic scores are entangled), but it means seven-eighths of "eight evaluation metrics" never get a number a future paper could try to beat.

Worth noting for a paper whose contribution is a meta-evaluation method: the paper doesn't claim that method is itself new. In Related Work it calls its own approach "a similar meta-evaluation of existing automatic metrics" to prior work on summarization (Fabbri et al., 2021; Gabriel et al., 2021), and credits Durmus et al. (2022) with the underlying finding — that reference-free metrics lean on spurious correlations like length — that this paper reproduces in the dialogue setting. What's new here is the substrate (a large, human-labeled attribution taxonomy purpose-built for grounded dialogue, with the extractivity and corpus stratification layered on top) rather than a new scoring function or statistical protocol.

What the classifier's 45.9% establishes, and what it doesn't

Table 2 does show something concrete: an adversarially-trained NLI classifier clears the gold-NLI-trained and AugWoW-trained baselines by a wide, consistent margin on both backbones, using a training set an order of magnitude smaller than MNLI. It just tops out well short of reliable — 45.9% test macro-F1 against a Human row that itself reports precision only (agreement with majority vote, not an external correctness check), so the ">95%" comparison in the text is a ceiling estimated from the annotation process itself, not from ground truth outside it. And the paper reports the RoBERTa backbone's gain from intermediate MNLI fine-tuning (+4.1 F1) without noting that the identical manipulation costs the T5 backbone a small amount on both splits — a detail visible only by reading the table, not the prose around it.

The response pool is small, base-sized, and dated to 2021

Three of the four systems are explicitly base-sized 2021 architectures — GPT2-base, T5-base, and a T5-based CTRL-DIALOG; DoHA's parameter count is never stated (only that it's "a BART-based conversational model") — and none of the four is instruction-tuned or retrieval-augmented. That's a reasonable design for a paper about metric robustness rather than model quality, but it means every distributional claim in the paper (the shape of the BLEURT box plots, the extractivity confound, the corpus-length effect) is calibrated against a hallucination profile — frequent, often blunt hallucinations from small non-retrieval models — that may not describe how a modern retrieval-augmented or heavily-tuned chat model fails.

Corpus and knowledge length are confounded, not isolated

The "shorter knowledge spans mean higher metric scores" conclusion is drawn from exactly three corpora, and those corpora differ in more than length: WoW, CMU-DoG, and TopicalChat also differ in domain, collection protocol (symmetric vs. asymmetric worker roles), and — per Figure 2(b), which prints no axis values — in the base rate of unattributable responses their trained models produce (read off the chart: roughly 61% for WoW, 68% for CMU-DoG, 82% for TopicalChat, against the paper's own precise figures of 70.1% overall and 30.8% for CTRL-DIALOG). The paper never holds corpus or model fixed while varying document length to isolate length as the causal factor; the length story and the corpus-identity story are the same three data points read two ways.

baselines

Four base-sized 2021 dialogue systems, no larger or retrieval-augmented model; the eight "metrics" split into one quantitatively benchmarked family (NLI classifiers, Table 2) and seven evaluated only by visual distribution overlap.

contamination

Not train/test leakage in the classic sense — BEGIN ships dev/test only, by design. Closer to real leakage: BEGIN-ADVERSARIAL's unattributable examples are built by perturbing "the knowledge spans K from the WoW test set" directly, and that same WoW test portion is what generated the WoW-derived responses annotated into BEGIN itself — so part of BEGIN-ADVERSARIAL's edge over MNLI/DNLI/AugWoW may be shared source material with BEGIN's own WoW test examples, not taxonomy fit.

what's missed

Per-category and per-phase inter-annotator agreement; a correlation coefficient for any of the seven non-classifier metrics; an independent (non-majority-vote) check on the ">95% human precision" ceiling; a design that varies knowledge length within a single corpus to separate length from corpus identity.

would raise confidence

Category-stratified κ; a modern (post-2022) instruction-tuned or RAG model added to the response pool; a length-controlled ablation within one corpus; a quantitative accuracy/correlation number for BLEU/ROUGE/F1/BERTScore/BARTScore/BLEURT/Q² to match Table 2's classifiers.

// bottom line

The paper's headline finding — that automatic attribution metrics lean on word overlap and length rather than genuine faithfulness — holds up against its own tables from every angle we checked. What's thinner is the infrastructure underneath the finding: one aggregate agreement number standing in for annotation quality, a quantitative score for only one of eight tested metrics, and a document-length effect read off three corpora that also differ along several other axes at once.

// takeaways

What to remember

  1. BEGIN reframes the target of evaluation: instead of ranking dialogue systems, it's a 12,288-example, three-way-labeled diagnostic set (23.5% Fully Attributable, 6.4% Generic, 70.1% Not Attributable) built to test whether eight existing automatic attribution metrics can be trusted at all — and its taxonomy became a measurement scale in its own right, since its own lead author's later FaithDial reports its headline faithfulness rate, and its own annotation-quality κ, as numbers in BEGIN's units rather than only borrowing the category names.
  2. The one metric that gets a real quantitative score — an NLI classifier fine-tuned on the authors' own adversarial silver data, BEGIN-ADVERSARIAL — clearly beats classifiers trained on MNLI, DNLI, or AugWoW on both a T5 and a RoBERTa backbone, yet still tops out at 45.9% test macro-F1, far below the annotators' own >95% majority-vote precision.
  3. Every metric tested conflates two things it shouldn't: literal word overlap with genuine attribution (extractive-but-unattributable responses often outscore abstractive-but-attributable ones), and knowledge-source length with faithfulness (scores fall as the grounding document gets longer) — though that length effect is read off only three corpora that differ in more ways than length alone.
