MedExpQA: Multilingual Benchmarking of Large Language Models for Medical Question Answering

The first multilingual medical-exam QA benchmark to include doctor-written gold explanations — and a demonstration that state-of-the-art medical RAG still barely beats no retrieval at all, especially outside English.

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

By 2024, medical-QA evaluation had two entrenched blind spots. First, essentially every widely-used benchmark — MedQA/USMLE (Jin et al., 2021), PubMedQA (Jin et al., 2019), MedMCQA, the MultiMedQA suite behind Med-PaLM (Singhal et al., 2023a), and the retrieval-focused MIRAGE (Xiong et al., 2024) — scores only a model's final multiple-choice letter. None of them ship reference explanations, so there is no way to check why a model landed on an answer, only whether it happened to be right. Second, and more starkly, every one of these benchmarks is English-only; the authors describe multilingual evaluation of medical LLMs as, "as far as we know, a totally neglected topic."

Both gaps matter together: claims that LLMs "pass" medical licensing exams (Singhal et al., 2023a; Nori et al., 2023) rest entirely on English multiple-choice accuracy, with no evidence about reasoning quality or about whether the same models are usable for clinicians working in Spanish, French, or Italian. MedExpQA is built to close both gaps in one benchmark: multilingual medical-exam questions paired with gold reference explanations written by practicing and resident physicians, usable both to grade final answers and to define upper-bound "gold knowledge" conditions against which automatic retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) can be measured.

// how it works

The method

MedExpQA is built on top of the Antidote CasiMedicos dataset (Agerri et al., 2023; Goenaga et al., 2023): Spain's annual Resident Medical Exams (Médico Interno Residente, MIR), for which the volunteer "CasiMedicos" project of practicing/resident physicians publishes gold reference explanations justifying the correct answer and, often, why each wrong option is wrong. The dataset covers 622 clinical-case documents per language, parallel across Spanish (original), English, French, and Italian — 2,488 documents total — split 434 train / 63 validation / 125 test per language. A typical document pairs a clinical case (137 tokens on average across the four languages) with multiple-choice options (79.6 tokens) — about 217 tokens of input combined — and a full explanation (E) averaging 170.25 tokens.

The benchmark defines three gold-knowledge grounding conditions, each fed to the LLM as context alongside the clinical case and options, to establish upper bounds: E — the full doctor-written explanation of both correct and incorrect options; EI — only the explanation of the incorrect options (testing whether knowing what's wrong is enough to infer what's right); and H — the full explanation with its explicit answer-pointing phrases (e.g. "this option is false") masked out by GPT-4 (prompted, not fine-tuned), so the model must reason over the medical content rather than pattern-match an explicit textual tell. The masking method was validated on 80 held-out documents (20 per language) against human annotation, reaching an average F1 of 0.85 (±0.02).

These gold conditions are compared against two automatic conditions: None (no external knowledge) and MedRAG (Xiong et al., 2024) — BM25 and MedCPT retrieval fused via Reciprocal Rank Fusion, over the MedCorp corpus (PubMed, medical textbooks, StatPearls, and Wikipedia), tested at two retrieval depths, RAG-7 and RAG-32. Four LLMs are evaluated as two general-purpose/medical-specialized pairs: LLaMA-2 13B vs. PMC-LLaMA 13B (LLaMA continued-pretrained on PubMed papers + textbooks), and Mistral 7B vs. BioMistral 7B (the DARE-merged variant, continued-pretrained on PubMed Central). Every model is tested both zero-shot and after LoRA fine-tuning (rank 8, alpha 16, 10 epochs per language, best checkpoint kept) directly on CasiMedicos. Accuracy is scored by matching the first character generated after the prompt "The correct answer is:" to the correct option letter.

// what they found

The results

Averaged across all four LLMs and all four languages (Figure 2), the gap between gold knowledge and automatic retrieval is stark. Zero-shot: full gold explanation (E) 82.2% vs. hidden-reference gold (H) 72.4% vs. incorrect-only gold (EI) 47.4% vs. RAG-32 (MedRAG at k=32) 37.6% vs. no grounding at all 35.6%. After LoRA fine-tuning on CasiMedicos: E 91.7%, H 83.9%, EI 72.1%, no-grounding 57.7%, RAG-32 54.4% — fine-tuning raises every condition, but RAG-32's average actually falls below the no-grounding baseline once models are fine-tuned.

The best model is the general-purpose one, not the medical specialist. Zero-shot with the full gold explanation (E), Mistral-7B tops every language: English 89.6%, Spanish 88.0%, Italian 87.2%, French 88.0% — beating the medical-specialized BioMistral-7B in the same setting (English 88.8%, Spanish 83.2%, Italian 80.8%, French 80.8%) and the larger PMC-LLaMA-13B (English 83.2%) and LLaMA-2-13B (English 81.6%). This pattern holds across nearly every setting the paper reports; after fine-tuning, BioMistral edges ahead only because of its especially high E scores (e.g. 94.4% English E, tied with Mistral's own 94.4%), while Mistral remains the strongest model on every other grounding condition.

RAG's gains are small and mostly not statistically significant. Zero-shot, the best automatic condition is RAG-7 (37.9% average) — barely ahead of no-grounding (35.6%) and RAG-32 (37.6%). Running a McNemar test on RAG-7/RAG-32 vs. the None baseline across all 64 zero-shot model×language cells in Table 8, only 5 are statistically significant at α = .05 (marked with an asterisk in the paper — four LLaMA-2 cells, Spanish and Italian at both k=7 and k=32, plus one Mistral cell, English at k=32). After fine-tuning, the pattern flips further: RAG-32 helps English specifically for Mistral (None 68.0% → RAG-32 75.2%, matching the paper's own "best results around 75% accuracy for English" framing), but for the same fine-tuned Mistral, RAG-32 reduces accuracy in every other language — Spanish 63.2% (None) → 55.2% (RAG-32), Italian 56.8% → 52.0%, French 66.4% → 60.0%.

The multilingual gap is real and shows up specifically in reasoning, not just raw grounding. Comparing the full explanation (E) to the same explanation with its explicit answer-pointers hidden (H) isolates how much of a model's score depends on textual shortcuts rather than medical content. For PMC-LLaMA zero-shot, English drops only 4.8 points (83.2% E → 78.4% H) while Spanish drops 14.4 points (77.6% → 63.2%); for Mistral, English drops 7.2 points (89.6% → 82.4%) versus 12.8 points in Spanish (88.0% → 75.2%). The paper also reports that a retrieval-depth sweep from 0 to 32 snippets shows a positive but plateauing trend for most models around 8 snippets (Figure 5) — except for PMC-LLaMA, whose zero-shot accuracy actually trends downward as more snippets are added.

// does it hold up?

The eval read

MedExpQA's central design is genuinely useful — using doctor-written explanations as controllable "gold knowledge" injections is a clean way to separate a model's reasoning capacity from its retrieval pipeline, and the authors are commendably transparent about where their own RAG results are statistically weak (the 5-of-64 significance count is reported plainly, not buried). But several parts of the eval design deserve scrutiny before treating the headline numbers as settled.

baselines

Only four open-source LLMs (13B/7B scale) are tested, all zero-shot or LoRA-fine-tuned with one fixed hyperparameter recipe (rank 8, alpha 16, 10 epochs) chosen without a per-model search — the authors state outright that an exhaustive grid search "exceeds the capacity of our lab." No GPT-4-class or larger open model is run on the benchmark itself, even though GPT-4 is used elsewhere in the pipeline (to generate the H masking). Of the two "medical-specialized" models, BioMistral trails its general-purpose base Mistral in essentially every zero-shot and fine-tuned setting, but PMC-LLaMA-13B actually beats LLaMA-2-13B zero-shot in all four languages (Avg. 57.9/46.8/46.9/46.1 vs. 52.1/43.2/42.7/45.2) and only falls behind LLaMA-2 after fine-tuning (69.3/58.7/62.7/61.6 vs. 71.1/67.6/65.6/66.5) — a genuinely interesting, pair-dependent finding, but one that means the benchmark, as currently populated, can't yet show whether medical continued-pretraining helps at frontier scale.

contamination

CasiMedicos is built from publicly commented Spanish government exams that have circulated online for years before these models were pretrained, and the paper does not test or discuss contamination at all. That cuts both ways: if pretraining leakage were driving scores, Spanish (the original-language exams) would be the likely leak vector, yet Spanish scores are consistently among the lowest — evidence against gross contamination, but not a substitute for an actual n-gram/decontamination check. More concerning for the paper's own "gold knowledge upper bound" framing: nothing rules out that some E/H explanation text itself (commented on every year by the same volunteer CasiMedicos community) was seen during pretraining, which would inflate the E/H ceiling numbers relative to what a genuinely novel medical explanation would produce.

what's missed

Despite billing itself as the benchmark that can finally "evaluate the reasoning of LLMs," MedExpQA only manipulates what knowledge is fed in — it never scores the model's own generated explanation against the CasiMedicos gold explanations. Accuracy is still graded on a single output character. The paper's own Concluding Remarks concede this: "Future work may involve evaluating LLMs not only regarding their accuracy in predicting the correct answer, but also on the quality of the explanations generated" — i.e. explanation-quality scoring doesn't exist yet in this release, only explanation-as-input.

would raise confidence

A decontamination check against each model's pretraining corpus; at least one frontier/proprietary model run directly on MedExpQA rather than only used as a masking tool; a per-model hyperparameter search (or an ablation showing results are recipe-robust); and, most importantly, an actual scored evaluation of model-generated explanations against the CasiMedicos gold text — turning the benchmark's namesake feature from an input-only manipulation into a real reasoning-quality metric.

// bottom line

MedExpQA is the right shape of benchmark — multilingual, with real doctor-written explanations, and honest about where its own RAG numbers are statistically thin — but its current results describe four small-to-mid-size open models under one untuned fine-tuning recipe, not a frontier-model or contamination-checked picture, and the "explanation quality" half of its premise is still future work rather than something this paper measures.

// takeaways

What to remember

  1. MedExpQA is the first multilingual medical-exam QA benchmark with doctor-written gold explanations — 622 CasiMedicos MIR-exam documents per language across English, Spanish, Italian, and French (2,488 total), each explanation usable as a controllable "gold knowledge" grounding condition.
  2. Gold explanations dominate automatic retrieval by a wide margin: zero-shot, the full gold explanation (E) averages 82.2% vs. just 37.9% for MedRAG's best automatic setting (RAG-7) and 35.6% with no grounding — and only 5 of 64 zero-shot RAG-vs-none comparisons are statistically significant.
  3. Fine-tuning lifts every condition but doesn't fix the language gap or make RAG reliably useful — fine-tuned Mistral's English accuracy rises to 75.2% with RAG-32 (up from 68.0% with no grounding), yet its Spanish, Italian, and French accuracy each fall under the same RAG-32 condition relative to no grounding.
