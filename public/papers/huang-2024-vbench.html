VBench: Comprehensive Benchmark Suite for Video Generative Models

VBench decomposes "video generation quality" into 16 disentangled, hierarchical dimensions -- each with its own hand-designed prompt suite and purpose-built automatic metric -- and then checks every one of those 16 metrics against large-scale human preference annotations, rather than reporting the single opaque number (FID, FVD, IS, CLIPSIM) the field had been using.

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

By late 2023, text-to-video (T2V) diffusion models were multiplying fast, but the metrics used to score them hadn't kept pace. The standard toolkit -- Inception Score (IS), Fréchet Inception Distance (FID), Fréchet Video Distance (FVD), and CLIPSIM -- collapses everything about a generated video into one number, and the paper's own introduction notes these metrics are "inconsistent with human judgement." That single-number design has two concrete costs: it can't tell you why a model is bad (poor temporal consistency? weak text alignment? both?), and it obscures a trade-off the authors later document directly in their own results -- a model can look artificially "consistent" simply by generating a nearly static video.

The prompts feeding these metrics were also borrowed rather than designed for the job: IS/FID/FVD lean on UCF-101's 101 class labels, and CLIPSIM leans on MSR-VTT's human-written video captions -- both real-video datasets never built to probe a generative model's specific failure modes (subject identity drift, physically implausible motion, style transfer, compositional binding of multiple objects). Meanwhile, off-the-shelf video-quality-assessment (VQA) methods are built to score real, camera-captured footage and don't account for the distinct artifacts of synthesized video.

VBench's stated bet is that video generation quality is not one thing but many disentangled things, each of which needs its own tailored prompt set, its own automatic metric, and -- critically -- independent proof that the metric actually agrees with what a human would say.

// how it works

The method

VBench is built from four linked components: an Evaluation Dimension Suite that defines what to measure, a Prompt Suite that supplies the test cases, an Evaluation Method Suite that scores each dimension automatically, and a Human Preference Annotation set used to verify that the automatic scores track human judgment.

16 dimensions, split two ways at the top

The dimension suite breaks "video generation quality" down top-down into two coarse categories, then into fine-grained dimensions under each -- 16 leaf dimensions in total:

  • Video Quality ("does the video alone look good, ignoring the prompt?") -- split into Temporal Quality (Subject Consistency via DINO feature similarity across frames; Background Consistency via CLIP feature similarity across frames; Temporal Flickering via mean absolute difference between static frames; Motion Smoothness via a video-frame-interpolation model's motion priors; Dynamic Degree via RAFT-estimated motion magnitude) and Frame-Wise Quality (Aesthetic Quality via the LAION aesthetic predictor; Imaging Quality via the MUSIQ distortion predictor trained on SPAQ).
  • Video-Condition Consistency ("does the video match what the prompt asked for?") -- split into Semantics (Object Class and Multiple Objects via GRiT detection; Human Action via UMT action recognition against Kinetics-400 action labels; Color via GRiT color captioning; Spatial Relationship via rule-based checking; Scene via Tag2Text captioning), Style (Appearance Style and Temporal Style, both via CLIP/ViCLIP feature similarity to style descriptions), and a standalone Overall Consistency dimension using ViCLIP video-text similarity on general prompts.

That's 7 dimensions under Video Quality and 9 under Video-Condition Consistency -- 16 total, each with a distinct automatic detector rather than one shared metric.

A prompt suite designed per dimension and per content category

For each of the 16 dimensions, the authors hand-curated "around 100 prompts" (their own hedge -- Figure 3's per-dimension counts range roughly in the 70s-100s, not a flat 100) built specifically to stress-test that dimension: Subject Consistency prompts guarantee a moving subject; Human Action prompts draw from 100 representative Kinetics-400 actions "with minimal semantic overlaps." Separately, an 8-category Prompt Suite per Category was built for Animal, Architecture, Food, Human, Lifestyle, Plant, Scenery, and Vehicles by having an LLM multi-label 800 internet-sourced prompts (following YouTube's own category scheme), then manually cleaning the labels down to exactly 100 prompts per category.

Human Preference Annotation and its QA process

To validate the 16 automatic metrics, the authors ran a large pairwise human-labeling exercise. For each prompt, they generate videos from 4 T2V models {LaVie, ModelScope, VideoCrafter, CogVideo}, forming a "group" of 4 videos; 5 such groups are sampled per prompt; each group is broken into all 4C2 = 6 pairwise combinations, and annotators pick the better video for a specific dimension only (video order randomized to avoid bias). That means a prompt suite of N prompts yields N × 5 × 6 pairwise comparisons per dimension. Labeling is done one dimension at a time, with annotators given a written instruction document and explicit exclusions (e.g., for Subject Consistency, "not to consider the degree of temporal flickering, or the video alignment with the text prompt").

Quality assurance is a 5-step procedure: (1) written labeling instructions per dimension, (2) a 60-sample pre-labeling trial used to correct annotator misunderstandings, (3) instructions updated from that trial, (4) post-labeling spot checks on a random 20% of each 60-sample package -- if the error rate in that 20% exceeds 10%, the whole package is re-labeled by a different annotator, and (5) the authors repeat that same spot-check on the annotators' output, with an entire dimension's data discarded and re-annotated from scratch if the error rate is still too high. Notably, none of this produces a reported inter-annotator agreement statistic (no Cohen's/Fleiss' kappa, or similar, appears anywhere in the main paper or its Human Preference Annotation appendix) -- the QA process controls error rate against the authors' own adjudication, not agreement between independent annotators on the same item.

// what they found

The results

VBench evaluates 4 open-source T2V models -- LaVie, ModelScope, VideoCrafter, and CogVideo -- across all 16 dimensions, plus two reference baselines: Empirical Min (approximated via Gaussian-noise clips for Video-Condition-Consistency dimensions, or frame concatenation for most Video-Quality dimensions) and Empirical Max, which is two different things depending on the dimension: for 5 dimensions (Motion Smoothness, Scene, Appearance Style, Temporal Style, Overall Consistency) it's the highest-scoring result among the top-5 WebVid-10M videos retrieved per prompt by CLIP text-feature similarity; for the other 11 dimensions the paper reports that a video can achieve a perfect score by construction, so it simply asserts 100% as the empirical maximum rather than measuring one. A separate WebVid-Avg baseline (the average score on those same retrieved WebVid-10M videos) is visualized in Figure 6(b) and tabulated in the Supplementary File’s Table A6 for the nine dimensions it is defined on, but is absent from the main paper's Table 1.

Two things are easy to misread in that table if you skip the baseline rows. First, several columns simply don't span 0-100%: the Empirical Max for Appearance Style is 28.55%, for Temporal Style 36.40%, for Overall Consistency 36.40% -- so CogVideo's 7.80% Temporal Style and 7.70% Overall Consistency look catastrophic next to a 100% ceiling but are closer to roughly a fifth of the way to the paper's own approximated ceiling. Second, the paper's own headline finding -- a trade-off between temporal consistency and dynamic degree -- is visible directly in the cells: VideoCrafter has the lowest score of the four models on all four temporal-consistency dimensions (Subject Consistency 86.24%, Background Consistency 92.88%, Temporal Flickering 97.60%, Motion Smoothness 91.79%) while posting the highest Dynamic Degree (89.72%, against LaVie's 49.72% and CogVideo's 42.22%). The paper reads this as models "cheating" temporal-consistency scores by generating relatively static video.

Validating the 16 metrics against humans

For each dimension, the authors compute a per-model win ratio from both the automatic VBench score and the human pairwise labels, then correlate the two using Spearman's ρ across the 4 models -- i.e., each scatter plot in Figure 5 has exactly 4 points, one per model.

Most dimensions correlate strongly with human preference: Motion Smoothness reaches ρ = 0.9980, Appearance Style ρ = 0.9965, Multiple Objects ρ = 0.9898, Aesthetic Quality ρ = 0.9865, and ten of the sixteen dimensions clear ρ = 0.93. The weakest is Color at ρ = 0.6073, followed by Object Class at ρ = 0.8037 and Dynamic Degree at ρ = 0.8209 -- the paper reports these values but does not discuss why Color in particular lags the rest, nor does it report a significance test or confidence interval alongside any of the sixteen ρ values.

Per-category insights and the T2V/T2I gap

Evaluating the same 4 models across the 8 content categories (Figure 7) surfaces two dataset-level observations the authors draw out explicitly. WebVid-10M allocates 26% of its content to the Human category -- the largest share of the eight -- yet Human is "one of the poorest results among eight categories," which the authors read as evidence that data volume alone doesn't fix a structurally hard category (they suggest human-specific priors like skeletons as a fix). Conversely, Food is only 11% of WebVid-10M by their count, yet "almost always" scores highest on Aesthetic Quality, which they read as a data-quality-over-quantity signal. Separately, comparing against three T2I models (SD1.4, SD2.1, SDXL) on 10 shared frame-wise dimensions, the T2V models "significantly underperform" on Multiple Objects and Spatial Relationship, which the paper partly attributes to weaker text encoders in the T2V models it tested (CLIP ViT-L alone for LaVie vs. OpenCLIP ViT-H for SD2.1 or CLIP ViT-L + OpenCLIP ViT-G for SDXL).

// does it hold up?

The eval read

VBench's core methodological move -- splitting one opaque score into 16 disentangled, independently validated dimensions -- is a genuine improvement over IS/FID/FVD/CLIPSIM, and the paper is honest about some of its own limits: it explicitly says it plans to add models "when they become available" and states plainly that it "does not assess safety and equality dimensions." But three things are worth flagging before treating its numbers, or its validation, as more solid than the paper itself claims.

The human-alignment check runs on only 4 data points per dimension. Figure 5's 16 scatter plots each report a Spearman's ρ, but by the paper's own description, each plot has exactly one dot per model -- 4 points total per dimension. Thousands of pairwise human comparisons were collected (N prompts × 5 groups × 6 pairs per dimension), but the validation metric collapses all of that into 4 aggregate win ratios before computing the correlation. A rank correlation over n=4 is fragile by construction (a single model's rank flip can swing ρ substantially), and no significance test, confidence interval, or per-prompt/per-video-level correlation is reported alongside any of the 16 ρ values -- so "ρ = 0.9980 for Motion Smoothness" and "ρ = 0.6073 for Color" are being compared on the same footing despite resting on the same tiny n. I checked the appendix's Human Preference Annotation section (I.1-I.2) and Section 4.2 for a finer-grained correlation (e.g. per-prompt or bootstrapped over comparisons) and found none reported.

Only 4 open-source models are benchmarked, all from the same generation of T2V systems. LaVie, ModelScope, VideoCrafter, and CogVideo were all released in 2022-2023; the paper's own Supplementary "Limitations and Future Work" section names its own bibliography entries for Gen-2, Morph Studio, Pika Labs, and Zeroscope-XL among the systems it hopes will "participate in the evaluation" in the future, but none of them are evaluated in Table 1 or Figure 2. That's a reasonable scope for a benchmark paper (open weights are needed to run 16 custom metrics), but it means every headline number in this paper describes a narrow, already-dated slice of 2023 open-source T2V, not the field the reader may assume "comprehensive" is describing.

No inter-annotator agreement statistic is reported. I searched the main paper's Section 3.3 and 4.2, and the appendix's full "I. Human Preference Annotation" section, for a Cohen's/Fleiss' kappa or any agreement figure between independent annotators on the same comparison -- there isn't one. What is reported is a QA error-rate process (post-labeling spot-checks against a 10% error threshold, adjudicated by the authors), which is a real quality control mechanism but answers a different question than "how often do two independent annotators agree" -- so the human labels' own internal reliability is unmeasured, even though those same labels are the yardstick the 16 automatic metrics are validated against.

None of this makes VBench's dimension taxonomy or metric design wrong -- the per-dimension results in Table 1 and the radar charts in Figures 2 and 7 are exactly the kind of fine-grained, model-differentiating output the paper set out to produce, and the trade-off it surfaces between temporal consistency and dynamic degree is a real, checkable pattern in its own numbers. The caution is narrower: treat the "human alignment" claim as validated at the coarse, 4-model level the paper actually tested, not as validated at the level of the individual human judgments that went into it.

The four models are not sampled under matched conditions, and the paper never flags it. Supplementary J.1 gives each model's settings, and they differ on the axes several dimensions are most sensitive to. LaVie is sampled at 16 frames of 512×512, ModelScope and VideoCrafter at 16 frames of 256×256, and CogVideo at 33 frames of 480×480 at 10 FPS. More consequentially, CogVideo doesn't receive the same prompts at all: “Since the official inference code requires simplified Chinese input, we translate all prompts into Chinese.” It is then scored on the same prompt suite as the others, and it lands last on Imaging Quality (41.03% against LaVie's 61.90%) and last on Object Class (73.40% against 91.82%) — a distortion metric and a semantic-matching metric, which are precisely what a resolution gap and a machine-translated prompt would move. None of this means CogVideo is secretly competitive; it means the table cannot separate model quality from sampling configuration and prompt language, and the paper doesn't attempt to. For a benchmark whose entire pitch is fine-grained, per-dimension attribution, that is the confound most worth stating out loud.

baselines

Empirical Min is the paper's own approximation (noise clips / frame concatenation). Empirical Max is a mix of two things: for 5 dimensions (Motion Smoothness, Scene, Appearance Style, Temporal Style, Overall Consistency) it's a measured top-5 WebVid-10M retrieval score; for the other 11 it's simply asserted as a theoretical 100%. Neither is an independently audited ceiling -- the four retrieval-based dimensions among them top out well below 100%, so raw percentages across dimensions are not comparable without these baseline rows.

contamination

Not directly applicable in the usual train/test-leakage sense (this is a generation benchmark, not a knowledge benchmark), but WebVid-Avg and the five retrieval-based Empirical Max cells are themselves built from WebVid-10M, and the paper describes WebVid-10M only as "the commonly used video generation training dataset" -- it never states whether any of the 4 evaluated models were actually trained on it, so the baselines' independence from the models under test is an open question the paper doesn't address either way.

what's missed

Per-video or per-prompt correlation between automatic and human scores (only a 4-point, per-model aggregate correlation is reported); any commercial/closed T2V system; an inter-annotator agreement statistic.

would raise confidence

Reporting Spearman's ρ (or an equivalent) computed at a finer grain than 4 points per dimension -- e.g. bootstrapped over prompts or videos -- plus a standard inter-annotator agreement figure for the human labels themselves.

// bottom line

VBench's contribution is the taxonomy and the metric-per-dimension design, not a load-bearing statistical proof of human alignment -- the paper's own Figure 5 validates that design against only 4 models per dimension, with no reported significance test or annotator-agreement figure, even as its abstract and introduction describe the suite as "comprehensive" six times between them.

// takeaways

What to remember

  1. VBench replaces one opaque score (FID/FVD/IS/CLIPSIM) with 16 disentangled dimensions under two coarse groups -- Video Quality (Temporal Quality: Subject/Background Consistency, Temporal Flickering, Motion Smoothness, Dynamic Degree; Frame-Wise Quality: Aesthetic Quality, Imaging Quality) and Video-Condition Consistency (Semantics: Object Class, Multiple Objects, Human Action, Color, Spatial Relationship, Scene; Style: Appearance, Temporal; plus standalone Overall Consistency) -- each scored by its own purpose-built detector (DINO, CLIP/ViCLIP, RAFT, LAION aesthetic predictor, MUSIQ, GRiT, UMT, Tag2Text).
  2. Its central validation claim -- that every dimension is human-aligned -- rests on Spearman correlations computed over just 4 models per dimension (ρ ranges from 0.6073 on Color to 0.9980 on Motion Smoothness), aggregated from thousands of pairwise comparisons with no reported per-video correlation, significance test, or inter-annotator agreement statistic.
  3. The paper's own numbers show a real trade-off: VideoCrafter posts the highest Dynamic Degree (89.72%) but the lowest score of the four models on every one of the four temporal-consistency dimensions, while LaVie and CogVideo show the reverse pattern -- exactly the kind of finding a single aggregate metric would have hidden.
