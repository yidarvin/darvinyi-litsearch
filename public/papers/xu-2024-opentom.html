OpenToM: A Comprehensive Benchmark for Evaluating Theory-of-Mind Reasoning Capabilities of Large Language Models

A theory-of-mind benchmark built by scripting characters with sampled personality traits and preferences, generating a motivated intention and action for them, and only then drafting the narrative — instead of filling a fixed Sally-Anne template. Its 23 questions per story push past object location into a genuinely psychological question type, character attitude, where zero-shot GPT-4-Turbo (0.544 F1) sits far below the paper's human-agreement ceiling (0.862) even as it leads most other question genres.

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

Most machine theory-of-mind (N-ToM) benchmarks descend from the Sally-Anne false-belief test: a character leaves the room, an object gets moved, and the model is asked where the character will look for it. ToMi (Le et al., 2019) turned this into a randomized story generator; Hi-ToM and Big-ToM extended it to higher-order beliefs and richer scenarios. The authors argue these template-based pipelines carry three structural costs. First, the narratives are artificial and ambiguous — a template sentence like "the stockings is in the drawer" never says whether a given character actually noticed the object there, so the "correct" answer can be underdetermined (Appendix A.1 works through a concrete ToMi example with this flaw). Second, the characters have no personality or preferences, and their actions are never motivated — nothing explains why an object gets moved, which strips out the causal structure a person would actually reason over. Third, and most consequentially, existing benchmarks ask almost exclusively about the physical world (where is the object, who saw it move) and essentially never about a character's psychological world — their attitudes, emotions, and reactions to what happened.

Table 1's comparison makes the gap concrete on scale and content alike. ToMi (999 narratives, 44.6 tokens on average) and Hi-ToM (1200 narratives, 213.68 tokens) are credited with physical-world ToM only — no social commonsense, no psychological ToM, no personified characters. Big-ToM (3000 narratives, 69.9 tokens) is marked as having personified characters but still no psychological-ToM question type, and FANToM (254 LLM-generated conversations averaging 1020 tokens) is credited with physical-world ToM but neither personification nor psychological ToM in the authors' scheme. The only prior dataset the table credits with all four properties — social commonsense, physical ToM, psychological ToM, and personified characters — is FauxPas-EAI, and it has just 44 narratives. OpenToM is built to combine that full feature set with real scale: 596 standard narratives (194.3 tokens on average) plus 100 extra-long OpenToM-L narratives (491.6 tokens, about 2.5× longer), each attached to 23 questions.

// how it works

The method

Every OpenToM story has two protagonists — a mover, who acts on an entity-of-interest, and an observer, who may or may not witness the action — plus several locations and containers, with the initial world state seeded from ToMi's location/container templates. Generation runs as a four-stage, human-in-the-loop pipeline (Figure 2). (1) Character personification: each character is assigned a personality trait sampled from three candidates — considerate, inconsiderate, negativistic — and a binary preference (like/hate the entity) toward the entity-of-interest. To block a spurious shortcut where a model could infer the mover's intention directly from the mover's own known personality, the pipeline also generates a (possibly false) belief the mover holds about the observer's preference, deliberately flipping it in some stories. (2) Intention and enaction: given the sampled personality, preference, and preference-belief, GPT-3.5-Turbo generates the mover's intention (e.g., "move the duck so it won't disturb Sam") and the concrete enaction that follows from it, which updates the world state. (3) Plot and narrative: a three-paragraph plot is assembled — personal preferences and beliefs, a prologue establishing the initial world state, and a main-event paragraph describing the mover's personality, intention, and action, explicitly stating whether the observer witnessed it — and GPT-3.5-Turbo (GPT-4-Turbo for the extra-long OpenToM-L split) turns that plot into narrative prose. (4) Human revision: annotators review every draft for narratives that leak the answer through lexical overlap with a question or otherwise reveal information a character shouldn't have access to; 17.8% of drafts were revised for this reason.

The paper formalizes the spurious-correlation risk in Attitude questions as a small Bayesian network (Figure 3): the mover's intention (Int) is caused by the mover's own preference, personality trait (T), and possibly their belief about the observer's preference; the enacted action (Act) follows from Int; and the observer's attitude (Att) should really be inferred only from what the observer can actually see — their own preference and the mover's action. But a model could shortcut this by conditioning on Int or T directly (the dashed edges in Figure 3), both of which are latent to the observer in the story but visible to a model reading the full narrative. Perturbing the mover's belief about the observer's preference (step 1) is the mechanism the authors use to sever both of these shortcuts at once (Appendix A.5).

Each narrative is paired with 23 questions across three genres, asked at both first order (character c's own perception) and second order (character c's belief about another character's perception), except Attitude, which is first-order only. Location (Loc) questions come in two granularities — Loccoarse, a binary judgment of whether the entity is still in its initial location (4 questions per narrative: 1st+2nd order × mover+observer), and Locfine, its exact named location (6 questions) — deliberately split apart so faithfulness between the two can be checked (§4.1). Multi-Hop (MHop) questions (12 per narrative, the largest share) add a further reasoning hop on top of Loc, asking how a container's fullness or the entity's accessibility changed; answering an accessibility question correctly requires social-commonsense knowledge that other people's containers/backpacks aren't there to take from without permission. Attitude (Att) questions (1 per narrative) ask what the observer's reaction to the mover's action would likely be, and are the paper's psychological-ToM contribution: OpenToM narratives are deliberately written to maximize the contrast between the observer's own preference and the mover's action (e.g., a considerate mover who nonetheless takes an observer's favorite object), so the "correct" attitude has a clear, low-ambiguity answer even though human attitudes in general are complex and multifaceted.

To check that this design produces low-ambiguity ground truth, the authors had 3 annotators independently label a 100-narrative, 5-question-per-narrative validation sample spanning all three genres and both orders, and report the resulting macro-averaged pairwise inter-annotator agreement per question type as the "Human" reference column in the main results table.

// what they found

The results

Six zero-shot models were evaluated — Llama2-Chat 7B/13B/70B, Mixtral-8x7B-Instruct, GPT-3.5-Turbo, and GPT-4-Turbo (the 1106 checkpoints) — plus a LoRA-finetuned Llama2-Chat-13B trained on an 80/20 in-domain split of OpenToM itself, each scored over five 50-narrative resamples (mean ± std, macro-F1). Vanilla zero-shot GPT-4-Turbo leads most genres by a large margin: 0.643 F1 on first-order Loccoarse and 0.658 on first-order MHop, against inter-annotator agreement of 0.990 and 0.855 respectively. But it is not the best model on every genre — on first-order Locfine, GPT-4-Turbo (0.507) trails Llama2-Chat-13B (0.545), Llama2-Chat-70B (0.534), and GPT-3.5-Turbo (0.515); and on second-order Loccoarse, Llama2-Chat-7B (0.462) edges out GPT-4-Turbo (0.442). On Attitude, every zero-shot model is far below agreement: GPT-4-Turbo's 0.544 F1 is the best of the six, still 0.318 below the 0.862 human-agreement figure. The in-domain finetuned Llama2-13B reaches 0.978 on first-order Loccoarse and 0.936 on first-order MHop, showing those question types are comparatively easy to learn from labeled data, but only reaches 0.547 on Attitude — barely above zero-shot GPT-4-Turbo and still well short of human agreement.

Table 3 reports CoT and SimToM results for Mixtral, GPT-3.5-Turbo, and GPT-4-Turbo. CoT was also run on all three Llama2-Chat sizes (Appendix Tables A7/A8), but its free-form reasoning chains were mostly unparseable there — 41–95% of outputs were flagged as corrupted and dropped, so Llama2-Chat's Locfine and Attitude results under CoT go unreported entirely and its Loccoarse/MHop numbers are omitted from Table 3's headline comparison; SimToM, by contrast, was never run on the Llama2-Chat models at all (the paper cites their "incompetency in generating character-centric narratives"). For the three models Table 3 reports, CoT brings GPT-4-Turbo to within the paper's human-level margin (±0.050) on first-order Loccoarse (0.643→0.942, +0.299) and on both orders of MHop (first-order 0.658→0.835, +0.177; second-order 0.637→0.755, +0.118) — but it degrades Locfine performance for all three tested models (GPT-4-Turbo first-order: 0.507→0.450, −0.057; second-order: 0.269→0.187, −0.082), and its effect on Attitude is marginal (GPT-4-Turbo: 0.544→0.580, +0.036 — still 0.282 below human agreement). SimToM prompting (asking the model to first write a character-centric narrative, then answer from it) shows a mixed pattern across models and question types rather than a uniform gain: it helps GPT-4-Turbo's Loccoarse (+0.195/+0.243 first/second-order) but hurts its Attitude score (−0.056), and for Mixtral it helps MHop while hurting first-order Loccoarse and Attitude (−0.098 and −0.072, both statistically significant); its second-order Loccoarse change for Mixtral, −0.004, falls inside the paper's own ±0.010 "equal performance" band.

Two further analyses probe how models are getting these scores. First, a faithfulness check (§4.1, Table A9): since Loccoarse should logically constrain Locfine (if a model says the entity moved, it shouldn't then say it's still in the initial container), the authors flag a model as "unfaithful" whenever its answers to the paired question contradict. When the two questions are asked separately, GPT-4-Turbo's unfaithful rate is 0.100 on first-order pairs and 0.200 on second-order pairs; presenting both questions jointly in one session lowers this to 0.030 and 0.112. Llama2-Chat-7B — the model that topped second-order Loccoarse — is unfaithful on 0.802 of its first-order pairs and 0.598 of its second-order pairs when asked separately, meaning most of its answers are internally contradictory (Llama2-Chat-7B is excluded from Figure 4 because its rate is off the chart's scale). Second, a role-gap analysis (Table 4, §4.2) compares each model's F1 on mover-centric versus observer-centric questions: on second-order Locfine questions, all five tested models score worse modeling the mover's mental state than the observer's (Llama2-13B −0.041, Llama2-70B −0.050, Mixtral −0.132, GPT-3.5-Turbo −0.333, GPT-4-Turbo −0.076); on second-order Loccoarse questions the pattern splits, with three models still worse (Llama2-70B −0.035, Mixtral −0.040, GPT-3.5-Turbo −0.029) but the other two better (Llama2-13B +0.047, GPT-4-Turbo +0.129). On MHop questions, in contrast, all five models do better on mover-centric than observer-centric questions, in both first order (+0.009 to +0.320) and second order (+0.008 to +0.176).

A follow-up analysis of Attitude errors (§4.3, Table 5) finds a specific failure mode: models substitute the mover's personality trait for genuine reasoning about what the observer can actually see. The paper reports that, across all four prompting methods tested (vanilla, CoT, SimToM, Self-Ask), more than 95% of the narratives where GPT-4-Turbo mistakes a positive attitude for a negative one have a mover with an inconsiderate or negativistic personality — i.e., the model appears to be pattern-matching "inconsiderate mover → bad outcome" rather than tracking what the observer actually witnessed, exactly the personality→attitude shortcut the story-generation pipeline was designed to break.

// does it hold up?

The eval read

OpenToM's central idea — build the narrative from a personality/preference/intention model instead of a fixed template, then design questions that specifically try to defeat the shortcuts that model creates — is a genuine step up from ToMi-style generation, and the faithfulness and role-gap analyses (not just the headline F1 table) are exactly the kind of second-pass scrutiny that keeps a leaderboard number from being over-read. A few things temper how far the headline numbers should be trusted, though.

baselines

The comparison in the main results table isn't fully apples-to-apples: the "FT" column is a Llama2-Chat-13B model both finetuned and tested on an 80/20 in-domain split of OpenToM itself (Appendix D.3), so its high Loccoarse/MHop scores reflect learning OpenToM's specific label distribution and narrative style, not zero-shot generalization the way the other six columns do — placing it in the same table as the zero-shot models invites a same-row comparison the setup doesn't actually support. Separately, only six models are tested, all snapshotted to the December 2023–January 2024 window (the 1106 GPT checkpoints, Llama2, Mixtral-8x7B); the paper's own Limitations section acknowledges the budget-driven model selection, but as of this writing none of the tested models are current frontier systems.

contamination

Pretraining-leakage contamination is unlikely — every narrative is freshly generated in December 2023 from sampled personality/preference/intention combinations. The more relevant risk is a self-generation loop: OpenToM's narratives are drafted by GPT-3.5-Turbo (GPT-4-Turbo for OpenToM-L), and GPT-3.5-Turbo/GPT-4-Turbo are then two of the six models evaluated on that same GPT-authored text. The paper doesn't test whether this gives the GPT family a stylistic home-field advantage over Llama2/Mixtral independent of ToM ability — e.g. by having a disjoint model family draft a held-out control split.

what's missed

The "Human" column read as a performance ceiling in Table 2 is actually pairwise inter-annotator agreement among 3 graduate-student annotators on a 100-narrative subsample (§2.6), not independently verified human accuracy against a separate gold standard — a reasonable proxy for label quality, but a softer claim than "this is what a human scores on this task." And the paper's own faithfulness result complicates a literal read of the leaderboard: Llama2-Chat-7B's apparent win on second-order Loccoarse (0.462 F1, beating GPT-4-Turbo's 0.442) coincides with an 0.802 unfaithful rate on paired first-order questions — a model contradicting itself on roughly 4 in 5 pairs isn't demonstrating superior ToM, it's demonstrating that F1 alone can't distinguish real reasoning from noise, and a reader skimming only the headline table would miss this. Table A9 itself has an internal inconsistency worth flagging: its printed Separate-condition row for GPT-3.5-Turbo reads 0.054 (first-order) / 0.000 (second-order) unfaithful rate — a second-order value that contradicts both Figure 4's bar chart for the same cell (~0.22) and the paper's own claim that the Joint approach lowers unfaithfulness relative to Separate for the GPT models (Joint GPT-3.5-Turbo is 0.128 second-order, higher than the table's printed 0.000 Separate figure). Readers relying on the appendix table rather than the figure for this cell would be misled.

would raise confidence

A disjoint-generator control (narratives drafted by a model family not in the evaluation set) to rule out in-family stylistic advantage; an independently collected human-accuracy baseline (not just agreement) run under the identical zero-shot protocol given to the LLMs, including on Attitude and second-order questions; and reporting the Table 5 personality-correlation statistics with the underlying error counts and confidence intervals, since "more than 95%" over what's likely a modest number of misclassified narratives is a striking claim that's hard to calibrate without the raw n.

// bottom line

OpenToM's generative pipeline and its Attitude question type are real contributions — they get at psychological-state ToM in a way ToMi/Hi-ToM/Big-ToM structurally can't, and the 0.318 GPT-4-Turbo-vs-human gap on Attitude (which CoT barely narrows) is a credible, specific finding about where LLM ToM breaks down. But the paper's own faithfulness and role-gap studies are doing real evidentiary work that the single F1 leaderboard doesn't convey on its own — read Table 2 alongside Table A9's unfaithfulness rates, not instead of them, and treat the finetuned column as evidence about learnability from labeled OpenToM data rather than as a zero-shot benchmark result.

// takeaways

What to remember

  1. OpenToM generates its 696 narratives (596 standard + 100 extra-long OpenToM-L) from a personality/preference/intention model rather than a fixed template, specifically to give characters motivated actions and to support a genuinely psychological question type (Attitude) that prior N-ToM benchmarks like ToMi, Hi-ToM, and Big-ToM don't ask.
  2. Zero-shot GPT-4-Turbo leads most of the 23 question types per story but is not uniformly best — it trails three other models on first-order Locfine and loses to Llama2-Chat-7B on second-order Loccoarse — and CoT prompting that helps location and multi-hop questions actively hurts Locfine accuracy and barely moves Attitude, so "the best model on average" and "the best model on any given genre" are different claims here.
  3. The paper's own faithfulness study is the sharpest caution against reading the F1 leaderboard alone: Llama2-Chat-7B's apparent second-order Loccoarse win comes with an 0.802 rate of self-contradictory answers, and even GPT-4-Turbo contradicts itself on 3–20% of location-question pairs, depending on question order and prompting setup — worst when the two Loc questions are asked separately (10% first-order, 20% second-order), best when they're asked jointly in one session (3% first-order, 11% second-order).
