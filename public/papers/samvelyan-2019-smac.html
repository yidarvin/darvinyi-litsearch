The StarCraft Multi-Agent Challenge

Fourteen StarCraft II micromanagement fights, each unit its own decentralised, partially-observable agent battling a scripted AI opponent — packaged as a standard benchmark for cooperative multi-agent RL, alongside an open-source framework, PyMARL, implementing QMIX, QTRAN, COMA, VDN and IQL.

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

By 2019, single-agent deep RL had standard, shared benchmarks — the Arcade Learning Environment and MuJoCo — that let the field measure progress reproducibly across labs. Cooperative multi-agent RL had nothing comparable. The authors note that a "growing number of recent works" (Foerster et al.'s COMA, Rashid et al.'s QMIX, Sunehag et al.'s VDN, Lowe et al.'s MADDPG) were each evaluating on their own one-off environments, "which can be overly simple or tuned to the proposed algorithms," making it hard to tell whether a new algorithm was actually better or just better suited to its own bespoke test bed.

The existing multi-agent testbeds each missed something. Keepaway Soccer and its Half Field Offense extension (built on the RoboCup simulator) use simplified 2D physics and don't scale in difficulty with the number of agents, since most agents need to do little coordination. Lowe et al.'s MADDPG environments are simple grid-worlds focused on low-level continuous control. Leibo et al.'s social-dilemma gridworlds were never released as code. Yang et al. and Zheng et al.'s many-agent gridworlds scale to hundreds or millions of agents but keep dynamics deliberately simple to stay tractable, targeting emergent behaviour rather than partial observability. Resnick et al.'s Pommerman (Bomberman-based) is closer in spirit but stays a simple 2D grid-world. None combined partial observability, genuinely challenging dynamics, and high-dimensional observation spaces in one place — the combination the paper argues cooperative MARL actually needs to be tested against.

SMAC's answer is to reuse a game that already has that complexity built in: StarCraft II. Rather than the full game (macromanagement plus micromanagement, played by a single centralised controller, as in DeepMind's SC2LE / AlphaStar line of work), SMAC isolates micromanagement — fine-grained combat control — and hands each individual unit to its own independent, decentralised learning agent that can only see a local neighbourhood of the battlefield. That reframing turns a familiar RTS game into a Dec-POMDP testbed purpose-built for the field's actual open problems: non-stationarity from simultaneously-learning agents, multi-agent credit assignment, and representing the value of joint actions.

// how it works

The method

SMAC formalises each scenario as a Dec-POMDP: a tuple of the true state, the joint action space, a state-transition function, a shared team reward, an observation function, the number of agents, and a discount factor. Each agent conditions its policy only on its own action-observation history, but training is centralised — the learning algorithm can see all agents' histories and the global state — while execution stays fully decentralised: a trained agent acts on nothing but its own local view. This centralised-training-with-decentralised-execution (CTDE) split is what lets algorithms like COMA and QMIX exploit extra information during learning without cheating at test time.

G = ⟨S, U, P, r, Z, O, n, γ⟩ // the Dec-POMDP tuple: state space, joint action space, transitions, shared team reward, observation space, observation function, agent count, discount

Each scenario is a battle between two armies on a StarCraft II map. Every allied unit is its own agent; the enemy army is controlled by the game's built-in scripted AI at difficulty level 7 ("very difficult"). An agent observes, within a fixed sight range (set to 9), the distance, relative x/y position, health, shield, and unit type of every ally and enemy unit in view, plus allies' last actions and eight terrain-height/walkability samples around it. It can only issue attack[enemy_id] against targets inside a separate, shorter shooting range (set to 6) — having sight reach further than fire forces agents to move into range before they can act, rather than sniping blindly. The action set is move[direction] (four compass directions), attack[enemy_id] (or heal[agent_id] for Medivac units), stop, and a no-op for dead agents; the number of legal actions per agent ranges from 7 to 70 depending on the scenario. The default reward is shaped: hit-point damage dealt each timestep, plus a bonus of 10 for each enemy killed and 200 for winning the battle, rescaled so a scenario's maximum cumulative reward is around 20; a sparse +1/-1 win/loss reward is offered as an alternative for researchers who want it.

SMAC ships 14 scenarios, built from three design patterns. Symmetric battles field identical armies on both sides (e.g. 2s3z: 2 Stalkers & 3 Zealots vs. the same), which is interesting whenever units "counter" each other by type — agents must learn to protect teammates who are weak against a specific enemy unit type. Asymmetric battles give the enemy a numeric edge (e.g. 5m_vs_6m: 5 allied Marines vs. 6 enemy Marines), forcing agents to track enemy health to focus fire efficiently. Micro-trick scenarios each require one specific tactic: corridor (6 Zealots vs. 24 Zerglings) needs the allies to wall off a map choke point; 3s_vs_5z (3 Stalkers vs. 5 Zealots) needs sustained kiting, since Zealots counter Stalkers in melee. The full 14-map roster spans unit compositions from 2 vs. 1 up to 27 vs. 30, with the largest army-size mismatch in 2c_vs_64zg (2 Colossi vs. 64 Zerglings — the paper's largest enemy count).

Alongside the benchmark, the authors release PyMARL — a separate, modular PyTorch codebase (github.com/oxwhirl/pymarl, distinct from the SMAC environment itself at github.com/oxwhirl/smac) built to make deep MARL research reproducible. PyMARL implements QMIX, QTRAN and COMA as "state-of-the-art" methods and VDN and IQL as baselines, batches data aggressively for speed, and wraps replay-buffer handling in a single data structure so new algorithms can be dropped in without re-plumbing the training loop.

// what they found

The results

The evaluation protocol pauses training every 10,000 timesteps to run 32 greedy, fully-decentralised test episodes; the fraction that end with every enemy unit dead within the time limit is the test win rate. Each configuration is run 5 independent times, and the paper reports the median test win % with 25-75th percentile bands rather than the mean, to avoid outliers dominating the picture. A heuristic AI — always attack the closest enemy, a basic form of focus fire — is included as a floor: the paper describes its performance as "relatively poor" — it averages roughly 19% across the 14 maps and scores 0% on nine of them — though unevenly so, since it actually beats both IQL and COMA on 2s3z (90 vs. 75/43) and 1c3s5z (81 vs. 21/31); the paper offers this as evidence that SMAC generally needs more than naive focus-firing to solve.

Averaged across all 14 maps (Figure 3), QMIX comes out on top, and is the single best-performing algorithm on up to eight of the fourteen maps at once during training. IQL, VDN and QMIX all clearly beat COMA, which the paper attributes to the sample-efficiency gap between off-policy value-based methods and on-policy policy gradients. Based on this cross-algorithm performance, the authors sort the 14 maps into three difficulty tiers: Easy (2s_vs_1sc, 2s3z, 3s5z, 1c3s5z, 10m_vs_11m), Hard (2c_vs_64zg, bane_vs_bane, 5m_vs_6m, 3s_vs_5z), and Super Hard (3s5z_vs_3s6z, 6h_vs_8z, 27m_vs_30m, MMM2, corridor). On the five Easy maps, QMIX exceeds 95% test win rate on all five, while IQL and COMA "struggle even on the Easy scenarios, performing poorly on four of the five." On Super Hard, only QMIX "makes meaningful progress" — and only on two of the five (27m_vs_30m and MMM2); the other three stay essentially unsolved by every algorithm tested. A separate experiment (Figure 7) runs QTRAN on three of the easiest maps, where it underperforms VDN and QMIX and, on 3s5z, "fails to achieve good performance."

// does it hold up?

The eval read

The most important thing to notice is who wrote the winning algorithm. Six of the paper's ten authors — Rashid, Samvelyan, Schroeder de Witt, Farquhar, Foerster, and Whiteson — are also the authors of QMIX (its entire author list), the method that comes out ahead on the aggregate metric and on the individual-map "best on N maps" count in Figure 3. That doesn't make the numbers wrong (QMIX's edge over VDN and IQL is consistent and the losses to a naive heuristic are real), but the same lab designed the benchmark, chose which four-to-five algorithms to compare, and then used exactly those algorithms' relative performance to define the Easy/Hard/Super-Hard difficulty labels that every subsequent paper on SMAC still cites. That's a difficulty taxonomy calibrated against its own creators' method, not an independent measure — a point worth remembering whenever "beats QMIX on the Hard maps" gets treated as a strong claim.

The aggregate headline — QMIX's rising curve toward roughly 60% median win rate averaged across all 14 maps in Figure 3 — is also doing more work than it should. Five of the fourteen maps are Easy, where QMIX and VDN are already at 84-100% (Table 2) — and even the naive heuristic clears 90% on 2s3z and 81% on 1c3s5z, two of those five; an aggregate that folds five near-saturated maps in with five essentially-unsolved Super Hard maps compresses the real signal. Zoom into the Super Hard tier alone and the picture is much starker: three of five maps (3s5z_vs_3s6z, 6h_vs_8z, corridor) top out at 0-3% for every algorithm including QMIX, and even the two maps where QMIX does "make meaningful progress" plateau well short of solved (49% on 27m_vs_30m, 69% on MMM2). None of that failure shows up distinctly in the single averaged curve the paper leads with.

baselines

Four learned algorithms (IQL, COMA, VDN, QMIX) plus a hand-coded focus-fire heuristic; QTRAN is checked separately on only 3 of the 14 maps. IQL (1993) and COMA/VDN (2017-2018) were state-of-the-art at the time, but QMIX — the paper's best performer — was co-designed by the same lab that built SMAC and calibrated its difficulty tiers, so "best baseline" and "benchmark author's own method" are the same entity here.

contamination

Not a data-leakage risk in the LLM sense — this is a simulation, not a scraped corpus — but the closer analogue is benchmark/method co-design: the Easy/Hard/Super-Hard split is defined post hoc from these five algorithms' own scores, not from an independent difficulty measure (e.g. state-space size, optimal-policy complexity). The opponent AI is a single fixed scripted policy (difficulty 7) with no adversarial diversity, and fairness (map files, AI difficulty, episode limits) is enforced by an honor-system recommendation in Appendix B, not automated checks.

what's missed

The aggregate win-rate curve buries the Super Hard failures that are arguably the paper's most interesting result. Win rate alone says nothing about sample efficiency in wall-clock terms (each run took "8 to 16 hours" on a GTX 1080 Ti, per Appendix B, but this isn't reported per-algorithm in the main results), nor about generalisation: every scenario starts from a fixed initial unit composition and position, so an agent that overfits to one deterministic starting configuration would look identical to one that generalises.

would raise confidence

An independently-set difficulty taxonomy (e.g. via search-based optimal-policy estimates rather than post hoc algorithm scores), a broader baseline set beyond the same lab's own QMIX/QTRAN lineage, formal significance testing across the 5 seeds rather than percentile bands alone, and randomised or held-out start-state variants of the Super Hard maps to separate memorisation from genuine coordination skill.

// bottom line

SMAC did what it set out to do — give cooperative MARL a shared, standardised, partially-observable benchmark the way ALE did for single-agent RL, and its 1,266 citations and continued use years later back that up. But the specific headline in this paper (QMIX leads the aggregate) should be read as "the benchmark's own designers' algorithm leads a benchmark tuned against exactly that algorithm's performance," and the more durable finding is the one buried under the average: half the Super Hard tier was unsolved by every 2019 algorithm, QMIX included.

// takeaways

What to remember

  1. SMAC ports the "ALE for single-agent RL" idea to cooperative multi-agent RL: 14 partially-observable StarCraft II micromanagement scenarios, each unit its own decentralised agent, grouped into Easy (5 maps), Hard (4 maps), and Super Hard (5 maps) tiers — plus a standalone open-source framework, PyMARL, implementing QMIX, QTRAN, COMA, VDN and IQL.
  2. QMIX wins on aggregate (best on up to 8 of 14 maps, >95% win rate on all 5 Easy maps) but the tiering that makes that look impressive was defined by the same lab's own algorithm's scores — and even QMIX makes "meaningful progress" on only 2 of the 5 Super Hard maps (27m_vs_30m: 49%, MMM2: 69%), with the other three (3s5z_vs_3s6z, 6h_vs_8z, corridor) at 0-3% for every algorithm tested.
  3. Treat the single averaged win-rate curve with caution: folding five near-saturated Easy maps into the same aggregate as five essentially-unsolved Super Hard maps flattens exactly the signal — near-total failure on the hardest tier — that a benchmark like this exists to surface.
