Understanding Factuality in Abstractive Summarization with FRANK: A Benchmark for Factuality Metrics

A 2,250-summary, human-annotated benchmark that replaces the binary factual/non-factual label with a seven-category typology of factual-error kinds — then uses it to show that FactCC and (per the authors' bug-fixed arXiv v2 — the published NAACL PDF has an uncorrected BERTScore result) BERTScore Precision are the two best-correlating automatic metrics, though even they still only weakly track human judgment.

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

By 2021, summarization research had a small pile of factuality metrics — FactCC, DAE, FEQA, QAGS — each proposed to catch the fabrications that fluent abstractive summarizers produce, and each evaluated on its own private slice of data with its own protocol. None of them could be compared to any other, because no shared benchmark existed. Worse, every one of them collapsed factuality into a single binary label: a summary (or sentence) was "factual" or it wasn't. That framing throws away exactly the information a system builder needs — which kind of mistake a model made, and how many of them — and it rests on a genuinely hard human judgment. Falke et al. (2019) had already shown crowd workers agree with expert factuality labels only about as often as κ = 0.65–0.74, even with nine or more annotators per item — evidence that "factual or not" alone is a fragile construct to build metrics against.

FRANK's authors argue the fix isn't a bigger binary-labeled dataset, it's a better-defined judgment task. If factual errors are decomposed into linguistically motivated categories — grounded in frame semantics and discourse theory — the resulting decision at the sentence level becomes more objective, annotators agree with each other and with experts far more reliably, and a benchmark built on the typology can score both summarization systems ("which mistakes do they make?") and the metrics meant to catch those mistakes ("which metric best predicts the human judgment?") on one common dataset.

// how it works

The method

The typology has three linguistic levels. Semantic frame errors — a predicate and its arguments are wrong: PredE (the relation itself is wrong), EntE (a core argument, e.g. an entity, is wrong or swapped), CircE (a non-core modifier like location or time is wrong). Discourse errors — the frame is fine but how statements relate to each other isn't: CorefE (a pronoun's antecedent is wrong or missing), LinkE (a temporal/causal connector between statements is wrong). Content verifiability errors — the statement can't be checked against the source at all: OutE (information not in the article — "extrinsic hallucination" in Maynez et al.'s terms) and GramE (grammar so broken the sentence is unverifiable). Two catch-alls, OthE (other) and NE (not an error), round out the scheme.

To build the dataset, the authors collected publicly available outputs from five CNN/DM systems (S2S, PGN, Bottom-Up Summarization, BERTSum, BART) and four XSum systems (PGN, TConvS2S, TranS2S, BERTS2S) — chosen to span a range of factuality behavior, from LSTM sequence-to-sequence models to pretrained transformers. For 250 articles per dataset, three independent MTurk annotators judged every sentence of every summary — factual or not, and if not, which categories apply, since a sentence can carry more than one error. Sentences were shown in the context of the full summary so discourse-level errors were visible. In total: 2,250 summaries, 4,942 annotated sentences.

Annotation quality was actively engineered, not just measured after the fact: a qualification test requiring >85% accuracy before a worker could annotate; continuous evaluation against artificially inserted errors (negated predicates, swapped pronouns, sentences from the wrong article, scrambled grammar) with workers blocked below 50% correct on these checks; a per-article "entity not in the article" probe to confirm workers actually read the text; and above-minimum-wage pay plus quality bonuses. The result: Fleiss' κ = 0.58 (p = 91% majority agreement) on the binary factual/non-factual judgment across all 4,942 sentences, and κ = 0.39 (p = 73.9%) on the finer 8-category split, restricted to the subset where all three annotators agreed a sentence was non-factual. Measured separately against a single domain expert on a 201-datapoint sample (10 CNN/DM + 10 XSum summaries), crowd-majority labels reached Cohen's κ = 0.86 — markedly higher than the κ = 0.65 Falke et al. (2019) reported for a comparable crowd-vs-expert setup, and higher than the ≤ 0.74 they reached with more than nine workers per item.

The benchmarking half of FRANK evaluates nine automatic metrics against the human judgments: three n-gram metrics (BLEU, ROUGE-1/2/L, METEOR), BERTScore (precision/recall/F1), a simple OpenIE-triple-matching baseline, two entailment-classification metrics (FactCC, DAE), and two question-generation-and-answering metrics (FEQA, QAGS). Rather than a single pooled Pearson correlation between metric score and human judgment, the paper uses partial correlation: it regresses out a confounding categorical variable — which (system, dataset) pair a summary came from — from both the metric scores and the human scores, then correlates the residuals. Figure 3 is the paper's own justification for this: fitting one line across the whole dataset gives FEQA and FactCC a much steeper (apparently more correlated) slope than fitting a separate line within each system-dataset subset — for FEQA those per-partition lines are nearly flat, though FactCC's own per-partition fits still show visible slope (the paper's point is that the pooled line is steeper than any of them, not that the per-partition lines are all flat). Pooling conflates "this metric tracks factuality" with "this metric tracks which system produced the summary" — partialling out the system/dataset identity removes that confound.

// what they found

The results

Which systems are more factual. Pooling both datasets, 60.0% of the 2,250 annotated summaries contain at least one factual error. The two datasets look very different: on CNN/DM, the best models (BERTSum, BART) are wrong on 23–27% of summaries, while the weaker LSTM models (S2S, Bottom-Up) are wrong 62.6–74.8% of the time. XSum is harder for everything — its best model, BERTS2S, is still wrong 83.7% of the time, and the non-pretrained XSum models (PGN, TConvS2S, TranS2S) are wrong 89.8–96.9% of the time. The error mix differs by dataset too: CNN/DM's dominant categories are EntE (entity errors) and CorefE (coreference), while XSum's are OutE (out-of-article/hallucinated content) and EntE. The paper attributes this to XSum's single-sentence summaries precluding cross-sentence discourse errors (CorefE, LinkE) — Table 3 backs that up closely, with CorefE and LinkE each at just 0–1% across all four XSum models, essentially but not literally absent.

Which metrics best track human judgment. Table 2 reports partial Pearson (ρ) and Spearman (r) correlation between each metric's score and the human factuality judgment, pooled and per-dataset. The two best-correlating metrics overall are FactCC (ρ = 0.20, r = 0.30) and BERTScore Precision (ρ = 0.27, r = 0.24) — both entailment- or embedding-based, and both clearing statistical significance. General n-gram metrics land surprisingly close behind: Rouge-1 and METEOR both reach ρ = 0.14, not far under FactCC's 0.20. The two question-generation-and-answering metrics fare worse — QAGS is weakly but significantly correlated (ρ = 0.06), and FEQA's correlation is not statistically significant at all (ρ = 0.00, p = 0.83).

Where metrics break down. Splitting the partial Pearson correlation by dataset (Figure 4), entailment metrics and QAGS correlate far better on CNN/DM than on XSum — the paper describes the drop as "a factor of four" — which it attributes to XSum's unusually high rate of out-of-article (OutE) errors, a category none of these metrics were designed to catch well.

An ablation that removes each broad error category in turn and re-measures partial correlation (Figure 5) shows each metric has a distinct fingerprint: Rouge-L's correlation comes almost entirely from content-verifiability errors (it's an n-gram overlap metric, so it can spot missing/added spans but not swapped relations); OpenIE is comparatively better at semantic-frame errors; and DAE has the highest correlation with discourse errors of any metric tested — a small positive bar that several other metrics (BERTScore P, Rouge-L, OpenIE) also show at a much smaller scale, while FactCC is "nearly uncorrelated" with that category, which the paper attributes to its synthetic training perturbations never including cross-sentence errors. BERTScore Precision's own bar is tallest of all on content-verifiability errors and comparatively small on semantic-frame errors — the paper attributes its overall 0.27 correlation mostly to catching content that can't be verified against the source at all, not to precisely judging whether a predicate or entity is right.

Finally, a confusion analysis (Figure 6) — built from sentences where two of three annotators agreed on a category and the third picked a different one — finds most categories are frequently confused with NE ("not an error"), which the authors attribute to annotator noise rather than a flaw in the typology. Three categories break that pattern: PredE and CircE are instead most often confused with OutE (0.32 and 0.18 respectively, against 0.11 and 0.15 for NE), and LinkE peaks at EntE (0.34) — a separate trend the paper attributes to annotators conflating "wrong relation" (PredE/EntE) with "unverifiable against the article" (OutE) judgments. The standout: when CorefE is the majority label, the disagreeing annotator picks NE 69.7% of the time — far above any other category's confusion rate — which the paper explains as a specific annotator blind spot: having just read the source article, an annotator tends to unconsciously supply an antecedent for a pronoun even when the summary never actually names it.

// does it hold up?

The eval read

FRANK is a meta-evaluation benchmark, so its entire foundation is the human annotation, and that foundation is more solid than most: three independent annotators per sentence across 4,942 sentences, an enforced qualification bar, continuous artificial-error checks, and — the headline number — κ = 0.86 crowd/expert agreement, well above the κ = 0.65–0.74 the field's prior best (Falke et al., 2019) had managed. Worth keeping in view, though: that 0.86 figure comes from a 201-datapoint validation slice (20 summaries) judged against a single expert annotator, not the full 2,250-summary corpus, and not multiple experts — it's a spot-check on annotation quality, not itself a large-sample estimate of expert agreement. The two more central agreement numbers, computed on the full dataset, are far more modest: κ = 0.58 for the binary judgment and κ = 0.39 for the 8-category split — "moderate" agreement in the paper's own words, not "near perfect."

The choice to report only partial correlations (controlling for system and dataset identity) is well-motivated by Figure 3's visual case for confounding, but the paper never reports the corresponding unpartialled Pearson correlations alongside them, so a reader can't see how much the correction actually moves any metric's ranking, or whether any pairwise ordering (e.g., FactCC vs. BERTScore Precision) would flip under the naive pooled statistic. Table 4's own significance test (the Hotelling-Williams comparisons) is likewise computed only on the partial correlations, compounding rather than checking that choice.

As flagged above, the officially indexed NAACL proceedings PDF (the ACL Anthology / DOI copy) still carries a since-corrected bug in every BERTScore result — worth restating in full here since most citations resolve to that DOI, not to arXiv. ArXiv's own comment on this paper's v2 upload reads "Second version fixes bug with BERTScore results," and diffing the Anthology PDF against arXiv v2 confirms it: in the Anthology copy, BERTScore Precision's partial correlation with human judgment is a statistically insignificant −0.02 (p = 0.35), and the Anthology's own prose names FactCC as the sole "best metric overall," never mentioning BERTScore as competitive. This page follows the authors' corrected arXiv v2 throughout, as they themselves endorse post-acceptance. A second, smaller prose/table mismatch survives even in v2: §5.3 states BERTScore Precision reaches "0.27 Pearson and 0.35 Spearman correlation" — but Table 2's Spearman-all-data cell for BERTScore Precision is 0.24, not 0.35; 0.35 is actually that same row's Pearson-CNN/DM cell, two columns over. A third, independent paper-internal inconsistency sits in Figure 4 itself: its own printed caption claims entailment metrics "have highest correlation on pretrained models in the CNN/DM dataset," which is the opposite of both its own bars (FactCC 0.44→0.16, DAE 0.27→0.08, QAGS 0.16→0.035 moving non-pretrained→pretrained) and of §5.3's prose two paragraphs below it — this page's Figure 4 caption follows the bars and the prose, not the paper's own caption.

Substantively, the paper's own error-type analysis undercuts using its "best" metrics as a general-purpose factuality proxy: FactCC and DAE are trained on synthetic, rule-inserted errors, and §5.3 finds both correlate significantly better with human judgment on non-pretrained CNN/DM models than on pretrained ones — i.e., these metrics' artificial training errors resemble what a weak LSTM gets wrong more than what a fine-tuned BART gets wrong. That's a specific, checkable limitation on generalizing FactCC/DAE scores to stronger models, and the paper states it plainly rather than glossing over it. Two further, more concrete caveats sit underneath the same two "best" metrics and aren't discussed as such in the paper: FactCC is trained on CNN/DM itself (§6: "FactCC is trained on the CNN/DM dataset augmented with four types of artificial mistakes as supervision"), so its ρ = 0.36 on CNN/DM against just 0.07 on XSum (Table 2) is at least as consistent with training-domain overlap as with the paper's own out-of-article-errors explanation for the same gap. And DAE (§A.2) "was trained with a max length of 128 after concatenating both article and summary," so its CNN/DM articles are truncated before scoring — its ρ = 0.25 on CNN/DM is a number measured on partial inputs, a caveat the paper discloses but doesn't factor into its "best metrics" framing. One more anomaly the paper never addresses: OpenIE's Spearman correlation on XSum is −0.45 (p = .00, Table 2) — the single largest-magnitude coefficient anywhere in the table, and strongly negative, meaning the simplest baseline is anti-correlated with human factuality judgments on XSum specifically.

Finally, every model in FRANK's pool — S2S, PGN, Bottom-Up, BERTSum, BART on CNN/DM; PGN, TConvS2S, TranS2S, BERTS2S on XSum — is a 2015–2020-era encoder-decoder summarizer (S2S dates to Rush et al. 2015, PGN to See et al. 2017). None resemble the instruction-tuned, RLHF-trained decoder-only models a 2026 reader would actually want a factuality metric for. The typology (what kinds of errors exist) has generalized well — both BEGIN and FActScore build on FRANK's core idea of meta-evaluating metrics against fine-grained human labels rather than a single benchmark leaderboard — but the specific correlation numbers in Table 2 describe this particular, dated error distribution, not a ceiling on any factuality metric's usefulness against today's summarizers.

baselines

Nine metrics spanning three families (n-gram, entailment, QGA) plus BERTScore, evaluated under one shared protocol for the first time — a genuine improvement over each metric's original, incomparable evaluation. No metric from the FActScore/atomic-fact era or LLM-as-judge era is included, since none existed yet.

contamination

Not applicable in the leakage sense (this predates LLM benchmark contamination concerns), but FactCC/DAE's reliance on synthetic training errors is a closely related generalization risk the paper itself surfaces via the pretrained-vs-non-pretrained-model split.

what's missed

No unpartialled correlation numbers alongside the partial ones; a κ = 0.86 crowd-expert headline drawn from only 201 datapoints against one expert; and a prose sentence (BERTScore's "0.35 Spearman") that doesn't match its own table.

would raise confidence

Reporting the raw pooled correlations next to the partial ones, a second expert rater on a larger validation slice, and — for future use — a version notice pointing readers away from the still-buggy Anthology PDF toward arXiv v2.

// bottom line

FRANK's typology and meta-evaluation protocol were the right idea — turning "factual or not" into a category and a shared benchmark is genuinely why BEGIN and FActScore could build fine-grained factuality work later — but the specific numbers are noisier and more version-dependent than a citation count of 400 might suggest: check you're reading arXiv v2, not the Anthology PDF, and treat the correlation table as a snapshot of one dated model pool, not a universal metric leaderboard.

// what to remember

What to remember

  1. FRANK's real contribution is procedural: a seven-category typology across three linguistic levels (semantic frame, discourse, content verifiability) that replaces the binary factual/non-factual label every prior metric (FactCC, FEQA, QAGS, DAE) was built and evaluated against — validated by κ = 0.86 crowd-expert agreement versus Falke et al.'s κ = 0.65–0.74.
  2. Benchmarked on the same 2,250-summary, partial-correlation protocol, FactCC (ρ = 0.20) and BERTScore Precision (ρ = 0.27, per the authors' bug-fixed arXiv v2 — the published NAACL proceedings PDF still has BERTScore at a statistically insignificant −0.02) are the two best-correlating automatic metrics with human judgment — but "best" means "least weak": every metric tested is a poor substitute for a human reading the summary.
  3. Metrics are non-uniform detectors, not a single scalar of "factuality skill": entailment metrics catch semantic-frame and content-verifiability errors well but (DAE aside) miss discourse errors, and the entailment/QGA metrics' correlation with humans drops by roughly a factor of four moving from CNN/DM to XSum — while the plain n-gram metrics (BLEU, ROUGE, METEOR) actually score slightly higher on XSum, since surface overlap isn't sensitive to which kind of error dominates a dataset.
  4. Cite carefully: the officially indexed NAACL proceedings PDF still has the pre-fix BERTScore bug the authors themselves flagged in arXiv v2's comment field, and the model pool (S2S through BART, 2015–2020) means the correlation numbers describe that generation's failure modes, not a guarantee about how these metrics behave on today's summarizers.
