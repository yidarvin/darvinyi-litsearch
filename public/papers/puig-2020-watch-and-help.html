Watch-And-Help: A Challenge for Social Perception and Human-AI Collaboration

An AI agent ("Bob") watches a single demonstration of a human-like agent ("Alice") performing a household task, has to infer her goal from that one demonstration, and is then dropped into a brand-new apartment where he must help her finish the same goal as fast as possible — with the whole loop also validated against real human collaborators.

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

Human children help each other with almost no prior coordination: watch someone reach for a goal once, guess what they were trying to do, then pitch in — even in a place you've never seen them act in before. Machine social intelligence had nothing that tested this specific combination. Multi-agent RL platforms of the era (StarCraft, Hanabi, Neural MMO, Pommerman, OpenAI Five) simulate rich strategic interaction but in toy or game-like worlds with no realistic household semantics. Human-robot-interaction work on collaborative planning (Overcooked-style coordination in Carroll et al., 2019) gets closer to the social-reasoning problem, but the shared goal is either handed to both agents up front or drawn from a small, enumerable space — the helper never has to infer a goal from watching a demonstration. Meanwhile the realistic embodied-AI environments of the day (AI2-THOR, Habitat, Gibson, VirtualHome, ALFRED) test navigation, embodied QA, or single-agent task completion; most of them are single-agent outright, and even AI2-THOR's multi-agent support ships with no humanoid avatar and no built-in human-like agent — none of them offer a second agent that emulates believable human behavior or lets a real human step into the loop.

Watch-And-Help (WAH) targets the gap directly: an agent needs social perception — recognizing another agent's goal, which may be one of many plausible combinations of predicates, from a single video-length demonstration — and generalizable collaborative planning — carrying that inferred goal into a different, unseen apartment and acting alongside the demonstrator (who is now also acting, and reacting) to finish faster than she would alone. No existing benchmark packaged the two together, evaluated them with real humans as well as a synthetic stand-in, or did it inside a house realistic enough that the underlying activities (setting a dinner table, washing dishes) look like actual chores rather than an abstract grid-world token game.

// how it works

The method

The authors first build VirtualHome-Social, an extension of the single-agent VirtualHome simulator (Puig et al., 2018) that supports concurrent multi-agent actions, symbolic scene-graph observations (nodes = objects with class/state/coordinates, edges = spatial relations) as well as multi-modal visual observations (RGB, depth, instance/semantic segmentation, 3D boxes, skeletons), a pool of humanoid avatars, and — critically — an interface that lets a real human take over an avatar. It ships 7 furnished apartments; the last two are held out exclusively as helping environments in the test set, so a Bob agent is always evaluated in a home it never trained in. On average an agent's action space includes 167 distinct actions per step, since interacting with an object requires specifying both an action type and the visible object's instance id.

Alice, the demonstrator, is not scripted — she is driven by a hierarchical planning-based "human-like agent" with bounded rationality: a belief module tracks a distribution over each object's possible location (updated only where new observations contradict the current belief), Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) searches over a sequence of subgoal predicates given the sampled belief state, and a regression planner (RP) works backward from each subgoal to a concrete action sequence. Alice replans at every step from her latest partial observation (about 0.05s per replan), and a heuristic makes her close containers once she's done with them, which a follow-up rating study found indistinguishable from real human behavior (Likert similarity ratings of 3.38 ± 0.93 for the built-in agent vs. 3.72 ± 0.92 for real humans, t(29) = −1.35, p = .19).

A task is defined by a goal g: a set of 2–8 predicates with counts (e.g. "ON(plate, dinnertable):2; ON(wineglass, dinnertable):1"), drawn from one of five household-activity predicate sets — setting up a dinner table, putting groceries in the fridge, preparing a simple meal, washing dishes, and reading a book with snacks — spanning 30 distinct predicate types in total. Training uses 1,011 tasks; two 100-task test sets are held out (test-1, single-activity goals; test-2, goals mixing predicates from two different activity sets to probe compositional generalization), plus a separate 5,303-demonstration set used only to train goal inference. An episode ends in success once all of Alice's goal predicates are satisfied, or fails at a 250-step cap.

The baseline goal-inference model encodes each demonstration state with a Transformer over visible objects, feeds the sequence into an LSTM, average-pools over time, and runs 30 independent classifiers (one per predicate type) to predict each predicate's count. For the Help stage, all baselines share a hierarchical high-level/low-level architecture — a high-level module picks the next subgoal predicate, a low-level module turns it into Bob's next primitive action — instantiated three ways: HP (MCTS + RP planner at both levels, the same planner class as Alice's, but with its own belief and, when given the true goal, aware of Alice's plan so it avoids redundant subgoals), Hybrid (an RL-trained high-level policy paired with the RP planner for low-level execution), and HRL (both levels trained with RL, off-policy A2C/RMSprop). A Random baseline takes random actions, and two oracles upper-bound performance: OracleB (Bob has full environment knowledge and the true goal) and OracleA,B (Alice does too).

// what they found

The results

On the Watch stage (test-1), the full-demonstration goal-inference model reaches 0.85 precision / 0.96 recall on predicate recognition, against an 0.08 / 0.09 chance baseline. Ablating to just the last observed state (dropping the temporal sequence) drops this to 0.79 / 0.75; conversely, feeding Alice's actions directly (rather than raw states) pushes it up to 0.99 / 0.99 — the demonstration's action sequence, not just the final state, is where most of the inferable goal signal lives.

For the Help stage (test-1), planning dominates: HPTG (true goal given) is the best non-oracle baseline, since it explicitly reasons about Alice's planned subgoals to avoid redundant work and collisions. Using the model's own inferred goal instead of the true one, both HP and Hybrid still help effectively — but feeding a deliberately random goal (HPRG) makes a capable planner actively counterproductive, since it starts undoing progress Alice has already made toward her real (different) goal; the paper reports such goal conflicts arising in 40% of episodes overall, and 65% for the Put Groceries and Set Meal categories specifically. HRL performs no better than Random despite sharing its global policy structure with Hybrid — the high-level policy picks sensible subgoals, but the low-level policy typically grabs the right object and then fails to deliver it to the target location. There is no significant difference between the Random and Alice-alone baselines (t(99) = −1.38, p = .17).

Generalizing to test-2's multi-activity goals is harder: goal-inference precision/recall falls to 0.68 / 0.64 (from 0.85 / 0.96 on test-1), which the authors attribute to overfitting on the predicate combinations seen in training rather than compositional generalization. Downstream, Alice-alone success rate is 95.40 ± 0.01, while HP achieves 88.60 ± 0.02 success and only a 0.21 ± 0.04 speedup — a substantial degradation versus test-1, traced directly to the weaker Watch-stage goal recognition.

Two human studies close the loop. Experiment 1 (6 subjects, 30 tasks, controlling Alice alone) found real humans slightly faster than the built-in human-like agent but not significantly so (t(29) = −1.63, p = .11). Experiment 2 (12 subjects, 90 collaboration trials across the same 30 tasks, each paired with HP, Hybrid, or HPRG) reproduced the same agent ranking with real humans as with the built-in Alice, and subjective 1–7 ratings (perceived goal knowledge, helpfulness, trust) tracked the objective scores. The one notable divergence: paired t-tests on cumulative reward showed HPRG scored significantly higher when paired with real humans than with the built-in agent (t(29) = −2.36, p = .03), driven by a higher success rate — real people recognized a conflicting AI goal and worked around it by finishing their other subgoals first, something the built-in Alice could not do; HP and Hybrid showed no such gap in cumulative reward (t(29) = −1.78, p = .1 and t(29) = −0.5, p = .62, respectively).

// does it hold up?

The eval read

WAH is not a contaminated-corpus benchmark in the LLM sense — it's a simulator, so leakage isn't really the risk. The risk here is narrower: how much of the reported gap between "good" and "bad" collaborators is a property of social reasoning versus a property of relatively weak baselines and a small, fixed world.

baselines

HP, Hybrid, and HRL are the only learned/planned baselines, and HRL — trained with off-policy A2C over a hierarchical policy — performs no better than a random-action agent (Figure 5 shows the two essentially tied, with overlapping error bars). The paper attributes this to the low-level policy failing to deliver grabbed objects to their targets, which is a specific, fixable RL-training failure rather than evidence that RL "can't" solve the task. Because none of the non-planning baselines manages to out-help a random agent, the paper's central claim ("planning-based approaches are the most effective") is close to true by default rather than by a fair fight between comparably-tuned methods.

contamination

Not corpus contamination, but a related generalization gap: only 7 apartments exist total, with just 2 held out for testing the Help stage, and every demonstration Bob ever watches (train or test) is generated by the same bounded-rational MCTS+RP planner playing Alice. The behavioral-realism check (3.38 vs. 3.72 Likert, n=8 raters × 15 videos) validates that this synthetic Alice's trajectories look human-like, but the paper never tests whether a goal-inference model trained on these synthetic demonstrations still works when the Watch-stage input is an actual human-controlled demonstration rather than a stand-in — only the Help-stage collaboration is validated against real humans, not the Watch-stage perception step the paper names first.

what's missed

Every Bob baseline is given "ground-truth states of objects within its field of view" rather than raw pixels — the paper explicitly notes this as a design choice ("one could also use raw pixels as input") — so despite VirtualHome-Social's rich multi-modal rendering (RGB, depth, segmentation, skeletons), the reported numbers say nothing about visual social perception, only symbolic-state social perception. The headline success-rate/speedup/cumulative-reward metrics also don't directly penalize the disruptive behaviors the qualitative analysis (Figure 7) surfaces — an agent that blocks Alice in a doorway or induces a false belief in her only shows up in the score as extra wasted steps, not as a distinct "harmful collaborator" signal.

would raise confidence

A stronger, comparably-tuned RL baseline (or an ablation isolating why HRL's low-level policy fails to deliver objects) would make the planning-vs-learning comparison meaningful rather than a foregone conclusion. Testing goal inference directly on real human demonstration videos — not just Help-stage collaboration with real humans — would validate the "social perception" half of the challenge, not just the "collaboration" half. And given the human studies rest on n=30 paired comparisons (several "no significant difference" conclusions with p in the .1–.2 range, e.g. t(29) = −1.78, p = .1 for HP), reporting effect sizes/confidence intervals alongside the t-tests would make clear how much of the "consistent with real humans" story is a genuinely tight replication versus an underpowered test.

// bottom line

The two-stage watch-then-help framing and the real-human validation loop are the paper's real, durable contributions — later Theory-of-Mind and embodied-collaboration benchmarks (including MMToM-QA, which builds directly on this VirtualHome-Social pipeline) inherit both. But the specific numbers in Figures 5 and 8 compare a competent MCTS+RP planner against a set of baselines — Random, a clearly under-trained HRL, and two oracles — that don't triangulate tightly enough to say how much headroom is really left for a stronger collaborative agent to close.

// takeaways

What to remember

  1. Watch-And-Help splits machine social intelligence into two testable stages — infer a goal from a single demonstration, then help achieve it in an unseen environment as fast as possible — which is a sharper task than prior HRI/multi-agent work, where the goal was usually shared up front or drawn from a tiny space.
  2. VirtualHome-Social is the enabling contribution: the first realistic, multi-agent household simulator with humanoid avatars, a believable built-in "human-like" planner-driven agent, and — unusually for the time — a working interface for real humans to step into the loop and be measured against the synthetic stand-in.
  3. Swapping a model's own inferred goal for the true one (or for a random one) is where the benchmark bites hardest: goal-inference recall collapses from 0.96 (test-1) to 0.64 (test-2, multi-activity), and a wrong goal doesn't just fail to help — HPRG actively conflicts with Alice in 40% of episodes overall (65% for the Put Groceries and Set Meal categories), a "confidently wrong collaborator is worse than no collaborator" failure mode that recurs across later Theory-of-Mind and embodied-agent benchmarks.
