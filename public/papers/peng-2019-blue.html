Transfer Learning in Biomedical Natural Language Processing: An Evaluation of BERT and ELMo on Ten Benchmarking Datasets

Ten pre-existing biomedical and clinical shared-task corpora, repackaged behind one common evaluation harness modeled explicitly on GLUE — the template that LexGLUE, LEXTREME, and CMB would each later copy for law and Chinese medicine. The authors' own BERT-Base, continued pretraining on PubMed abstracts and MIMIC-III clinical notes, tops the aggregate score — but the paper's printed "Total" row doesn't reconcile with its own columns, its ELMo citation points to a different paper entirely, and one results row (ShARe/CLEFE) doesn't match its own appendix.

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

By mid-2019, the general-domain NLP world had a shared yardstick: the GLUE benchmark (Wang et al., 2018) had "been successfully promoting the development of language representations of general purpose," giving ELMo, GPT, and BERT a common leaderboard to be measured against. Biomedical NLP had no equivalent. Pre-trained representations for biomedical and clinical text — word embeddings, sentence embeddings, contextual models — existed, but "most of these works... were evaluated on either different datasets or the same dataset with slightly different sizes of examples," which the authors say "makes it challenging to fairly compare various language models."

The paper's fix is not to build new data, but to standardize evaluation over data that already existed: it assembles five task types across ten corpora — sentence similarity, named entity recognition, relation extraction, document multilabel classification, and natural language inference — all of them preexisting shared-task datasets, "because they have been widely used by the BioNLP community." The authors are explicit that two parallel efforts were doing something similar at the same time: Lee et al. (2019, BioBERT) evaluated on three task types (NER, relation extraction, QA), and Jin et al. (2019) added NLI to NER. BLUE's claimed differentiation is threefold: it spans both biomedical-literature and clinical-note genres in one suite (not just one), it adds a document-classification task beyond sentence/sentence-pair tasks, and it ships "a comprehensive suite of codes to reconstruct dataset from scratch without removing any instances."

// how it works

The method

BLUE is an aggregation, not a new annotation effort: every one of its ten corpora already existed as a shared task before this paper. What the paper contributes is a single train/dev/test packaging, a fixed metric per task, reference code for reproducing every split "from scratch," and — the actual novel empirical content — a head-to-head evaluation of BERT and ELMo baselines pretrained specifically for biomedical and clinical text.

Ten rows, but nine distinct source corpora: BC5CDR-disease and BC5CDR-chemical are two separate label sets — disease mentions and chemical mentions — scored as two NER tasks over the same 1,500 PubMed abstracts. Two of the ten corpora carry splits the BLUE authors built themselves, not the original release: BIOSSES, whose data "has no standard splits," so the authors "randomly select 80% for training and 20% for testing"; and HoC, where they "use 315 (~20%) abstracts for testing and the remaining abstracts for training." The rest state they reuse "the standard training and test set" from each corpus's own shared task — though MedNLI is the only one the paper explicitly says reuses an official dev set too ("the same training, development, and test sets in Romanov and Shivade"); DDI and i2b2 are instead described by file/document counts ("624 train files and 191 test files"; "170 documents for training and 256... testing, which is the subset of the original dataset") rather than called a standard split, even though Table 1 lists a dev count for every one of the ten rows.

Two pre-training corpora feed the paper's own BERT models: more than 4,000M words of PubMed abstracts and more than 500M words of MIMIC-III clinical notes. Starting from Devlin et al.'s public BERT checkpoint, the authors continue pre-training for 5M steps on PubMed and a further 0.2M steps on MIMIC-III, releasing four variants that cross two sizes with two pre-training-corpus mixes: Base(P), Base(P+M), Large(P), Large(P+M) — where P = PubMed-only and P+M = PubMed + MIMIC-III. Fine-tuning follows task-appropriate recipes: sentence pairs are packed into a single BERT input; NER is cast as BIO tagging; relation extraction replaces the two entity mentions with placeholder tags (e.g. @CHEMICAL$, @GENE$) and treats the sentence as a classification problem; multi-label document classification fine-tunes BERT to predict a label set per sentence, then pools sentence predictions to the document.

The second baseline is ELMo, "pre-trained on PubMed abstracts," whose token-level output states from both LSTM layers are concatenated per word; for sentence-level tasks the word vectors are averaged into a sentence embedding and fed to a dense layer, while NER uses a Bi-LSTM-CRF tagger over concatenated GloVe, character, and ELMo embeddings.

The results

All ten metrics in Table 3 run the same direction — higher is better, whether Pearson correlation, (micro-)F1, or accuracy — so bold in the table below (the paper's own font-weight convention, verified per cell against the PDF's font layer) marks the best score in each row without any sign flips to track. BERT-Base (P+M) — pretrained on both PubMed abstracts and MIMIC-III — is the row-max on 5 of the 10 rows (MedSTS, BIOSSES, ShARe/CLEFE, i2b2, MedNLI); BERT-Base (P) wins 2 (the two BC5CDR NER rows); BERT-Large (P) wins 3 (DDI, ChemProt, HoC); BERT-Large (P+M) wins 0. ELMo and the cited state-of-the-art baselines never top a row.

Four of the five row-max wins for BERT-Base(P+M) sit in the clinical domain (MedSTS, ShARe/CLEFE, i2b2, MedNLI) — the fifth, BIOSSES, is biomedical — which matches the paper's own reading: "BERT-Base (P+M) is significantly superior to other models" in the clinical domain specifically, since MIMIC-III continuation-pretraining directly targets that genre. The large models are the surprise: the paper reports that BERT-Base beats BERT-Large everywhere except relation extraction and document classification, and offers as a partial explanation that those two task types carry the longest average sentence lengths in Table 1 — i.e. Large's edge shows up specifically where sentences run longest, not where Base is said to handle anything better. It separately flags BERT-Large(P+M) as "worse than other models overall" and attributes that partly to MIMIC-III being "relatively smaller" than the PubMed corpus, insufficient to pre-train the large model — yet BERT-Large(P) alone is still the single best performer on the multilabel HoC task.

ELMo trails BERT everywhere in aggregate, but it isn't uniformly the worst model on every row: as printed, it beats the cited SOTA* baseline on 3 of the 10 rows — ShARe/CLEFE (75.6 vs. 70.0, SOTA* cited to Leaman et al. 2015), DDI (78.9 vs. 72.9, cited to Zhang et al. 2018), and ChemProt (66.6 vs. 64.1, cited to Peng et al. 2018) — all clinical- or biomedical-NER/relation-extraction tasks, two of them (DDI, ChemProt) against a SOTA* citation from 2018, the year before this paper. The ShARe/CLEFE margin doesn't hold up, though — see the table-inconsistency callout below: against the appendix's own F1 for Leaman et al. (75.3), ELMo's real margin there is 0.3, not 5.6.

// table anomaly — verified, not a transcription error

The printed Total row above is reproduced exactly as it appears in the PDF: six numbers, sitting under the ELMo, BioBERT, Base(P), Base(P+M), Large(P), and Large(P+M) columns, with nothing at all printed under SOTA*. Re-deriving each column's own macro-average from its ten printed cells (a plain mean, per §3.6's stated method) shows the six Total values reconcile almost exactly with the BioBERT (80.46≈80.5), Base(P) (82.18≈82.2), Base(P+M) (82.34≈82.3), Large(P) (81.46≈81.5), and Large(P+M) (79.20=79.2) columns — but the value sitting under ELMo's header, 78.8, is nowhere near ELMo's own re-derived average of 74.79 (a 4.0-point gap, far outside 1-decimal rounding noise). It isn't a clean match for the SOTA* column's own average either — 78.15 is 0.65 away from 78.8, outside the roughly ±0.05 rounding envelope a ten-cell mean should produce — so a plain one-column shift doesn't fully explain the printed value. No column average matches 78.8 exactly; the nearest is Large (P+M)'s 79.20, 0.40 away, which is not the neighbouring column either. Every other total in the row checks out to within 0.04.

// table inconsistency — ShARe/CLEFE and i2b2, Table 3 vs. Appendix A

Table 3 labels every ShARe/CLEFE and i2b2 column "F" (F1), but the printed cells don't all match the paper's own Appendix A, which gives full P/R/F1 for both rows. For ShARe/CLEFE, Appendix A.1 reports BioBERT 79.2/72.8/75.9, Base(P) 80.0/75.4/77.6, Base(P+M) 81.3/77.1/79.2, Large(P) 73.0/72.7/72.8, and Large(P+M) 73.2/74.4/73.8 (P/R/F1) — but Table 3's ShARe/CLEFE row prints 72.8, 75.4, 77.1, 72.7, and 74.4 for those five models, each one matching the appendix's Recall column, not its F1. Table 3's ELMo cell (75.6) is the exception that does match the appendix's F1 (P/R/F1 = 77.9/73.4/75.6). Table 3's SOTA* cell for the row, 70.0, matches none of Leaman et al.'s appendix-reported P/R/F1 (79.7/71.3/75.3). The same pattern appears once more in i2b2: Table 3's ELMo cell (71.2) is the appendix's precision (P/R/F1 = 71.2/71.1/71.1), not its F1 (71.1); every other i2b2 cell in Table 3 — SOTA*, BioBERT, and all four "Our BERT" columns — does match the appendix's F1 exactly. The appendix's own P/R/F1/TP/FP/FN figures are internally consistent with each other in both rows; Table 3 is the one that doesn't reconcile with them.

// does it hold up?

The eval read

BLUE's actual contribution is narrower than "ten new biomedical benchmarks" — it is a standardized packaging of ten pre-existing shared tasks behind one split, one metric-per-task, and reference code, plus one new empirical result (domain-continued BERT beats ELMo and old task-specific SOTA on this suite). That packaging work is genuinely useful and is why the field adopted the GLUE-style aggregate-score idea for biomedicine — but several things in the paper itself are worth checking before trusting a number lifted from it.

A bibliographic error attaches to the one baseline the title names. The in-text ELMo citations — in the introduction ("one makes use of ELMo (Peters et al., 2017)") and in §4.2 ("We adopted the ELMo model pre-trained on PubMed abstracts (Peters et al., 2017)") — both cite (Peters et al., 2017). The paper's own bibliography resolves that citation to: "Matthew E. Peters, Waleed Ammar, Chandra Bhagavatula, and Russell Power. 2017. Semi-supervised sequence tagging with bidirectional language models. In Proceedings of ACL, pages 1756–1765" — that is TagLM, a real but different model. The actual ELMo paper is Peters, Neumann, Iyyer, Gardner, Clark, Lee & Zettlemoyer, "Deep Contextualized Word Representations," NAACL 2018 — which does not appear anywhere in BLUE's 42-entry reference list. This doesn't cast doubt on what was actually run (the method description and the AllenNLP ELMo link in §4.2 make clear the baseline is genuine ELMo, not TagLM), but the citation itself is simply wrong throughout, on the one baseline the title names.

The Total row doesn't reconcile with its own columns (see the callout above the fold in "what they found"): the value sitting in ELMo's column position (78.8) is 4.0 points from ELMo's own re-derived average (74.79), while every other column's total matches to within 0.04. Anyone quoting "ELMo's BLUE score is 78.8" is quoting a number that does not correspond to the ten ELMo cells printed two lines above it.

Table 3 doesn't always print what its own header says. Five of the seven "F1" cells in the ShARe/CLEFE row (BioBERT, Base(P), Base(P+M), Large(P), Large(P+M)) are actually the appendix's Recall values, not F1, and the row's SOTA* cell matches none of Leaman et al.'s appendix-reported P/R/F1; i2b2's ELMo cell is likewise the appendix's precision rather than its F1. See the table-inconsistency callout in "what they found" for the full comparison — the appendix's own P/R/F1/TP/FP/FN figures are internally consistent, so Table 3 is the one that doesn't reconcile with them.

No spread is ever reported, even though the BERT results are a best-of-several selection. The paper flags exactly where this would matter — BIOSSES has only 20 test examples, and §5 notes "all BERT models' performance was unstable" (that same sentence miscounts the split, calling it "only 16 sentence pairs in the test set" when Table 1 gives 16 as the dev size and 20 as the test size) — and says its own BERT results were obtained "by following the same strategy: selecting the best model on the development set after several runs." So every BERT cell in Table 3 is a best-of-N pick, not a single run (the paper doesn't say whether ELMo or the cited SOTA* numbers follow the same practice) — which, if anything, is more likely to overstate a model's true expected performance the higher its run-to-run variance is, exactly the situation the paper flags for BIOSSES. Neither the run count, nor a standard deviation, nor a confidence interval appears anywhere in the paper.

The aggregate blends metrics and test-set sizes without weighting. The Total score is an unweighted mean over ten rows that mix Pearson correlation (MedSTS, BIOSSES), F1/micro-F1 (seven rows), and accuracy (MedNLI) — and treats BIOSSES's 20-example test set as equally influential on the aggregate as i2b2's 6,293 relations or MedNLI's 1,422 pairs. That is the same aggregation choice GLUE made (the paper says so explicitly, citing Wang et al. 2018a and Lee et al. 2019 for the practice), so it's a shared, not unique, limitation — but it means a single small, high-variance corpus can move the headline number by more than its sample size would justify.

The "SOTA*" column is not a controlled baseline. It's ten numbers, one per row, lifted from eight different papers (2011–2019) — two of them (Chen et al. 2019 for MedSTS/BIOSSES; Yoon et al. 2018 for both BC5CDR rows) supply two rows each — each with its own feature engineering, tokenization, and — per the footnote — current only "as of April 2019." BLUE never re-runs any of them under its own harness, so a comparison like "ELMo beats SOTA* on DDI" is really "ELMo beats whatever Zhang et al. (2018) reported for DDI," not a head-to-head run under identical conditions.

baselines

BERT-Base/Large (both continued-pretrained on PubMed and PubMed+MIMIC-III) and ELMo (PubMed-pretrained) are reasonable, contemporaneous 2019 choices; the "SOTA*" column is eight uncontrolled, non-reproduced citations from 2011–2019, not a run-under-one-harness baseline.

contamination

Mostly outside the paper's frame — these are 2019 supervised fine-tuning baselines on their own official test splits, not pretraining-corpus leakage in the modern LLM sense. One overlap is checkable in the paper's own text, though, and goes unmentioned: MedNLI is “a collection of sentence pairs selected from MIMIC-III” (§3.5), and the winning model on that row — BERT-Base (P+M), at 84.0 against Base (P)'s 82.2 — is the one continue-pretrained on MIMIC-III clinical notes (>500M words, Table 2). That is unlabeled source-text overlap rather than label leakage, and the margin is small, but the paper never raises it. The comparison does not extend to ShARe/CLEFE, which the paper sources to MIMIC-II, a different release.

what's missed

Any run-count/seed/variance reporting despite the BERT results being a best-of-several dev-set pick and despite explicitly noting BIOSSES instability; a reconciled Total row (the ELMo aggregate as printed does not match ELMo's own ten cells); two rows (ShARe/CLEFE, i2b2) where Table 3 prints recall or precision under an "F1" header instead of the appendix's actual F1; a controlled re-run of the SOTA* baselines; and the ELMo citation itself, which points to TagLM (Peters et al. 2017) rather than the actual ELMo paper (Peters et al. 2018) throughout.

would raise confidence

Reported run counts and variance, especially for the 20-example BIOSSES test set; a corrected Total row, ELMo citation, and ShARe/CLEFE/i2b2 cells matching the appendix's own F1 values; and a same-harness re-run of the cited SOTA* systems rather than static literature numbers.

// bottom line

BLUE did what it set out to do — give biomedical NLP a GLUE-shaped common yardstick — and its central finding (domain-continued BERT beats general-domain ELMo and old task-specific baselines) is well supported by most of Table 3's individual rows. But the paper is less careful with itself than with its benchmark: no BERT run reports a spread despite being a best-of-several dev-set pick, the printed Total row's ELMo cell doesn't reconcile with ELMo's own column, its ELMo citations point at the wrong paper (TagLM, not ELMo), and — the largest of the three — Table 3 prints recall instead of F1 for five of seven ShARe/CLEFE models and precision instead of F1 for i2b2's ELMo cell, both contradicted by the paper's own, internally consistent appendix. None of that changes the paper's broad conclusion, but it does mean no single number in Table 3 should be repeated without checking it against Appendix A first.

// takeaways

What to remember

  1. The GLUE-for-biomedicine template. Five task types, ten dataset rows (nine source corpora — BC5CDR contributes two label sets), all pre-existing shared tasks repackaged behind one split and one metric per task, with a macro-averaged Total score modeled directly on GLUE's aggregation. The same recipe was later copied for law (LexGLUE, LEXTREME) and Chinese medicine (CMB).
  2. Domain-continued pretraining wins, unevenly. BERT-Base (P+M) — continued-pretrained on PubMed abstracts and MIMIC-III clinical notes — is the row-max on 5 of 10 rows, mostly clinical-domain tasks; BERT-Large wins only on relation extraction and document classification; ELMo trails BERT everywhere in aggregate but still beats the cited (uncontrolled) SOTA* numbers on 3 of 10 rows, though its ShARe/CLEFE margin shrinks from 5.6 to 0.3 once checked against the appendix's own F1. Every BERT number is a best-of-several dev-set pick, and none of it comes with a reported spread — including on BIOSSES, whose 20-example test set the authors themselves call unstable.
  3. Verify before you quote it. The Total row doesn't reconcile as ELMo's own average (78.8 printed vs. 74.79 re-derived from its ten cells); every ELMo citation in the paper resolves in the bibliography to TagLM (Peters et al., 2017), not the actual ELMo paper (Peters et al., 2018); and Table 3's ShARe/CLEFE row prints recall, not F1, for five of seven models, with its i2b2 ELMo cell printing precision. All three are checkable against material the paper itself provides — none is a matter of opinion.
