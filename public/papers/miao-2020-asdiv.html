A Diverse Corpus for Evaluating and Developing English Math Word Problem Solvers

Academia Sinica introduces ASDiv (Academia Sinica Diverse MWP Dataset) — 2,305 English math word problems spanning 24 problem types and six elementary grade levels — alongside a new bidirectional-BLEU lexicon usage diversity metric. The pitch: existing corpora reward pattern-matching rather than understanding, and once you control for that, accuracy craters — the neural GTS solver goes from 86% on the low-diversity MathQA-C to 36% on ASDiv, and all three solvers tested land in the mid-30s% there.

What problem is this solving?

By 2020 there were plenty of English math-word-problem (MWP) corpora, but each was compromised in a different way. The paper groups them into four families: Number Word Problem corpora like Dolphin1878 (Shi et al., 2015); Arithmetic Word Problem corpora like AI2 (395 problems) and IL (562); Algebraic Word Problem corpora like KAZB, ALGES and DRAW (500–1,000 problems each); and large Mixed-type corpora — Dolphin18K (18K), AQuA (100K), MathQA (37K) — that trade quality for scale. The authors' claim, backed by a citation to Huang et al. (2016), is that low-diversity corpora yield over-optimistic results: a solver can often just retrieve the equation template of the most similar training problem instead of understanding the text, and Roy and Roth (2017) had already shown accuracy drops sharply once near-duplicate MWPs are stripped out. So dataset diversity, not dataset size, is the paper's central claim about what actually judges an MWP solver's real capability.

// a typical MWP (paper's Table 1)

"A sandwich is priced at $0.75. A cup of pudding is priced at $0.25. Tim bought 2 sandwiches and 4 cups of pudding. How much money should Tim pay?" — Solution: 0.75 × 2 + 0.25 × 4 = 2.5

The paper makes the diversity problem concrete by auditing MathQA, the second-largest corpus in their comparison (37K problems) and, per Amini et al. (2019), cleaner than AQuA, the largest of the mixed-type corpora. Its corpus lexicon diversity (CLD, defined below) is only 0.05, with 85% of its MWPs scoring a lexicon-diversity of exactly 0 against some other MWP in the set — meaning the vast majority of MathQA problems are near-verbatim restatements of one another. Figure 3 (Appendix) shows one such cluster: 105 MathQA problems that, after sentence normalization, all share the sentence pattern "a train running at the speed of [NUM1] km/hr crosses a pole in [NUM2] sec. [what is | find] the length of the train?" — differing essentially only in the two quantities (and a what-is/find variant). The authors also ran a consistency check on MathQA's arithmetic subset — resolving each problem's annotated formula with SymPy and comparing to the labeled answer — and found 27% of that subset's annotated formulas didn't match their own labeled answers; a manual audit of 30 such inconsistent problems attributed 67% to an incorrect formula, 23% to a problematic/incomplete problem description, and 10% to a valueless (wrong or nonsensical) answer.

// how it works

The method

ASDiv is built on three deliverables: a corpus, a taxonomy, and a metric.

Corpus construction

The authors collected MWPs from 28 websites, pruning or rewriting a candidate problem whenever it was highly similar to one already kept (judged by the diversity metric described below), which yielded 2,305 final MWPs. A single hired master's-degree research assistant with a background in automatic MWP solving then manually annotated problem type, equation, answer, and grade level for each problem — reusing the source site's own fields directly where present (22.6% of source MWPs already had equations and answers; 52% had answers only; 63.5% already carried grade-level information) and annotating the rest from scratch. Quality control was two automatic/sampled passes rather than a multi-annotator agreement study: the paper argues that because a correct MWP answer is objective rather than a matter of interpretation, "human carefulness, instead of human agreement, is a more critical issue" here. Concretely, a program automatically checked that each annotated equation's evaluated result matched the labeled answer (re-annotating and re-checking any mismatch), and the authors then randomly spot-checked 480 samples (20 per problem type) for overall annotation correctness — all 480 were found correct.

A 24-way problem-type taxonomy

Every MWP is labeled with one of 24 problem types (PTs), grouped into three categories. Basic arithmetic operations covers Addition, Subtraction, Difference, Multiplication, three Division variants (common/floor/ceil), Sum, Surplus, Number-Operation, three Time-Variant-Quantity (TVQ) types (Initial/Change/Final — tracking an entity whose value updates across a sequence of events), and Multi-step. Aggregative operations covers Comparison, Set-Operation, Ratio, Number-Pattern, and two Algebra types (Algebra-1 and Algebra-2, with one and two unknown variables respectively). Additional domain knowledge required covers Greatest Common Divisor, Least Common Multiple, Geometry, and UnitTrans (unit conversion). Each MWP is also tagged with a grade level (G1–G6), annotated against the Achieve Inc. (2008) and Common Core State Standards (2010) references, to indicate difficulty.

The lexicon usage diversity metric

To quantify diversity directly rather than relying on corpus size as a proxy, the authors define a per-problem lexicon usage diversity (LD) score. Text is first tokenized and POS-tagged with Stanford CoreNLP, lemmatized with NLTK, then normalized by removing stopwords and replacing person names and quantity values with meta-symbols (so two MWPs that differ only in names or numbers count as identical). LD is then a bidirectional BLEU (n-grams up to 4) distance to the single most similar other problem of the same problem type:

LDi = 1 − maxj≠i BLEU(Pi,Pj) + BLEU(Pj,Pi)2 // P_i, P_j range over MWPs sharing a problem type; LD is 0–1, higher = more diverse. The corpus lexicon diversity (CLD) is the mean of LD_i over the corpus.

For corpora that carry no problem-type annotation at all (every baseline corpus in Table 2 except ASDiv itself), CLD is instead computed over the whole corpus rather than within same-type groups — a basis difference the paper states in passing (Section 3.4) but doesn't flag in the diversity figures themselves. The same bidirectional-BLEU construction, applied to POS-tag sequences instead of lemmatized words, gives a parallel syntactic pattern diversity (SD) metric used later as a secondary check.

// what they found

The results

On the whole-corpus diversity metrics, ASDiv comes out ahead of the other corpora the paper measures it against. Its mean corpus lexicon diversity (CLD) is 0.49 (std 0.12), against MathQA's 0.05, AI2's 0.30, IL's 0.27, KAZB's 0.39, ALGES's 0.35, DRAW's 0.35, and AllArith's 0.40. The share of MWPs with LD exactly 0 (i.e., near-duplicated) is 0% for ASDiv, versus the four corpora the paper reports this statistic for — 85% (MathQA), 28% (IL), 22% (AI2), and 20% (ALGES). The syntactic-diversity metric (SD) tells the same story: ASDiv's mean SD (0.21) is again the highest of the eight corpora measured, and its share of SD-exactly-0 problems (4%) is far below MathQA (87%), IL (54%), and AI2 (46%). One comparison basis breaks the pattern, though: measured between each corpus's own test-set and training-set (Figures 5–6, Appendix), MathQA actually has the highest diversity of any corpus — 0.85 LD and 0.66 SD, against ASDiv-A's 0.52 LD and 0.22 SD — which the paper itself flags as "surprisingly higher than that of all other corpora."

The paper then asks whether that diversity actually matters for measuring solver capability. Three solvers were evaluated: two rule/statistics-based systems, LCA++ (Roy and Roth, 2015) and UnitDep (Roy and Roth, 2017), and one neural system, GTS (Xie and Sun, 2019, a goal-driven tree-structured model). Because all three solve only arithmetic MWPs, the authors built two arithmetic-only evaluation sets: MathQA-C (3,000 problems, filtered down from 4,117 candidate MathQA problems to those with only arithmetic operations, no external constants, and no reused operands; CLD 0.08) and ASDiv-A (1,218 arithmetic-only problems drawn from ASDiv; CLD 0.50). They then also evaluated all three solvers on the full ASDiv corpus (2,305 problems across all 24 PTs; CLD 0.49), using 5-fold cross-validation throughout (GTS's numbers are averaged over 5 repeated runs).

// does it hold up?

The eval read

The diversity claim is well-supported on the corpus-level measures: across whole-corpus mean LD/CLD, mean SD, and — on the test-vs-training-set basis — the %-of-problems-with-diversity-≥0.5, ASDiv (or ASDiv-A, in the arithmetic-only comparisons) comes out ahead of every baseline corpus it's checked against, and on the %-of-problems-with-zero-diversity statistic it beats the four corpora the paper reports a value for (MathQA, IL, AI2, ALGES). But the paper's own appendix breaks that pattern on one statistic: measured between each corpus's test-set and training-set (Figures 5–6), MathQA has the highest lexicon and syntactic diversity of any corpus — 0.85 LD and 0.66 SD, against ASDiv-A's 0.52 LD and 0.22 SD (SD ties DRAW) — and the paper calls it "surprisingly higher than that of all other corpora" (p.10). Its own explanation (Figure 7) is that MathQA's diversity collapses once you restrict to within-test-set comparisons alone (CLD 0.27, versus 0.57 for ASDiv/ASDiv-A), meaning MathQA's test set is internally repetitive (0.27) while differing from its training set, of which the paper says the same is "suspected" — a distinct, and less flattering, sense of "diverse" than the corpus-level headline number.

The comparison basis for that headline claim, though, is quietly uneven. Section 3.4 states that CLD is computed within same problem-type groups when a corpus has PT annotation, and over the whole corpus otherwise — and by the paper's own Table 2, ASDiv is the only corpus in Figure 1 that carries problem-type annotation at all. So the 0.49 CLD credited to ASDiv is a same-type (harder, more conservative) comparison, while MathQA's 0.05 and the other six corpora's numbers are whole-corpus (easier, more generous) comparisons — a methodological asymmetry the figure and its caption never surface, even though it happens to work against ASDiv's own headline number rather than for it.

A sharper issue sits in Table 3. ASDiv-A (arithmetic-only, CLD 0.50) and full ASDiv (CLD 0.49) are almost identically diverse by the paper's own metric — yet accuracy for every solver roughly halves between them: GTS 0.68 → 0.36, LCA++ 0.68 → 0.36, UnitDep 0.78 → 0.37. Since CLD barely moved, diversity alone cannot explain that drop. The more obvious candidate is scope: LCA++, UnitDep and GTS are described as solving "only arithmetic MWPs," and full ASDiv is stated to span all 24 problem types, including the Aggregative-operations and Additional-domain-knowledge categories (algebra with unknowns, GCD/LCM, geometry, unit conversion) that ASDiv-A was explicitly built to exclude. The paper never states what these solvers score on non-arithmetic problem types, or whether the ASDiv column in Table 3 is dominated by structural failures on out-of-scope problem types rather than by lexical novelty within problems the solvers were designed to handle. Both effects plausibly point the same direction — lower solver accuracy — which is convenient for the "diversity beats size" narrative but makes it hard to tell how much of the ASDiv-A→ASDiv drop is diversity at all.

On annotation: the paper explicitly defends using a single hired annotator rather than a multi-rater agreement study, arguing MWP answer/equation correctness is objective rather than subjective, and backing that with an automatic equation-answer consistency checker plus a 480-sample (20-per-PT) manual spot check that came back 100% correct. That's a reasonable design for verifying arithmetic correctness — but the 480-sample check's scope ("annotation correctness") isn't broken out by field, so it's unclear whether it specifically audited problem-type or grade-level labels rather than just equation/answer correctness, which the automatic checker already covers. Grade-level provenance is also mixed: 63.5% of source MWPs already carried grade-level metadata from their original (28-website) source and were reused directly, while the rest were freshly labeled by the one annotator against the Common Core / Achieve Inc. references — two different labeling processes feeding one difficulty axis, with no reported check that they agree.

On durability: the three evaluated solvers — two statistical/rule-based, one small tree-structured neural network — are all pre-LLM systems; none is a transformer-based language model (GTS itself postdates the Transformer, but is a small tree-structured net built from gated feed-forward layers, not a language model). The paper offers no data point on how a modern LLM would score on ASDiv, and none should be inferred from this reading; the ~36% headline speaks only to the pre-LLM solver landscape the field has since left behind.

baselines

LCA++ and UnitDep are statistical/rule-based systems built for older arithmetic corpora and fail outright on MathQA-C; GTS is a small tree-structured neural net. None of the three is a transformer-based LLM, so the accuracy ceiling reported here says nothing about modern solvers.

contamination

Not applicable to this pre-LLM 2020 evaluation as reported; the paper doesn't address later-era pretraining leakage since it precedes that era's benchmark-contamination concerns.

what's missed

Table 3's ASDiv-A→ASDiv accuracy drop (roughly half, for all three solvers) happens with almost no change in CLD (0.50→0.49), so a confound with structural problem-type coverage — not just diversity — is plausible and untested. Figure 1's cross-corpus CLD comparison mixes within-type (ASDiv) and whole-corpus (every other corpus) diversity bases — a distinction the text states (Section 3.4) but the figure and caption don't flag.

would raise confidence

Reporting solver accuracy broken out by problem-type category on full ASDiv (to separate "harder problems" from "out-of-scope problems"); a stated inter-annotator agreement rate, or a targeted re-check of the 63.5% of grade-level labels inherited from source sites; and computing baseline corpora's CLD within their own dominant problem categories where possible, for a same-basis comparison with ASDiv.

// bottom line

ASDiv's real contribution is making "diversity, not size" a testable claim: on a corpus close in size to the MathQA-derived MathQA-C it's compared against (2,305 vs. 3,000 — ASDiv is 16× smaller than MathQA itself, but MathQA-C is where the 86% figure comes from), the same neural solver's accuracy falls from 86% to 36%, with grade 5–6 problems collapsing to near-zero. The lexicon/syntactic diversity metric is a genuinely useful diagnostic tool for corpus builders, even if the comparison basis behind its headline number, and the diversity/scope confound behind its headline solver drop, are less clean than the framing suggests.

// takeaways

What to remember

  1. ASDiv is 2,305 MWPs, not a big-data play. 24 problem types across three categories (basic arithmetic, aggregative operations, additional domain knowledge), each annotated with equation, answer, problem type, and grade level (G1–G6) — deliberately smaller than MathQA (37K) or AQuA (100K) but built to eliminate near-duplicate problems.
  2. The diversity metric is the real deliverable. A bidirectional-BLEU lexicon usage diversity (LD) score, averaged into a corpus lexicon diversity (CLD), quantifies "does this corpus reward pattern-matching?" directly — ASDiv scores 0.49 CLD against MathQA's 0.05, with 0% of its problems lexically identical to another versus MathQA's 85%.
  3. Diversity (mostly) predicts a solver-accuracy cliff. The neural GTS solver falls from 86% (MathQA-C) to 68% (ASDiv-A) to 36% (full ASDiv), and grade 5–6 accuracy craters to 1–7% for all three solvers tested. But ASDiv-A and full ASDiv have almost identical CLD (0.50 vs 0.49) despite that accuracy roughly halving between them — so some of the "diversity" story is plausibly a scope story (arithmetic-only solvers meeting non-arithmetic problem types) that the paper doesn't separately measure.
