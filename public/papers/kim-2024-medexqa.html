MedExQA: Medical Question Answering Benchmark with Multiple Explanations

MedExQA is a 965-question medical QA benchmark spanning five specialties underrepresented in existing datasets — biomedical engineering, clinical laboratory science, clinical psychology, occupational therapy, and speech-language pathology — and pairs every question with two reference explanations, human-validated for distinct writing styles, letting generation-quality metrics score against multiple references instead of one.

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

Existing medical QA benchmarks — MMLU's medical subjects (Hendrycks et al., 2020), MedQA (Jin et al., 2021), MedMCQA (Pal et al., 2022) — are built almost entirely around multiple-choice classification accuracy: pick the right letter, count correct answers. The authors argue this obscures whether a model actually understands the reasoning behind an answer or arrived at the right letter for the wrong reason, a distinction that matters most in exactly the settings where these models are pitched for deployment — clinical decision support, where a fluent but hallucinated rationale is dangerous even when the final choice happens to be correct.

Explanations, where current benchmarks have them at all, are thin. The paper singles out MedMCQA specifically: "nearly half of the evaluation dataset lacks explanations and instances of duplicate explanations are also observed. In fact, accuracy is only reported as the evaluation metric and explanation is not used in their paper entirely." None of MMLU, MedQA, or MedMCQA provides more than a single explanation per question, so there is no way to check whether an automatic generation-quality metric (BLEU, ROUGE, METEOR, BERTScore) is scoring a model's output against one arbitrary phrasing of the correct rationale or a representative one.

Topic coverage is also uneven. MMLU's nine healthcare-related subjects (1,871 test questions total), MedQA's USMLE-style questions, and MedMCQA's AIIMS/NEET-PG questions concentrate on physician-facing medical knowledge; allied-health specialties such as speech-language pathology, occupational therapy, and clinical laboratory science — all licensed medical professions with their own board exams — are largely absent. The paper's own results end up making the case for why that gap matters: every model it tests, including GPT-4, struggles specifically on the one specialty (speech-language pathology) that none of the prior benchmarks covered.

// how it works

The method

MedExQA has two components released together: a benchmark dataset, and a new small open-source medical model (MedPhi-2) trained partly to stress-test the benchmark. The dataset was built by manually collecting questions from freely accessible online mock tests and practice exams across five specialties — Biomedical Engineering, Clinical Laboratory Science, Clinical Psychology, Occupational Therapy, and Speech Language Pathology — drawn from sources whose own pass mark was 60%. Preprocessing removed duplicate questions and explanations, used BERT cosine-similarity analysis to filter near-duplicate questions, stripped questions keyed to specific laws/regulations, and shuffled answer options under fair-use guidance. Critically, every question carries two sets of explanations collected alongside it from the source material, and both the questions and both explanation sets went through human validation to confirm each explanation pair used a genuinely distinct writing style while still being comprehensible.

The result is 965 questions total (Biomedical Engineering 148, Clinical Laboratory Science 377, Clinical Psychology 111, Occupational Therapy 194, Speech Language Pathology 135), split into a 25-question few-shot development set (5 per specialty) and a 940-question test set (every specialty has at least 100 test questions). The paper reports the average cosine similarity between each question's two explanations at 78.7% overall (ranging from 73.7% in Clinical Laboratory Science to 80.5% in Speech Language Pathology) as a lexical-difference diagnostic between the paired explanations — it does not set or justify a threshold for what similarity value would count as "different enough," so the number is best read as descriptive rather than as a validated pass/fail bar.

A t-SNE projection of MedExQA's questions against MedQA, MedMCQA, and MMLU's medicine subjects (Figure 1, using the all-mpnet-base-v2 sentence embedding) shows MedExQA occupying its own region of the embedding space, which the authors use as evidence the new specialties are not just a resampling of existing question content.

Baselines and MedPhi-2

The paper benchmarks 18 open-source baseline models from 2.7B to 70B parameters: a "Llama2-variant" family — Llama2 itself (7B/13B/70B), plus medically-adapted fine-tunes ClinicalCamel (70B), Asclepius (7B/13B), Med42 (70B), AlpaCare (7B/13B), Meditron (7B/70B), and Medinote (7B/13B) — and five other general-domain foundation models (Mistral 7B, Yi 6B, Phi-2 2.7B, SOLAR 10.7B, InternLM2 7B). On top of these, the paper evaluates its own model, MedPhi-2, and three closed-source OpenAI models (GPT3.5_1106, GPT4_1106, GPT4_0125) — 22 models in total across the result tables.

MedPhi-2 continues pretraining Microsoft's Phi-2 (2.7B) on a 110M-token medical corpus (Meditron medical guidelines, SNOMED CT descriptions, biomedical article abstracts, Wikipedia medical terms, and PMC-Patients notes), then instruction-finetunes it on 239,470 instructions drawn from the Asclepius, AlpaCare, and NHS QA/medical-task instruction sets. Training used a batch size of 16, learning rate 1e-5, 3 epochs, and took 36 hours total on 8 A6000 GPUs (the same hardware used for all experiments in the paper). The stated motivation is that essentially every existing open-source medical LLM is Llama2-based, so MedPhi-2 tests whether medical domain-adaptation transfers to a different, much smaller base model.

Evaluation methodology

Every model is scored on three axes, all zero-shot with temperature 0 and batch size 1:

  • Classification accuracy — logits. The next-token logit with the highest value among the answer-choice tokens is taken as the model's answer. GPT models are excluded from this axis since their logits aren't exposed via the API.
  • Classification accuracy — chat. Regex plus fuzzy string matching (the thefuzz package) searches the model's free-text response for the answer letter or the matching choice text — a more realistic simulation of how a chat-style deployment would actually be graded.
  • Explanation generation quality. BLEU, ROUGE-L, METEOR, and BERTScore (using SciBERT embeddings) are computed for the generated explanation against the reference explanation(s). Any response whose string-matched answer choice was wrong is zeroed out on these metrics, so a fluent-but-wrong explanation cannot inflate the generation score.

A fourth axis, human evaluation, was run only on the 25-question development set: three human annotators with MSc degrees in health-related subjects scored each generated answer-explanation pair as 0 (wrong answer, and/or no or fully irrelevant explanation), 0.5 (correct answer but incorrect or incomplete explanation), or 1.0 (both correct). The reported human-eval scores in Table 6 and Figure 3 are shown on a 0–5 scale per specialty rather than this raw 0/0.5/1 rubric; the paper does not spell out the exact aggregation step between the two, so the 0–5 numbers should be read as the paper's own reported scale rather than a value independently re-derivable from the 0/0.5/1 description alone.

Finally, the paper checks whether pairing two explanations (instead of one) actually helps: it reports the Pearson correlation between the generation-quality metrics and the human-evaluation scores rises from 0.9347 (scoring against one explanation set) to 0.9385 (scoring against both), which it offers as evidence that multi-reference evaluation tracks human judgment slightly better than single-reference evaluation.

// what they found

The results

Classification accuracy (logits). Med42 (70B) had the best macro-average logits accuracy at 72.0%, leading the 19 logits-scored models in Biomedical Engineering (83.2%) and Clinical Laboratory Science (80.9%), and tying Meditron-70B for the top Clinical Psychology score (84.9% each). Llama2-70B had the top Occupational Therapy score (80.4%). Every one of the 19 logits-scored models struggled on Speech Language Pathology: the best logits accuracy there was SOLAR's 33.1% — roughly half of what the strongest models score on the other four specialties.

Classification accuracy (chat vs. logits). Moving from logit-based scoring to free-text chat generation dropped accuracy for most models (see the parenthetical deltas in the table below). Meditron-70B fell the furthest (−29.3 points on the macro average), Llama2-70B nearly as much (−28.5). Among the 70B models, ClinicalCamel was the most robust to the format change (−7.7 points) and ended up 0.7 points ahead of Med42 in chat-based macro-average accuracy despite trailing it on logits. The paper flags Phi-2, Llama2-13B, Yi, InternLM2, and Meditron-70B specifically as failing to clear the source exams' original 60% pass mark under chat scoring, calling them the least robust to the format shift; MedPhi-2 is described as the smallest chat-format drop (−0.2 points) among the models the paper counts as passing.

Explanation generation quality (Table 5, averaged across the 5 specialties). This is where the benchmark-vs-model distinction matters most. SOLAR — a general-domain 10.7B model with no medical fine-tuning — has the best AVG lexical-overlap score (ROUGE-L/METEOR/BLEU/BERTScore combined) among every open-source model at 21.45, ahead of MedPhi-2's 19.15. The paper is explicit about this: "The SOLAR model performed the best among the open-source models... although it was not trained specifically for the medical domain." MedPhi-2's own claim is narrower and does hold up: it beats every domain-adapted medical LLM baseline on this metric, including the 70B ones — ClinicalCamel (18.79), Med42 (15.82), and Meditron-70B (9.74). GPT-4 dominates both axes: GPT4_0125 leads AVG at 32.79 and GPT4_1106 close behind at 31.93, though GPT4_1106 specifically beats GPT4_0125 on METEOR (35.74 vs. 35.21) and BERTScore (54.50 vs. 54.40) even though GPT4_0125 wins on ROUGE-L, BLEU, and the overall AVG.

Human evaluation (Table 6 / Figure 3, 0–5 scale, development set only). GPT4_0125 scores highest overall (AVG 4.6), passing 4 or 5 in every specialty. Among open-source models, the paper states MedPhi-2 "has the best quality... Our model was the only open-source model that passed (a score of 3 or above) in all specialties" — a claim about breadth of coverage that checks out against Table 6 (MedPhi-2 scores 3, 3.5, 3, 3, 3 across Biomedical Engineering, Clinical Psychology, Speech Language Pathology, Occupational Therapy, and Clinical Laboratory Science respectively). It's worth noting that on the raw AVG column specifically, ClinicalCamel ties MedPhi-2 at 3.1 each — ClinicalCamel leads only on Clinical Laboratory Science (4.0 vs. 3), ties MedPhi-2 on Clinical Psychology (3.5 each) and Speech Language Pathology (3 each), and falls below 3 on Biomedical Engineering (2.5) and Occupational Therapy (2.5), where MedPhi-2 holds at 3 on both. So the "best" framing rests specifically on all-specialty coverage, not on a clean lead in the aggregate score. Only 11 of the 22 models ever reach a score of 3 in any specialty at all; the other 11 — Llama2 (7B/13B/70B), Meditron (7B/70B), Asclepius (7B/13B), Medinote (7B/13B), InternLM2, and Phi-2 — never do.

// does it hold up?

The eval read

The core methodological contribution — pairing every question with two human-validated reference explanations and showing this raises the correlation with human judgment (0.9347 → 0.9385) — is a real, if modest, empirical result, and the breadth of baselines (2.7B to 70B, seven medically fine-tuned model families, five general-domain foundation models, and three GPT variants) is genuinely comprehensive for a benchmark paper of this size. But several things are worth flagging before treating the headline numbers as settled.

baselines

Broad and reasonably current for a mid-2024 paper, but every reported number is zero-shot despite the benchmark itself providing a 5-question-per-specialty few-shot development set. That development set is used only for the (25-question) human-evaluation study, not for a few-shot-prompted accuracy run — so there's no way to tell from this paper whether the large Speech Language Pathology gap, or the general model ranking, would hold up under in-context few-shot prompting rather than the zero-shot setting tested here.

contamination

Not addressed. The question source ("freely accessible online sources, including mock tests and online exams") is exactly the kind of material that circulates in pretraining corpora, and the paper's only anti-duplication step (BERT cosine-similarity filtering) checks for near-duplicate questions within MedExQA, not overlap between MedExQA and any model's training data. No contamination or membership-inference analysis is reported for any of the 22 evaluated models.

what's missed

Most fundamentally, the paper never says where the second explanation comes from. §3.1 accounts for one: the collected mock tests and online exams are described as sources where “some questions … have explanations for the answers, which we used.” Every later section then treats two sets as given — separate word-count distributions per set, a per-set lexical analysis, and the §5.5 correlation study that is the paper's argument for having two at all — without ever stating whether the second was written by the authors, taken from a different source, or produced some other way. For a benchmark whose headline contribution is multiple explanations, that provenance is the one thing a user most needs, and ExSIM's 78.7% similarity between the pair makes it more pressing rather than less. Separately, the human evaluation that anchors the paper's central claim (multi-reference generation metrics track human judgment better than single-reference) ran on only 5 questions per specialty (25 total) with 3 annotators, and no inter-annotator agreement statistic (e.g. Cohen's kappa) is reported — the paper's own Limitations section acknowledges the scale issue directly. More pointed: that same Limitations section discloses the human evaluation was "performed... through three authors" — three of the paper's own four authors served as the MSc-qualified annotators, meaning the people grading MedPhi-2's generated explanations (the basis for the "only open-source model that passed... in all specialties" claim) were the model's own creators. Separately, the lexical generation metrics (BLEU/ROUGE-L/METEOR) are known to reward surface overlap with the reference wording; the paper's mitigation — zeroing a response's generation score whenever its string-matched answer is judged wrong — only helps if the regex/fuzzy string-matching classifier itself is accurate, and no precision/recall check on that classifier is reported.

would raise confidence

A larger, IAA-reported human-evaluation sample; an explicit contamination check against known pretraining corpora given the public, exam-prep source material; a few-shot-prompted accuracy run to disentangle "the model doesn't know this" from "the model wasn't shown the expected answer format"; and a direct accounting of how the 0/0.5/1 human-rating rubric in §4.5 becomes the 0–5 scale reported in Table 6 and Figure 3.

// bottom line

Keep the benchmark's story and the model's story separate. MedExQA's contribution — five underrepresented specialties, two human-validated reference explanations per question, and a demonstrated (if small) correlation gain from using both — stands on its own. MedPhi-2's contribution is narrower than "beats 70B medical LLMs" might suggest: it wins on generation-quality metrics against domain-adapted medical baselines and on breadth of human-eval coverage, but it does not out-accuracy the larger medical models on the underlying MCQ task (56.0% macro-average logits accuracy vs. Med42's 72.0% and ClinicalCamel's 69.3%), and it doesn't beat the best-scoring open-source model on generation quality either — that's SOLAR, which had no medical training at all.

// takeaways

What to remember

  1. MedExQA is a 965-question medical MCQ benchmark across five specialties (biomedical engineering, clinical laboratory science, clinical psychology, occupational therapy, speech-language pathology) whose distinguishing feature is two reference explanations per question, human-validated for distinct writing styles — letting BLEU/ROUGE/METEOR/BERTScore be computed against multiple references rather than one, which the paper shows nudges correlation with human judgment from 0.9347 to 0.9385.
  2. Speech-language pathology is a genuine blind spot for every model tested, including GPT-4: the best logits-based accuracy among the 19 logits-scored models on that specialty is SOLAR's 33.1%, far below what the same models score on the other four specialties.
  3. Don't conflate the benchmark's numbers with the model's. MedPhi-2 (the paper's own 2.7B Phi-2 derivative) leads every domain-adapted medical LLM baseline — including 70B ones — on generation-quality score (19.15 AVG) and is the only open-source model to score ≥3/5 across all five specialties in human evaluation, but it trails Med42 and ClinicalCamel by double digits on raw MCQ accuracy (56.0% vs. 72.0%/69.3% macro-average), and even on generation quality it's SOLAR — a model with no medical training — that scores highest among all open-source models.
