MedDG: An Entity-Centric Medical Consultation Dataset for Entity-Aware Medical Dialogue Generation

A 17,864-dialogue, 217,205-entity Chinese gastroenterology consultation corpus built to give medical dialogue generation the entity-level supervision its larger, unlabeled predecessor lacked. Its most surprising finding: a simple retrieval baseline beats both trained generators it was human-judged against on fluency, relevance, and expertise.

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

Medical dialogue is different from generic chit-chat in a way that matters for training data: it is entity-centric. A doctor's response is only useful if it names the right disease, symptom, medicine, examination, or attribute (duration, severity, and so on) — a wrong entity is not a stylistic slip, it's a wrong answer. Yet the corpora available for training and evaluating Chinese medical dialogue generation before MedDG were small: MZ (Wei et al., 2018) has 710 dialogues and 70 entity labels; DX (Xu et al., 2019) has 527 dialogues, 2,816 utterances, and 46 entity labels; CMDD (Lin et al., 2019) has 2,067 dialogues, 87,005 utterances, and 161 entity labels; MIE (Zhang et al., 2020) has 1,120 dialogues, 18,129 utterances, and 71 entity labels. None of them is large enough, in the authors' framing, to train a genuinely competitive end-to-end dialogue system.

The obvious alternative, MedDialog (Zeng et al., referenced here as [3]), solved the scale problem — its Chinese side reaches millions of crawled dialogues — but not the entity problem: it carries no entity annotation at all, so a model trained or evaluated on it can be perfectly fluent while still hallucinating the wrong diagnosis, and MedDG's authors note directly that MedDialog's pretrained dialogue models "did not consider medical entities, which are the most central part of medical dialogues." A supporting motivation comes from the authors' own manual analysis of 1,000 sampled Doctor Chunyu conversations: 40.1% of patient-authored sentences turned out to be redundant chit-chat that contributes nothing to diagnosis, and the order in which a doctor asks about different symptoms is not fixed — both observations argue for entity-level structure over raw dialogue text. MedDG's gap, then, is squarely: build a corpus with MedDialog-scale ambitions but with the entity annotation MedDialog never had, so both training (predict the next entity) and evaluation (did the response mention the right ones) become possible.

// how it works

The method

The raw dialogues come from the gastroenterology department of Doctor Chunyu (chunyuyisheng.com), a Chinese online consultation platform, chosen because gastrointestinal consultations are common, diverse, and rely less on physical examination than many other specialties — a better fit for a text-only online setting. Over 100,000 dialogues were collected and then filtered down to those with between 10 and 50 utterances and no non-text content (images, audio), leaving 17,864 dialogues and, per the abstract, 385,951 utterances (Table 3 in the paper lists 385,949 utterances — a two-utterance discrepancy between the two places the paper reports this count). Privacy was handled in two passes: the source platform had already stripped sensitive fields before release, and the authors additionally ran regex rules to catch ages, phone numbers, and addresses, manually checked the hits, and asked annotators to flag anything they noticed during entity labeling; neither pass turned up any residual private information.

Five entity categories were defined after discussion with domain experts and by reference to the terminology in the Chinese medical knowledge graph CMeKG: disease, symptom, medicine, examination, and attribute, comprising 12, 62, 62, 20, and 4 entities respectively (160 total). Annotation is semi-automatic. First, eight annotators with over a year of consultation experience — paid $50/hour, roughly 5 minutes per dialogue — independently double-annotated 1,000 dialogues, with a third annotator adjudicating disagreements; pairwise inter-annotator Cohen's kappa ranged 0.948–0.976. Second, those same 1,000 human-labeled dialogues were used to hand-derive a set of regular-expression rules, refined over four revision cycles by checking the program's output against expert re-labeling of 2,000 sampled utterances, reaching 96.75% annotation accuracy on that check set — which the authors use to argue (p < 0.01) that whole-dataset accuracy exceeds 95%. That automatic program then labeled the remaining 16,864 dialogues. The final split is 14,864 train / 2,000 dev / 1,000 test, with the 1,000 human-annotated dialogues placed entirely in the test set and the rest randomly divided between train and dev.

Two tasks are built on top of the annotation. Entity prediction treats each of the 160 predefined entities as an independent binary classification given the dialogue history so far, scored with a sigmoid over an encoder's context vector and trained with binary cross-entropy. Entity-based response generation concatenates the predicted entity set with the dialogue history and asks a generation model to produce the next response, trained with the standard per-token negative log-likelihood.

g(ei) = Sigmoid(wpT hD) // per-entity presence score from the dialogue-history context vector h_D; thresholded at 0.5 to form the predicted entity set

Entity-prediction baselines: LSTM, TextCNN, BERT-wwm (whole-word-masked Chinese BERT), PCL-MedBERT, and MedDGBERT — a BERT further pretrained on MedDG's own training set with a masked-entity objective. Generation baselines: Retrieval, which fills the response slot with a real training-set doctor utterance whose annotated entities are the minimal cover of the predicted entity set; Seq2Seq; HRED; GPT-2; DialoGPT; BERT-GPT (a BERT encoder plus GPT decoder, fine-tuned on the MedDialog corpus); and MedDGBERT-GPT, the same architecture with the encoder weights swapped for MedDGBERT's. Every generation baseline except Retrieval also has a "w/o Ent." ablation that skips entity prediction entirely and generates straight from the (truncated to the last patient utterance) dialogue history. Critically, the entities fed into every entity-aware generator are MedDGBERT's own predictions, not gold labels.

// what they found

The results

On entity prediction (all figures in %), MedDGBERT posts the top overall F1 (29.81) and the top recall (36.20) of the five encoders — ahead of PCL-MedBERT (F1 29.40), BERT-wwm (28.35), LSTM (26.49), and TextCNN (25.67) — but it is not uniformly best: PCL-MedBERT has the higher precision (26.46 vs. 25.34) and the higher disease-category F1 (33.72 vs. 33.29), and BERT-wwm has the higher attribute-category F1 (52.44 vs. 49.41). Every one of the five encoders scores far higher on the attribute category (46.85–52.44 F1) than on any other — disease sits at 29.54–33.72, symptom at 20.55–26.39, medicine at 15.66–21.35, examination at 23.58–27.60 — and no encoder's overall F1 clears 30%, which the authors read as evidence entity prediction from dialogue context alone remains largely unsolved.

On response generation, entity-aware conditioning raised both BLEU and entity-overlap scores for every generative baseline tested. The paper's own headline comparison is MedDGBERT-GPT against its w/o-Ent ablation: a 14.51% relative BLEU-4 gain (20.95 → 23.99) and a 65.54% relative F1Ent gain (11.23 → 18.59). MedDGBERT-GPT ends up with the top BLEU-4 (23.99) and top F1Ent (18.59) of the seven generation systems, narrowly ahead of BERT-GPT (BLEU-4 23.84, F1Ent 17.65) — but HRED alone has the highest raw BLEU-1 of any system (38.66), beating every GPT-family model including MedDGBERT-GPT's 36.62; the authors attribute this to GPT-style models being pretrained mostly on generic chit-chat data and drifting off the medical topic in ways that cost n-gram overlap even as they raise diversity (several entity-aware GPT variants — GPT-2, BERT-GPT, MedDGBERT-GPT — actually see both Distinct-1 and Distinct-2 fall relative to their own w/o-Ent ablation, which the paper attributes to entity-aware generation converging on more fixed phrasing). Retrieval, which by construction always covers its predicted entities, has by far the highest entity recall (REnt 36.25 vs. 20.76–28.71 for every generative model) but the lowest BLEU-4 (12.58) in the table.

Human evaluation covered 3 of the paper's 7 generation systems (5 of the 13 rows in Table 5): Retrieval, HRED, HRED w/o Ent., MedDGBERT-GPT, and MedDGBERT-GPT w/o Ent. Three of the paper's own annotators rated 100 sampled test dialogues (average 9.71 doctor utterances each) on a 1–5 scale for Fluency, Relevance, and Expertise. Retrieval swept all three axes (4.23 / 4.18 / 4.29), well ahead of MedDGBERT-GPT (3.93 / 3.62 / 3.55) and HRED (3.46 / 3.16 / 3.12); the entity-aware variant beat its own ablation on every axis for both HRED (2.84/2.73/2.40 → 3.46/3.16/3.12) and MedDGBERT-GPT (3.27/2.83/2.69 → 3.93/3.62/3.55), matching the automatic-metric story on that narrower question. Inter-annotator Cohen's kappa on this human study was 0.41 (Fluency), 0.52 (Relevance), and 0.59 (Expertise) — moderate agreement, well below the 0.948–0.976 the same annotator pool reached on entity labeling.

// does it hold up?

The eval read

MedDG's headline claims rest on two evaluations of very different rigor. The entity-annotation pipeline is checked hard: near-perfect human inter-annotator agreement (kappa 0.948–0.976) and a 96.75%-accuracy spot check of the auto-labeler against expert relabeling. The response-quality human study is checked much more lightly: 100 dialogues, 3 raters, kappa 0.41–0.59, and only 3 of the paper's 7 generation systems (5 of the 13 rows in Table 5, counting the w/o-Ent ablations) included. That asymmetry matters because the study's most attention-grabbing result — that plain retrieval beats both of the trained generators it was actually rated against, HRED and MedDGBERT-GPT, on fluency, relevance, and expertise — is exactly the result carried by the thinner evaluation.

baselines

Reasonable for late-2020 Chinese medical dialogue work (LSTM/TextCNN/BERT-wwm/PCL-MedBERT for entity prediction; Seq2Seq/HRED/GPT-2/DialoGPT/BERT-GPT/MedDGBERT-GPT for generation), but no knowledge-graph-conditioned or retrieval-augmented generator beyond the paper's own simple entity-coverage Retrieval baseline is tried, and only 3 of the 7 generation systems (5 of 13 Table 5 rows) were human-rated — BERT-GPT, essentially tied with MedDGBERT-GPT on automatic BLEU-4 (23.84 vs. 23.99) and F1Ent (17.65 vs. 18.59), was never put in front of a human rater.

contamination

Not a pretrain/benchmark-overlap issue (this predates the LLM-benchmark era and the corpus is a fresh single-platform crawl), but there is a related design coupling worth flagging precisely: per Sec. 3.2, the 160-entity taxonomy was fixed first, from CMeKG terminology lists plus entity frequency across the whole 17,864-dialogue corpus (so the test dialogues contributed to that frequency count too), and then the same 1,000 human-annotated dialogues that make up the entire test set were the basis on which the regex rules were first hand-derived, before being refined over four cycles against 2,000 separately expert-relabeled utterances and used to auto-label all 16,864 remaining train/dev dialogues — rather than being held out from that design choice.

what's missed

BLEU/Distinct/F1Ent say nothing about whether a response is medically sound — the paper's own "Expertise" axis is the only stab at that, and it's measured on just 100 dialogues at kappa 0.59. Retrieval's high entity recall (36.25) is close to circular, since its selection rule is defined to maximize predicted-entity coverage. And because every entity-aware generator is conditioned on MedDGBERT's own predictions rather than gold entities, and MedDGBERT's own overall F1 never exceeds 29.81%, the reported "entity-awareness helps" gains are bottlenecked by (and partly just reflect) how good that one predictor already is.

would raise confidence

A human-eval pass over all 7 generation systems rather than 3; higher inter-annotator agreement than the 0.41–0.59 currently reported for Fluency/Relevance/Expertise (or more raters); an independent audit of the auto-labeled 16,864-dialogue majority of the corpus beyond the original 2,000-utterance spot check; and at least a second consultation platform or disease area to test whether a single-platform, single-department (gastroenterology, 12 diseases) corpus generalizes.

// bottom line

MedDG's real contribution is scale-with-labels: 217,205 annotated entities across 17,864 dialogues make it, by the paper's own Table 1, one to two orders of magnitude larger by dialogue and utterance count than the entity-annotated Chinese corpora that preceded it (MZ, DX, CMDD, MIE) — though CMDD's raw entity-label count (161) is nominally higher than MedDG's own 160, so "largest" here means dialogue/utterance scale and per-entity frequency, not the size of the entity vocabulary. It's the first Chinese medical dialogue set built to check whether a generation model names the right entities, not just whether it's fluent. But the "entity-awareness helps" finding rides on an entity predictor that tops out near 30% F1, and the paper's most striking result — that a dumb retrieval baseline outrates both trained generators it was actually judged against — was checked on only 100 dialogues, 3 raters, and 3 of 7 systems.

// takeaways

What to remember

  1. MedDG pairs MedDialog-scale ambition with the entity annotation MedDialog never had: 217,205 entities across 5 categories (disease/symptom/medicine/examination/attribute) over 17,864 Chinese gastroenterology dialogues, so a model's entity accuracy — not just its fluency — can be scored directly.
  2. Entity-aware conditioning helped every generation baseline tried, on both BLEU and entity-F1 (MedDGBERT-GPT: +14.51% relative BLEU-4, +65.54% relative F1Ent over its own w/o-Ent ablation) — but the fed-in entities are themselves noisy, since the strongest entity predictor tested (MedDGBERT) never exceeds 29.81% F1.
  3. Automatic and human evaluation tell different stories: BLEU/Distinct/F1Ent favor the trained generators (MedDGBERT-GPT, BERT-GPT), but the paper's own 100-dialogue human study rates a simple retrieval baseline highest on fluency, relevance, and expertise alike — a caution against reading n-gram metrics alone as a proxy for response quality on this task.
  4. The 1,000 dialogues held out as the test set are the same 1,000 human-annotated dialogues used to hand-derive the regex auto-labeling rules applied to the other 16,864 train/dev dialogues — worth knowing before treating MedDG's test numbers as a fully independent holdout.
