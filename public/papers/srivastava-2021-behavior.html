BEHAVIOR: Benchmark for Everyday Household Activities in Virtual, Interactive, and Ecological Environments

A benchmark of 100 household activities — cleaning, cooking, tidying, packing — grounded in a real government survey of how Americans spend their time, defined declaratively in a predicate-logic language so a simulator can generate unlimited instances and grade partial progress, and evaluated with 500 human VR demonstrations as the efficiency reference. State-of-the-art RL scores zero on nearly all of even the 12 easiest activities.

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

By 2021, embodied-AI benchmarking had split into two camps that each captured only part of what a household robot needs. Visual-navigation and instruction-following benchmarks (VirtualHome, ALFRED) offered diverse scenes and language grounding but let agents pick among discrete, predefined action primitives with full access to world state — low physical complexity. Manipulation benchmarks (RLBench, Meta-World, Robosuite, SoftGym) delivered real physics but confined themselves to a handful of hand-picked, short-horizon tasks — low diversity. Rearrangement-style benchmarks (the Rearrangement challenge, TDW Transport, AI2-THOR Visual Room Rearrangement) added realistic scenes and physics but reduced "success" to moving objects to target poses — a small slice of what housework actually involves, since most chores also change an object's temperature, wetness, or cleanliness, not just its position.

The paper frames this as three unresolved difficulties, one per stage of building a benchmark. Definition: which activities even belong in a household benchmark, and on what basis — existing benchmarks pick a handful by hand, with no claim to represent how people actually spend their time. Realization: most simulators can change an object's position but not its extended physical state (burnt, frozen, dusty, soaked), so entire categories of housework (cooking, cleaning, laundering) are out of scope. Evaluation: a binary success/failure signal says nothing about partial progress on a long, multi-step activity, and efficiency (time, distance, disruption caused) has no common scale across wildly different tasks and scenes. BEHAVIOR is built to answer all three at once, and to do so by anchoring activity selection in an independent, real-world source rather than the authors' own judgment.

// how it works

The method

BEHAVIOR's 100 activities are not hand-picked. They are drawn from the American Time Use Survey (ATUS), a U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics survey covering more than 2,200 activities Americans report spending time on, filtered for semantic diversity, diversity of required state changes, and simulation feasibility (indoor, mostly rigid or articulated objects — no outdoor scenes, other humans/animals, or heavy cloth/fluid simulation). The resulting set spans cleaning (cleaningBathtub), tidying (puttingAwayToys), packing (packingPicnics), and food prep (preservingFood, choppingVegetables), among others.

Each activity is defined declaratively, not as a scripted trajectory, using BEHAVIOR Domain Definition Language (BDDL), a predicate-logic representation the authors describe as inspired by but distinct from PDDL. An activity τ is a pair of state sets — an initial-condition set and a goal-condition set — each expressed as logical formulas over unary and binary object-state predicates: unary predicates like Cooked(fish), Sliced(tomato), or Dusty(table) capture kinematic, temperature, wetness, and cleanliness states; binary predicates like OnTopOf(apple, plate), InsideOf(food, fridge), or Under(present, tree) capture spatial relations. Variables and constants are grounded in categories from a new BEHAVIOR Object Dataset (1,217 object models across 391 categories, organized by WordNet). Because the definition is purely declarative — a set of acceptable states rather than a specific path to reach them — the simulator can sample "potentially infinite" concrete initial scenes that satisfy the initial condition, and can accept any physically valid solution that reaches any state satisfying the goal condition, rather than only the one geometry the designer happened to script.

Activity definitions themselves were crowdsourced, not written by the authors: US-based Upwork annotators ($15/annotation, roughly $20-30/hour) used a Blockly-based visual interface to compose BDDL initial and goal conditions per activity, with a live "check feasibility" button that sampled the draft definition against remote iGibson 2.0 simulator instances before it was accepted. This produced 200 crowdsourced definitions — two per activity — from which 300 activity instances were generated in simulation.

The reference implementation runs in iGibson 2.0, built on the Bullet physics engine, across 15 fully-interactive reconstructions of real homes (100 rooms total). iGibson 2.0 extends the earlier iGibson with the non-kinematic state machinery BDDL needs: heat and water sources, dust and stain particles, and object appearance that changes with extended state. Two embodiments are provided: a 24-degrees-of-freedom bimanual humanoid avatar (what human VR demonstrators control) and a 12/13-DoF Fetch mobile-manipulator robot whose kinematics match real hardware. Both operate at 30 Hz and can act either through low-level continuous control or through six temporally extended action primitives — navigate_to, grasp, place_onTop, place_inside, open, close — each available in a "fully-simulated" variant (motion-planned and collision-checked via bidirectional RRT*, actually executing the interaction) or a "partially-simulated" variant (teleporting directly to a feasible target configuration, skipping physical execution).

Because a binary complete/incomplete signal can't measure partial progress on a long activity, the primary metric is a graduated success score Q. A goal condition is flattened into a set of disjunctive clauses, each a conjunction of ground literals; Q is the maximum, over those clauses, of the fraction of that clause's literals currently true:

Q = maxCi∈C|{lji ∈ Ci : lji = True}||Ci| // fraction of satisfied goal literals in the best-matching disjunct; Q=1 only when some clause is fully satisfied

Six secondary efficiency metrics complement Q: simulated time (Tsim), kinematic disarrangement (DK, physical displacement the agent causes), logical disarrangement (DL, change in logical state), distance navigated (Lbody), and hand displacement while manipulating (Lleft, Lright). All six are costs, not scores — lower is better: the paper motivates them by noting that "a successful solution in real-world tasks may be ineffective if it takes too long or causes scene disruption," so less accumulated time, displacement, and disarrangement is the efficient outcome. Each can also be expressed as a fraction of the matching human VR demonstration's value on that same metric — a "human-centric" normalization meant to make efficiency comparable across activities and scenes of very different scale. To supply that reference, the authors collected the BEHAVIOR Dataset of Human Demonstrations: 500 successful demonstrations (758.5 minutes total) captured via an HTC Vive Pro Eye headset, with humans controlling the same bimanual-avatar embodiment as AI agents. For each of the 100 activities the dataset includes three demonstrations of the same activity instance, one demonstration of a different instance in the same scene, and one demonstration in a different scene — deliberately spanning both within-instance variance and BEHAVIOR's own instance diversity. These are a reference for normalizing efficiency, not a performance ceiling the agents are meant to beat outright — and being collected in VR, they inherit VR's own interaction and sensing constraints rather than being unconstrained natural human behavior.

// what they found

The results

Because training RL policies across all 100 activities is computationally expensive, the paper's experiments run on "the 12 most simple activities," selected by the types of state changes they involve: bringingInWood, collectMisplacedItems, movingBoxesToStorage, organizingFileCabinet, throwingAwayLeftovers, puttingDishesAway, puttingLeftoversAway, re-shelvingLibraryBooks, layingTileFloors, settingUpCandles, pickingUpTrash, storingFood — an easiest-case subset of the full 100, not a random sample. Two general-purpose RL algorithms are tested: Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) for continuous low-level control, and PPO for the temporally-extended action primitives.

Under the fully realistic setup — continuous control, onboard realistic sensing, real physics — the mean success score Q at convergence (3 seeds) is 0 on all 12 activities: the trained agents satisfy not one goal literal on any of them. Swapping to PPO with action primitives (still realistic sensing and physics) doesn't move most of that: 11 of the 12 activities are still Q=0, with only bringingInWood reaching a nonzero Q of 0.13.

Two further ablations isolate which dimension of difficulty is doing the damage. An oracle-initialization experiment starts SAC agents from a saved human-demonstration state a few seconds before that demonstration's own successful completion, and reports success rate SR (full completion over 100 eval episodes) — a different metric from Q, measured under a very different protocol. At 1 second from the goal, 6 of the 12 activities reach SR=1 (bringingInWood, movingBoxesToStorage, collectMisplacedItems, layingTileFloors, settingUpCandles, storingFood) and pickingUpTrash reaches 0.97; by 10 seconds from the goal every one of the 12 activities is back to SR=0. A realism ablation then varies observability and physics independently, both with action primitives. Granting the agent privileged full state observability alone (physics and actuation still realistic): Q becomes nonzero on 5 of the 12 activities (0.20, 0.02, 0.49, 0.13, 0.09) — still mostly zero. Dropping physics alone instead — action primitives execute as idealized teleports to a feasible target configuration rather than physically-simulated contact, while observations stay realistic — does far more: Q is nonzero on 9 of the 12 activities, up to 0.92 (bringingInWood), with movingBoxesToStorage 0.73, puttingDishesAway 0.55, collectMisplacedItems 0.47, puttingLeftoversAway 0.44, throwingAwayLeftovers and storingFood both 0.32, settingUpCandles 0.27, and re-shelvingLibraryBooks 0.04 — only organizingFileCabinet, layingTileFloors, and pickingUpTrash stay at Q=0. Combining no-physics with full observability adds relatively little on top of that: it pushes two activities (bringingInWood, storingFood) to a clean Q=1.0 and 10 of 12 nonzero overall. So it is dropping physics — idealized actuation rather than realistic contact dynamics — that does most of the work of making performance nonzero; full observability's marginal contribution on top of that is mainly the two activities that reach a clean 1.0. Two activities — organizingFileCabinet and layingTileFloors — stay at Q=0 across every one of these six Q-based conditions (only layingTileFloors recovers, and only under the separate oracle-SR protocol).

A separate diversity ablation trains SAC on five single-predicate, one- or two-object probe tasks that are not part of the 100 BEHAVIOR activities — onTop, sliced, soaked, stained, cooked — under increasing instantiation diversity. With no diversity (fixed object pose, fixed object instance), Q is 1.0 on onTop, soaked, stained, and cooked, but already only 0.15 on sliced. Adding pose diversity alone drops sliced to 0 and stained to 0.28 (onTop 0.825, soaked 0.935, cooked 0.66). Adding object-instance diversity on top drops onTop further to 0.46, stained to 0.11, and cooked to 0.265, while sliced stays at 0 and soaked stays close to its prior value (0.925). Even in these deliberately trivial one- or two-object tasks, performance degrades quickly once the environment varies.

// does it hold up?

The eval read

BEHAVIOR's contribution is mostly infrastructure — a declarative task language, a simulator instantiation, and an evaluation protocol — rather than a solved benchmark, and the paper is honest that the experiments are meant to demonstrate difficulty rather than to characterize the space of solutions. A few things are worth being precise about before quoting its numbers.

The headline zero scores come from only 12 of the 100 activities — explicitly "the 12 most simple activities" by the types of state changes involved. That the easiest eighth of the benchmark defeats standard RL is a meaningful floor, but it says nothing directly about the other 88 (likely harder) activities, and none of the paper's numbers should be read as "BEHAVIOR" scores in general. Whether those 12-activity runs are trained and evaluated on a single fixed activity instance or across multiple instances of the same activity definition is not stated: the realism/complexity setups in Sec. 7 and Appendix A.7 describe what varies between conditions (observability, physics, action space) without ever saying how many instances a given training run saw, so a reader can't tell whether a nonzero cell reflects generalizing across BEHAVIOR's declared "potentially infinite" instance space or fitting one instantiation of it. The paper's own diagnosis leans toward the latter being at least part of the story — it hypothesizes that diversity in scenes, objects, and initial states is itself a cause of the near-zero 12-activity results, which only makes sense if those runs were exposed to varied instances. The one place a single fixed state is explicit is the oracle-initialization ablation, where every SAC run starts from a saved state a few seconds before one human demonstration's own successful completion, so a nonzero SR there is finishing near a known-good state rather than generalizing across instances. The diversity ablation (Table 3) tests instance diversity directly, but on five single-predicate probe tasks that are explicitly not among the 100 named activities — a related but distinct experiment, not a diversity breakdown of the headline results.

Only two RL algorithms are tested, SAC and PPO, both general-purpose flat policies. The paper itself hypothesizes that hierarchical RL or task-and-motion-planning (TAMP) approaches might do better given BEHAVIOR's long horizons, but doesn't test either — so the near-zero results establish a floor for flat model-free RL specifically, not for embodied AI as a field. On the realism side, the paper is candid that its sim-to-real argument is not experimentally validated: the appendix states plainly that testing whether solutions transfer to a real robot is "a claim that we plan to evaluate experimentally after the pandemic," so every realism claim in the main text rests on physics-engine and rendering-fidelity arguments, not measured transfer.

The two success metrics used across the results — the graduated score Q and the binary success rate SR used in the oracle-initialization ablation — are reported in the same table but are not on the same scale or protocol (Q is mean end-of-training performance across 3 training seeds; SR is fraction of 100 eval episodes reaching full completion from a human-demonstrated near-goal state). A reader skimming Table 2's rows for a single "how close did it get" number needs to track which metric a given row is actually reporting.

Even the rows this page calls "realistic sensing" aren't sensor-only: the paper is explicit that "Even though the agent only relies on sensory observations to decide on action primitive, the primitives themselves internally assume access to privileged information (e.g. object identities, poses, and geometric shapes for planning)," and the appendix adds that for both the fully- and partially-simulated primitive variants, "the agent knows how many activity-relevant objects are in the scene, and the motion planner knows the full geometry of the environment." So the primitive-based rows (Qap, Qap_NoPhys) already give the low-level controller state its own camera wouldn't recover — the continuous-action Qca rows use no primitives and no motion planner, so the leak does not reach them — the realism axis Table 2 varies is physics and full-state observability specifically, not privileged planning information, which rides along with the action primitives wherever they are used, throughout.

A second confound runs the other way, and the paper states it plainly in A.7: “We train with partially simulated motion primitives until convergence, and evaluate and report the results on partially simulated motion primitives and fully simulated motion primitives, since training with motion planning in a complex scene is very time-consuming.” So every fully-simulated-primitive number is zero-shot transfer from a policy trained under the cheaper approximation — the physics penalty those rows show is a mix of physics being harder and the policy never having seen it. The compute rationale is honest and the scope is limited (the continuous-action rows are trained and evaluated under identical conditions, so the near-zero floor there is unaffected), but it means the physics axis in Table 2 is not a clean ablation.

One internal inconsistency worth flagging: the abstract, Table 1, and the main text all put the VR demonstration count at 500 ("500 successful VR demonstrations," 758.5 minutes total), but the expanded comparison in Appendix Table A.1 prints BEHAVIOR's "# Human VR demos" as 400 — a discrepancy the paper doesn't reconcile anywhere in the text.

baselines

Only SAC and PPO — general-purpose, flat RL, no hierarchical-RL or TAMP baseline despite the paper's own authors nominating that as the likely fix for BEHAVIOR's long horizons. Experiments cover 12 of the 100 activities, chosen as the simplest by state-change type, not a random sample.

contamination

Not the LLM-pretraining-leakage kind — no pretrained LLM agents are evaluated here. The nearer analogue, train/eval instance diversity, is unreported for the 12-activity RL runs: the paper never states how many instances of each activity definition a training run was exposed to, so a nonzero cell could reflect generalizing across BEHAVIOR's instance space or fitting a single instantiation of it — and the paper's own hypothesis that instance diversity is itself a cause of the near-zero results argues for the latter. The one case with an explicit single fixed state is the oracle-initialization ablation, which starts every run from one saved near-goal state taken from a human demonstration.

what's missed

No per-step failure-mode breakdown (grasp failure vs. navigation failure vs. sequencing failure) for any of the near-zero cells, and no real-robot transfer experiment — the appendix explicitly defers sim-to-real validation to future work ("after the pandemic").

would raise confidence

A hierarchical-RL or TAMP baseline on the same 12 activities; an eval split across different instances of the same activity definition, to show whether any nonzero cell actually generalizes; and the deferred sim-to-real transfer study.

// bottom line

BEHAVIOR's real claim is narrower and more defensible than "current AI can't do household chores": under realistic sensing and physics, standard RL scores essentially zero on even the easiest eighth of the benchmark's activities. Relaxing realistic actuation (removing physics) is what does most of the work of lifting that floor — 9 of 12 activities become nonzero, up to Q=0.92 — while relaxing sensing (full observability) with physics still realistic leaves most activities at zero; the two conditions combined only add two activities reaching a clean Q=1.0. That's a credible, well-instrumented floor for flat RL on long-horizon, multi-object household tasks, and it locates the bottleneck at actuation rather than perception — the harder question of what actually works, and whether it generalizes across BEHAVIOR's own instance diversity, is explicitly left open.

// takeaways

What to remember

  1. BDDL makes "success" a declarative logic check — Q, the maximum fraction of satisfied goal literals across a goal condition's disjunctive clauses — rather than a match against one scripted trajectory. That's what lets BEHAVIOR generate unlimited scene instances per activity while still knowing when any of them counts as solved, and it's the paper's most transferable methodological idea for other embodied benchmarks.
  2. The 100 activities aren't hand-picked: they're filtered from the American Time Use Survey's 2,200+ real activities, spanning 391 WordNet-grounded object categories and 1,217 object models across 15 real-home scene reconstructions, with 500 VR human demonstrations (758.5 minutes) as the efficiency reference. But the paper's own RL experiments exercise only the 12 simplest of those 100 activities.
  3. Under realistic physics and sensing, SAC/PPO score Q=0 on all or nearly all of even those 12 easiest activities. Removing physics alone (idealized actuation, sensing still realistic) is what actually moves most activities off zero — 9 of 12, up to Q=0.92 — while removing only the sensing constraint (full observability, physics still realistic) leaves 7 of 12 at zero; combining both pushes just two activities to a clean Q=1.0. Two activities (organizingFileCabinet, layingTileFloors) never rise above Q=0 in any of the six Q-based conditions the paper tests.
