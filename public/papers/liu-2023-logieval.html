Evaluating the Logical Reasoning Ability of ChatGPT and GPT-4

A same-season-as-GPT-4 audit of logical reasoning: ChatGPT and GPT-4 are prompted zero/one-shot on LogiQA 2.0, ReClor, and AR-LSAT (multi-choice) and on six NLI datasets, with a newly-built out-of-distribution split used to check whether the well-known benchmarks' scores actually generalize — and the whole prompt-style harness is released as a suite the authors name LogiEval.

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

GPT-4 shipped in March 2023 billed as more capable at reasoning, but there was no focused, apples-to-apples read on logical reasoning specifically — broader ChatGPT audits from the same weeks (Bang et al., Qin et al., both cited here) covered many task families at once without isolating logical inference. The two established logical-reasoning benchmarks in the field, LogiQA and ReClor, were built and scored for the fine-tuning regime: train a supervised model like RoBERTa on the training split, test on held-out. Neither was designed for the zero/few-shot prompting regime that ChatGPT and GPT-4 are actually used in, and both were released in 2020 — years before either model's training cutoff — so a strong score on them doesn't by itself distinguish genuine reasoning from benchmark familiarity. The paper's response is threefold: reformat LogiQA 2.0, ReClor, and six NLI datasets into single-turn prompts; incorporate AR-LSAT (Wang et al., 2022) — a pre-existing, harder, and more recently published dataset of LSAT analytical-reasoning "logic games"; and construct a new out-of-distribution split from 2022 Chinese Civil Servant Exam questions, so a model's performance on familiar benchmarks can be checked against its performance on material that couldn't have been seen the same way during training. All three model families — RoBERTa (fine-tuned), ChatGPT, and GPT-4 — are then scored against each other and against reported human performance.

// how it works

The method

The evaluation splits into two task families. Multi-choice reading comprehension covers LogiQA 2.0's English test set (1,572 items) and Chinese test set (1,594 items), ReClor's dev set (500 items — ReClor withholds its test-set gold labels), AR-LSAT's test set (230 items, unusually five-option rather than four), and a LogiQA 2.0 "ood" split the authors built themselves (1,354 items) from 2022 Chinese Civil Servant Exam questions that postdate the source material behind the original LogiQA 2.0 splits. Natural language inference covers ConTRoL, ConjNLI, HELP, and MED (all logic-flavored 3-way entailment/contradiction/neutral datasets), TaxiNLI, and MNLI dev — MNLI is included only as a non-logic-specific comparison point, since TaxiNLI is itself a re-annotated MNLI subset.

RoBERTa-base is fine-tuned for 5 epochs per dataset as the supervised baseline; the paper also reports human-average and human-ceiling numbers per dataset. ChatGPT is queried as gpt-3.5-turbo (the March 23, 2023 snapshot) through OpenAI's Evals framework, with one in-context example appended to every call "to guarantee controlled output." GPT-4 is queried two ways: through early API access to a "default-gpt-4" build (March 14, 2023) over the full test sets via the same Evals framework, and by hand through the GPT-4 ChatUI on two Plus accounts — which only covers a manually-tested subsample of 85 to 112 items per dataset, well under the full test-set sizes. The multi-choice prompt (Figure 1) instructs the model to answer with a single letter A–D; the NLI prompt asks for one of three labels, 'e' / 'c' / 'n'.

Three smaller follow-up analyses round out the paper: a hand-categorized breakdown of GPT-4's first 10 errors on LogiQA (4 logical errors, 3 scope errors, 3 semantic-ambiguity errors); a 20-instance in-context-learning probe comparing GPT-4 accuracy when the same 20 questions are asked inside one chat conversation versus in 20 separate fresh conversations; and a zero-shot chain-of-thought run ("Let's think step by step") on the same 112-item LogiQA 2.0 ood subsample used for the GPT-4 ChatUI manual test. The authors name the prompt-style suite LogiEval (github.com/csitfun/LogiEval).

// what they found

The results

On the five multi-choice reading-comprehension datasets (Table 1), ChatGPT (API) beats the fine-tuned RoBERTa-base baseline on four of five: 52.37% vs 48.76% on LogiQA 2.0 test, 53.18% vs 35.64% on the Chinese LogiQA 2.0 zh test, 57.38% vs 55.01% on ReClor dev, and 38.44% vs 33.22% on the newly-built LogiQA 2.0 ood split — but loses on AR-LSAT, 20.42% vs RoBERTa's 23.14%, both close to the dataset's 20% five-option chance floor. GPT-4 (API) beats RoBERTa on all five, including AR-LSAT (33.48% vs 23.14%): 72.25% on LogiQA 2.0 test, 70.56% on the Chinese version, and 87.20% on ReClor dev — the highest ReClor score among the paper's three compared models (RoBERTa, ChatGPT, GPT-4 API) — alongside 58.49% on LogiQA 2.0 ood. Even so, GPT-4's OOD score trails its LogiQA 2.0 test and ReClor scores by 13.8 and 28.7 points (58.49% vs. 72.25% and 87.20%), and its AR-LSAT score — a harder, five-option task with a lower 20% chance floor — trails them by 38.8 and 53.7 points (33.48% vs. 72.25% and 87.20%); both AR-LSAT and OOD remain below the reported human averages on those two datasets (56.00% and 83.00%) and human ceilings (91.00% and 99.00%). The manually-tested GPT-4 ChatUI subsample follows the same pattern with wider swings on its smaller samples — 92.00% (92/100) on ReClor dev against just 18.27% (19/104) on AR-LSAT.

On the six NLI datasets (Table 2), ChatGPT (API) again beats RoBERTa on five of six — the exception is MNLI dev, where RoBERTa's 90.02% (near its fine-tuning ceiling on a dataset it's essentially trained for) dwarfs ChatGPT's 55.40%. GPT-4 (API) beats RoBERTa on the same five of six, also missing only MNLI dev (64.08% vs 90.02%). The paper characterizes GPT-4 specifically as underperforming here relative to its own multi-choice reading comprehension — a comparison it doesn't extend to ChatGPT, and one that isn't strictly apples-to-apples in any case since the NLI datasets use a three-way label space (roughly 33% chance) against the four- or five-way multi-choice datasets (20–25% chance): GPT-4 (API) reaches 89.42% on MED and 72.71% on ConjNLI but only 46.01% on HELP and 56.40% on ConTRoL, and the paper notes that GPT-4 has trouble reliably emitting one of the three e/c/n labels the NLI prompt asks for, despite following the multi-choice instruction format cleanly.

Two smaller experiments round out the results. With 20 questions kept in the same chat conversation, GPT-4 answers 9/20 (45.00%) LogiQA 2.0 ood items and 13/20 (65.00%) ConTRoL items correctly, versus only 5/20 (25.00%) and 7/20 (35.00%) when each of the same 20 questions is asked in a fresh conversation window (Table 3, below). Adding a zero-shot chain-of-thought instruction to the same 112-item LogiQA 2.0 ood subsample used for the GPT-4 ChatUI manual test raises correct answers from 54/112 (48.21%, per Table 1) to 61/112.

// does it hold up?

The eval read

The paper's own ID-vs-OOD design is well-suited to test a memorization hypothesis, but it never does — and the case is cleanest on ReClor, released in 2020, well before the roughly September-2021 training cutoffs commonly reported for ChatGPT and GPT-4, against AR-LSAT (2022) and the authors' own LogiQA 2.0 ood split (built from 2022 Civil Servant Exam items), which clearly postdate that cutoff. LogiQA 2.0 is a weaker anchor for the same argument: the paper evaluates the updated, enlarged LogiQA 2.0 test set (Section 2.1.1), and that update was itself published in 2023 — after the cutoff — so its pre/post-cutoff status is murkier than ReClor's, even though the original LogiQA dataset it enlarges dates to 2020. Either way, the paper attributes the sharp LogiQA 2.0/ReClor-vs-AR-LSAT/ood gap purely to "out-of-distribution" difficulty and never raises benchmark memorization as a candidate explanation, even though the pre/post-cutoff contrast sitting in its own tables — clean on ReClor, murkier on LogiQA 2.0 — is exactly the evidence that would let it test one.

There is also one genuine internal inconsistency worth flagging precisely: Section 3.1.1 states that ChatGPT's LogiQA 2.0 ood accuracy (38.44%) is "still lower than that of RoBERTa base" — but RoBERTa's own OOD score, in the same Table 1, is 33.22%; ChatGPT is actually higher, not lower. (Two smaller numeral slips exist elsewhere in the prose — 53.37% is printed where Table 1 reads 52.37% for ChatGPT on LogiQA 2.0 test, and 42.31% is printed where Table 2 reads 42.13% for ChatGPT on HELP — neither affects any of the paper's other directional claims, but the OOD sentence does get the comparison backwards.)

The GPT-4 ChatUI numbers come from small, hand-tested subsamples (85–112 items, against full test sets of 230–1,594) run through a consumer chat interface with no stated decoding settings, sitting in the same table rows as the programmatic GPT-4 API numbers over the complete test sets — without a caveat distinguishing the two result types' reliability. And stacking a fully-supervised, per-dataset fine-tuned RoBERTa-base against zero/one-shot prompted ChatGPT and GPT-4 is informative about "does the model already know this" but isn't a controlled test of reasoning ability in isolation, since RoBERTa never sees the OOD split's underlying question style during fine-tuning either.

Two further wrinkles sit in the method itself. The paper's own multi-choice prompt template (Appendix A, mirrored in Figure 1 above) instructs the model to answer with one of four labels, A–D, and nothing in the paper describes an adapted five-label version for AR-LSAT, whose own questions have five options (Section 2.1.3) — so the reported AR-LSAT accuracy may reflect a genuinely five-way task being scored through a four-way answer key. And for HELP, the paper evaluates on the train split rather than a held-out test or dev split (Section 2.1.2), an unusual choice given the paper's own claim to be measuring generalization.

baselines

RoBERTa-base fine-tuned per dataset for 5 epochs is the sole model baseline — a fully-supervised, task-specific model stacked against zero/one-shot prompting, with no larger or instruction-tuned open baseline reported for comparison against the two closed-API models.

contamination

Not discussed anywhere in the paper, despite ReClor (2020, clearly pre-cutoff) and AR-LSAT/the OOD split (2022, clearly post-cutoff) bracketing the models' training cutoffs — LogiQA 2.0's own 2023 update date makes it a murkier pre-cutoff comparator, but the clean ReClor contrast alone is the setup needed to test a memorization hypothesis, left untested.

what's missed

No confidence intervals or significance tests anywhere, so small deltas (e.g. ChatGPT's 38.44% vs RoBERTa's 33.22% on a 1,354-item OOD set) carry no sense of noise; the small hand-tested GPT-4 ChatUI subsamples sit alongside full-test-set API numbers without a reliability flag.

would raise confidence

Re-running the released LogiEval harness against multiple model vendors/snapshots with fixed decoding settings, adding an explicit contamination probe (e.g. verbatim-recall prompting on LogiQA/ReClor items), and replacing the small ChatUI subsamples with full-test-set API runs throughout.

// bottom line

What holds up three years on is the released LogiEval harness and the ID-vs-OOD study design; what doesn't is the specific ChatGPT/GPT-4 scores, which belong to two long-superseded 2023-era model snapshots. The paper's framing of the OOD gap as pure "difficulty" rather than a possible memorization signal is the clearest missed opportunity, given that the pre-cutoff/post-cutoff split needed to test that was already built into its own experiment.

// takeaways

What to remember

  1. Prompted zero/one-shot, ChatGPT and GPT-4 beat a fine-tuned RoBERTa-base baseline on 9 of the 11 tested benchmarks (all but AR-LSAT for ChatGPT, and MNLI dev for both models) — but GPT-4's own AR-LSAT and OOD scores (33.48% / 58.49%) trail its LogiQA 2.0 test / ReClor scores (72.25% / 87.20%) by roughly 14 to 54 points, and both remain below reported human averages on those datasets.
  2. The out-of-distribution construction is the paper's most durable contribution: performance on well-known benchmarks (LogiQA, ReClor) doesn't predict performance on a split the authors built themselves (LogiQA 2.0 ood) or a more recently published one they incorporate (AR-LSAT, 2022) — a caution that outlives the specific 2023 model scores, even though the paper stops short of investigating contamination as the cause.
  3. The released LogiEval suite (github.com/csitfun/LogiEval) packages the ten reformatted multi-choice and NLI datasets from Tables 1–2 — LogiQA 2.0 (English and Chinese), ReClor, and AR-LSAT alongside the six NLI sets — into one prompt-based benchmark harness; the newly-built OOD split is released separately at github.com/csitfun/LogiQA2.0. Either way, it's reusable against any current LLM regardless of how ChatGPT and GPT-4 specifically scored on it back in early 2023.
