FaithDial: A Faithful Benchmark for Information-Seeking Dialogue

Rather than collecting a new knowledge-grounded dialogue corpus, the authors edit an old one: every hallucinated or uncooperative Wizard turn in Wizard of Wikipedia is rewritten by crowdworkers into a response faithful to the same knowledge snippet. The result is a curated benchmark — and a controlled A/B pair with WoW — for testing whether hallucination in dialogue systems is a data problem more than a modeling one.

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

Knowledge-grounded dialogue is supposed to work like this: a human or model "Wizard" is handed a knowledge snippet K and asked to answer a "Seeker's" questions using it. In practice, the gold-standard training corpora that teach this behavior are themselves unfaithful. The authors cite a companion study (Dziri et al., 2022a) auditing Wizard of Wikipedia (WoW), CMU-DoG, and TopicalChat and finding more than 60% of turns across these three corpora contain hallucination — the human Wizards who built the datasets routinely ignored the provided knowledge and answered from a fictitious persona instead. Table 1 of this paper reproduces the split by dataset: only 19.7% of WoW responses are full hallucinations, versus 61.4% for CMU-DoG and 46.8% for TopicalChat — which is why WoW, the least-corrupted of the three, is the one chosen as the editing backbone rather than the other two.

Worse, a model trained on this data doesn't just inherit the noise — it can amplify it: the authors note that GPT-2 fine-tuned on WoW hallucinates roughly 20% more often than the humans in the training data did. Two obvious fixes don't work well. Filtering out noisy examples (à la Zhang and Hashimoto, 2021) either breaks conversational cohesion or forces discarding whole dialogues wholesale, since a single turn can't always be excised cleanly from its context. And no existing metric or model-side trick addresses the root cause: the training signal itself rewards hallucination in roughly four out of five WoW Wizard turns. FaithDial's answer is a data-centric one — edit the corpus itself, turn by turn, until the training signal says the opposite of what it used to say.

// how it works

The method

The authors define what a faithful Wizard should be before touching any data: a bot that (1) is truthful — only states information attributable to K; (2) is conversational — uses natural phrasing of K, supports follow-up discussion, and prompts the Seeker's opinions; and (3) acknowledges ignorance when K doesn't cover the question, while still moving the conversation forward. Every edit in FaithDial is a crowdworker rewriting a WoW Wizard turn to satisfy those three rules.

Selecting the data to edit

WoW conversations are filtered to those where ground-truth knowledge was actually given (no personal-knowledge fallback) and to Seeker-initiated dialogues, then sampled: 44% of train (4,094 conversations), and 100% of validation (764) and test (791) — the smaller training sample is a deliberate budget choice, not an attempt at a large-scale corpus; the paper is explicit that its goal is "a high-quality faithful dialogue benchmark rather than...a large-scale dataset for training."

Crowdsourced editing

Workers (Amazon Mechanical Turk, US/Canada only) first identify, for each Wizard turn, its BEGIN attribution class (hallucination, partial hallucination, entailment/faithful, generic, uncooperative — the taxonomy from a companion paper, Dziri et al. 2022b) and its VRM speech act (disclosure, edification, question, acknowledgment, etc.). If a turn hallucinates, the worker removes the unsupported content and replaces it with a paraphrase of K — copying spans verbatim from K is explicitly disallowed, to force genuine paraphrase rather than extraction (a nod to the summarization-faithfulness literature: Ladhak et al. 2022, Lux et al. 2020, Goyal and Durrett 2021). If K can't answer the Seeker's question, the edit has the bot acknowledge ignorance rather than invent an answer. If a turn is faithful but uncooperative (e.g., a bare "That's nice"), the edit enriches it with grounded content instead. Because editing the Wizard sometimes leaves the following Seeker turn incoherent, workers also edit the Seeker's next line when needed to restore coherence — much more sparingly (this is a secondary repair, not the target of the annotation).

Quality control is deliberately heavy for a ~50K-turn corpus: a qualification test, a ~60-HIT pilot round with worker feedback and access revocation for poor performers, and — once the main task launched — a linguistics-major student manually reviewing about 10 HITs per worker per day, with repeated mistakes leading to a full ban. The result: only 10 workers were retained to produce the entire corpus. An automatic check additionally flags any response that copies a segment straight from K.

Overall, FaithDial totals 50,761 turns across 5,649 dialogues. Editing wasn't rare: 84.7% of Wizard turns (21,447 utterances) were touched, versus 28.1% of Seeker turns (7,172 utterances) — on average 3.8 Wizard turns per conversation were modified against just 1.2 Seeker turns, evidence the edits are surgical rather than a full conversation rewrite.

Validating the edits

Because there's no clean way to measure inter-annotator agreement on free-text edits themselves, the authors validate indirectly. Three new workers re-labeled the BEGIN and VRM categories on the same 500 responses used for the main annotation, giving Fleiss' κ of 0.75 (BEGIN) and 0.61 (VRM) — "substantial" agreement by Landis and Koch's (1977) scale, not nearly-perfect. Separately, three further new workers judged the faithfulness of the same 500 already-edited responses by majority vote; treating the original labels as correct, the majority vote reproduces them with F1 of 90% (BEGIN) and 81% (VRM). On that basis, the paper reports FaithDial is 94.4% faithful and 5.6% hallucinated (Figure 2a) — a validation that re-measures agreement on the annotation schema itself, not an independent faithfulness check against some ground truth outside the annotation pipeline.

The same BEGIN/VRM-labeled data doubles as FaithCritic, a training set for a hallucination-critic classifier that flags whether a generated utterance is faithful.

// what they found

The results

The clearest before/after comes from comparing the same taxonomy applied to WoW and to FaithDial. The paper's own large-scale audit of the entirety of WoW (distinct from Table 1's smaller, previously-published sample) finds it interspersed with hallucination: 71.4% of turns are hallucinated or partially hallucinated, and only 20.9% are faithful (Entailment). FaithDial inverts that: 94.4% Entailment, 5.6% hallucinated.

Faithfulness doesn't come at the cost of copy-pasting the knowledge snippet. Using Density and Coverage (Grusky et al., 2018) — Density measures how much of a response is copied verbatim from K, Coverage measures word overlap with K regardless of copying — FaithDial's responses cluster at much lower density than WoW's at comparable coverage:

Hallucination-critic transfer

A critic trained on FaithCritic reaches 86.5% in-domain accuracy, and transfers zero-shot to BEGIN at 71.6% — 10.5 points above the next-best zero-shot transfer to BEGIN (a critic trained on MNLI, at 61.1%), and well above critics trained on DECODE (58.8%) or DNLI (59.8%). (The paper's own abstract advertises this result as a gain of “12.8 F1 score on the BEGIN benchmark” — but Table 4 is captioned “Transfer results (accuracy),” the gap it reports is 10.5, and the string 12.8 appears nowhere else in the paper. The accuracy figure is the one that can be checked, so it is the one quoted here.) Interestingly, the MNLI-trained critic also transfers respectably to FaithCritic zero-shot (81.6%), suggesting general entailment training captures some of the same signal.

Dialogue generation

Seven systems (GPT-2, DialoGPT, DoHA, T5, plus T5 fitted with a CTRL-style controllable-generation head or with loss truncation, plus the authors' own T5+InfoNCE contrastive objective) are evaluated on the FaithDial test set across a Critic-hallucination score, the Q2 factual-consistency metric (both an F1 and an NLI variant), BERTScore and word-overlap F1 against the knowledge, and BLEU/ROUGE against the gold response. Six of the seven are each trained three ways — on WoW only, on FaithDial only, and on WoW-then-FaithDial; T5+InfoNCE is trained only the latter two ways, since — per Table 5's own caption — its contrastive objective needs faithful positive examples that WoW-only training doesn't supply.

Swapping WoW training data for FaithDial training data, holding the model fixed, is the single biggest lever in the table: T5's Critic-hallucination score drops from 46.5 to 4.3 (a 42.2-point drop) and its Q2-NLI score rises from 75.2 to 79.5 (+4.3) — though the authors flag in a footnote that T5-WoW's relatively high Q2-NLI score may itself be inflated, since Q2 is "not robust to partial hallucinations." Within the FaithDial-only block, the proposed T5+InfoNCE variant reaches the lowest Critic score of any model in that block (1.4) and, among the four T5-backbone variants (T5 59.2, T5-CTRL 62.2, T5-LossTruncation 59.1, T5-InfoNCE 55.8), the lowest word-overlap-with-knowledge F1 — matching the paper's own framing that it "achieves both faithfulness and abstractiveness." (GPT-2 and DialoGPT score even lower on that same F1 metric, 50.4 and 52.3, but they are far more prone to hallucination: Critic 5.8 and 5.6 versus InfoNCE's 1.4.) Initializing from a WoW checkpoint before fine-tuning on FaithDial (the hybrid setup) improves most models further across most metrics, with a marginal Critic regression only for GPT-2 and DialoGPT.

Human evaluation

1,600 responses (200 per model × 8 models) were each scored by 3 raters, agreeing at Krippendorff's α between 0.75 and 0.92 across the 7 rated qualities. T5-InfoNCE is the best model in every column of Table 7: 97.2% interpretable, 19.9% hallucination (lowest), and the top cooperativeness (3.79), abstractiveness (2.92), and engagingness (2.60) scores on a 1–4 scale. Comparing the same model (T5) trained on WoW versus FaithDial: hallucination in human eval falls from 55.8% to 23.2%, a 32.6-point drop.

Zero-shot transfer out of domain

The strongest evidence against "the model just memorized FaithDial's editing style" comes from testing T5-FaithDial, zero-shot, on TopicalChat, CMU-DoG (Zhou et al., 2018), and WoW, against a same-architecture T5 trained in-domain on each target. On TopicalChat: Critic drops 35.7 points (95.0→59.3), Q2-NLI gains 13.9 (53.2→67.1), and human-judged hallucination falls 30.4 points (71.4%→41.0%). On CMU-DoG: Critic drops 73.7 points (95.5→21.8) and human hallucination falls 20.0 points (68.4%→48.4%). On WoW: human hallucination falls 23.8 points (48.0%→24.2%). But FaithDial-trained doesn't win every cell in Table 8: knowledge word-overlap F1 on WoW actually decreases slightly (59.6→57.4) rather than rising, and out of domain the same table shows a real cost — T5-FaithDial is markedly less cooperative than the in-domain baseline on both TopicalChat (Coop. 3.07 vs. 3.53) and CMU-DoG (Coop. 3.29 vs. 3.43), and less engaging on TopicalChat specifically (Enga. 2.20 vs. 2.56), even as its abstractiveness rises in those same rows. The authors state this plainly: T5-FaithDial models tested on TopicalChat and CMU-DoG "enjoy a larger degree of abstractiveness than in-domain models but have lower scores of cooperativeness and engagingness."

// does it hold up?

The eval read

FaithDial's central move — editing rather than collecting — is efficient, but it means every claim of "faithfulness" ultimately traces back to a small, self-referential annotation pipeline: the same first author's own taxonomy (BEGIN, Dziri et al. 2022b) is used both to build the dataset and to certify it afterward.

baselines

Table 5's in-domain comparison (train on WoW or FaithDial, test on FaithDial's own test set) is the weakest evidence in the paper, because some of its automatic metrics are entangled with the benchmark itself: the Critic is trained on labels this same annotation project produced, and BLEU/ROUGE score against the gold response these same ten workers wrote. F1(u,K), Q2, and BERTScore are all scored against the Wikipedia knowledge snippet instead — not against FaithDial's own gold text — with Q2 (Honovich et al., 2021) and BERTScore (Zhang et al., 2020a) sourced from outside this project. The Critic is also never meta-validated against human judgment anywhere in the paper (Table 4 reports only its transfer accuracy on MNLI/BEGIN/FaithCritic), despite being a headline metric in both Table 5 and Table 8 — which makes the Critic the one headline metric that is itself trained on FAITHDIAL, and so the one that cannot independently certify FAITHDIAL-trained models, and the paper's strongest baseline comparison is really Table 8's zero-shot transfer to CMU-DoG/TopicalChat, where a model trained on FaithDial is tested against data it never touched.

contamination

Not leakage in the usual train/test sense, but a subtler circularity: FaithDial's "94.4% faithful" figure is validated by re-measuring inter-annotator agreement on the BEGIN/VRM labels (κ = 0.75 / 0.61 — "substantial," not "near-perfect," by the paper's own cited scale) and a majority-vote F1 against the original labels (90% / 81%), not by comparing against any faithfulness signal external to the annotation pipeline. And the entire ~50K-turn corpus was produced by just 10 retained AMT workers, all located in the US or Canada — a narrow, homogeneous annotator population for a benchmark whose whole premise is that human-provided labels can serve as ground truth.

what's missed

A model scoring well on FaithDial's own test set after fine-tuning on FaithDial's own training set is close to circular — it may just be reproducing this specific annotator pool's editing conventions. The out-of-domain transfer in Table 8 is where the paper actually earns its faithfulness claim, and the faithfulness numbers do hold up — but the same table shows what that costs: T5-FaithDial trades away cooperativeness (and, on TopicalChat, engagingness) relative to the in-domain baseline (TopicalChat Coop. 3.07 vs. 3.53, Enga. 2.20 vs. 2.56; CMU-DoG Coop. 3.29 vs. 3.43), and its knowledge word-overlap F1 on WoW itself slightly drops (57.4 vs. 59.6) rather than rises. The paper states this cost in one sentence and moves on; a page arguing the benchmark "holds up" should weigh it more than that. Table 9's abstractiveness-strategy breakdown, meanwhile, is five illustrative rows with frequencies (25.99% / 12.6% / 7.33% / 8.66% / 4.6%) drawn from a manual read of 150-or-200 responses — not a corpus-wide measurement — and its own caption ("200 responses") disagrees with the Appendix C text that introduces it ("we annotate manually 150 responses"), an internal inconsistency worth noting rather than silently resolving.

would raise confidence

An annotator-independent faithfulness audit (a separate lab, or a judge trained on data outside this pipeline) against a random FaithDial sample; a larger and more geographically diverse annotator pool than 10 US/Canada workers; a corpus-wide abstractiveness metric rather than five illustrative examples, so Table 9's strategy percentages could be checked against the full test set; and a fix to the 150-vs-200 discrepancy in how many responses that manual analysis actually covered.

// bottom line

The data-centric diagnosis is well-supported: three prior benchmarks (WoW, CMU-DoG, TopicalChat) really are heavily hallucinated by this taxonomy, and rewriting the training data — not the model — measurably reduces hallucination both in-domain and, more convincingly, zero-shot on data the model never saw. The main thing to keep in view is that "faithful" here means "faithful according to a taxonomy this same research group designed, applied by ten crowdworkers, and re-validated by measuring agreement on that same taxonomy" — a coherent and useful operationalization, but not an appeal to any faithfulness signal outside the paper's own annotation pipeline.

// takeaways

What to remember

  1. FaithDial is a curated edit of Wizard of Wikipedia, not an independent collection: 10 trained AMT workers rewrote 84.7% of Wizard turns (21,447 utterances) and 28.1% of Seeker turns (7,172) across 5,649 dialogues / 50,761 turns, turning WoW's own audited 20.9%-faithful rate into FaithDial's validated 94.4% faithful / 5.6% hallucinated.
  2. The fix is data, not architecture: holding the model fixed (T5) and swapping WoW training data for FaithDial training data drops Critic-flagged hallucination from 46.5% to 4.3% and human-judged hallucination from 55.8% to 23.2%; the paper's proposed InfoNCE contrastive objective pushes the Critic score to 1.4% (lowest of any model in the FaithDial block) while also posting the lowest word-overlap F1 among the four T5-backbone variants (55.8, vs. 59.1–62.2 for T5-LossTruncation/T5/T5-CTRL) — faithfulness without falling back on extractive copying, within that family.
  3. The gains mostly transfer zero-shot to CMU-DoG and TopicalChat (Table 8) — the paper's best answer to "did it just memorize FaithDial's own editing style?" — but not for free: word-overlap with knowledge on WoW itself slightly drops, and out of domain the FaithDial-trained model is measurably less cooperative (and, on TopicalChat, less engaging) than the in-domain baseline it replaces. Every faithfulness number, meanwhile, ultimately rests on a small (10-worker), self-referential annotation pipeline: the same taxonomy built FaithDial and later re-validated it.
