Hallucination of Multimodal Large Language Models: A Survey

A cause-traced taxonomy of why MLLMs describe things that aren't in the image — object category, attribute, and relation errors, mapped back to data, model, training, and inference sources — plus a catalogue of the benchmarks, metrics, and mitigation methods built against them.

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

Multimodal large language models (MLLMs) — LLMs given eyes, via a vision encoder and a cross-modal interface bolted onto a frozen or fine-tuned language backbone — inherited a text-generation failure mode along with the text-generation capability: they produce fluent descriptions that are inconsistent with the image or video actually in front of them. The paper is explicit that this isn't the same problem NLP already has a literature for. Text-only LLM hallucination splits into factuality (contradicts real-world facts) and faithfulness (contradicts the input context) — the two-way split the paper attributes to a prior survey it cites [65]: Huang et al.'s 2023 LLM-hallucination survey (arXiv:2311.05232), not Ji et al.'s NLG hallucination survey, which uses a different intrinsic/extrinsic split and isn't in this paper's bibliography at all. MLLM hallucination is instead a cross-modal consistency problem: the generated text has to answer to a second, non-linguistic modality, and the paper argues "studies in LLMs cannot be seemingly transferred to MLLMs" as a result.

The paper positions itself against exactly one prior attempt at this specific survey — Liu et al.'s "A survey on hallucination in large vision-language models" (arXiv:2402.00253, cited as a "concurrent work") — which it says is architecture-scoped and narrower in both taxonomy depth and literature coverage. This paper's stated differentiator is a "layered and granular classification" that traces hallucination back through four candidate origin points (data, model, training, inference) rather than organizing around specific model families, and that ties its mitigation-method review directly to that same cause taxonomy rather than treating causes and fixes as separate literatures.

The taxonomy

// which version this page follows

This is a survey with an unusually large version gap: arXiv hosts v1 (posted 29 Apr 2024, 30 pages) and v2 (posted 1 Apr 2025, 40 pages) at the same id, and source ↗ above resolves to whichever is current — v2. The revision is not cosmetic: v2's bibliography carries 19 references published in 2025, and its body runs roughly 37% longer by character count (about 176k vs. 129k characters of extracted text). This page follows v2 throughout, and every count-style claim below ("N benchmarks", "N mitigation methods") is a v2 count. The gap between v1 and v2 is additive rather than corrective — the five reference ids present only in v1 (2308.06394, 2312.01701, 2312.14238, 2402.09717, 2402.15721) were simply dropped, not replaced with revised numbers, and nothing v1 stated numerically is contradicted in v2. The node's date stays at v1's post month (2024-04, the arXiv convention this site uses for timeline placement), but the content described here — including the "Comparison with existing surveys" framing and the taxonomy figure — is the 2025 revision.

The organizing artifact is Figure 1, reproduced below: a three-branch tree covering causes, metrics/benchmarks, and mitigation, each subdivided further. Section 3 (causes) assigns hallucination to one of four stages: data (insufficient volume, noisy or non-diverse training data, and statistical biases toward frequent objects or co-occurring object pairs), model (a vision encoder that loses information or carries feature bias, a language model falling back on parametric/world knowledge over what the image shows, or an inferior cross-modal interface alignment), training (supervision signal choices — sequence-level, visual, or human-feedback supervision), and inference (decoding-time issues: visual attention deficiency, where the model's attention drifts off the relevant image region as generation proceeds, and "trap visual tokens" — a small subset of visual tokens, sensitive to encoder noise, that disproportionately distort the output).

Section 2 first fixes vocabulary. An MLLM's abstracted pipeline — vision input and text input, through a vision model/tokenizer, into the LLM, out through a decoding strategy — is shown in Figure 2; the paper distinguishes two integration styles, learnable-query interfaces (Q-Former, as in MiniGPT-4 and InstructBLIP) versus projection-layer interfaces (a linear or MLP layer, as in LLaVA and Shikra), plus a smaller class of end-to-end models trained from scratch without a separate pretrained vision encoder (Fuyu-8B, Gemini). Training for interface-based MLLMs is described as two-stage: cross-modal pretraining with both unimodal backbones frozen, then instruction tuning on visual-content–instruction–response triples.

Within object hallucination specifically — the paper's primary focus, since "objects are central to research in computer vision" — the survey adopts a three-way empirical split: category (a nonexistent or wrong object class in the description), attribute (the right object, wrong property — color, shape, material, count, action), and relation (correct objects and attributes, wrong relationship between them — spatial position, interaction). The paper folds counting and "object event" into the attribute bucket rather than treating them as separate categories, a scoping choice it states explicitly rather than leaving implicit. Figure 3 works a single example: asked to describe a park scene, an MLLM writes "there are some benches and a fence" and "there are some clouds" (category — neither is in the image), "pink blossoms" (attribute — the tree's blossoms are present but not pink), and in a follow-up turn, "the other people in the picture are standing around her, watching what she is doing" (relation — the people-to-subject interaction described doesn't hold).

// what they found

The benchmark and mitigation landscape

Section 4 surveys hallucination metrics (CHAIR — the original pre-MLLM object-hallucination metric from image captioning, EMNLP'18; POPE's yes/no-question protocol with Accuracy/Precision/Recall/F1; LLM-judge-based metrics such as GAVIE, HaELM, and HallusionBench's own scoring; and others like FaithScore and AMBER's compound score) and hallucination benchmarks, split by task type into discriminative (POPE, MME, MMBench) and generative (MMHal-Bench, AMBER). Table 1 tabulates the most relevant object-hallucination benchmarks in arXiv-chronological order — benchmark, venue, underlying data source, size, task type, metric, and which of category/attribute/relation/other hallucination types each one targets. Spot examples: CHAIR (EMNLP'18, MSCOCO, 5,000 instances, generative) and POPE (EMNLP'23, MSCOCO, 3,000 instances, discriminative) both cover category hallucination only; MME (arXiv'23, MSCOCO, 1,457 instances) additionally covers attribute; MMBench (ECCV'24, unspecified data source, 3,217 instances) is the first in the table to cover all of category, attribute, and relation.

Section 4 also runs a head-to-head compilation across "mainstream MLLMs" — Table 2 for generative benchmarks, Table 3 for discriminative ones. Both tables carry an explicit caption disclaimer: "the numbers come from the original papers of these benchmarks." This survey ran no evaluations itself; every cell is a number reported by that benchmark's own paper, under whatever prompt, decoding setting, and checkpoint that paper happened to use. Read narrowly and only within the columns a given model actually has values for, one legal comparison Table 2 supports: of the eight models it reports both a CHAIR (on AMBER, lower-is-better) and an AMBER Score (higher-is-better) for, GPT-4V posts the best value in both columns — CHAIR 4.3 versus the next-best 7.9 (CogVLM), and AMBER Score 92.7 versus the next-best 86.2 (InstructBLIP) and 86.1 (CogVLM). That comparison does not extend to the FaithScore or Hal-Eval columns of the same table, where GPT-4V has no reported value at all — most rows in both tables are dense with dashes, because each benchmark's paper only ever evaluated the models it happened to have access to.

Table 3's discriminative comparison is considerably larger — 39 model rows against up to 12 metric columns spanning MME's four sub-scores, three POPE splits, RAH-Bench, AMBER's discriminative F1 and overall score, and Hal-Eval's in-/out-of-domain Event-F1 — and its final eight rows (OPERA, VCD, M3ID, RITUAL, DeGF, SID, CCA, DAC) are not independent base models at all but mitigation methods layered on the same LLaVA-1.5 backbone — seven decoding-time interventions plus CCA, which the survey itself files as a training-time positional-alignment strategy (§5.3.1) — sitting in the identical column set as the base models above them. Section 5 then works through mitigation in the same four-way structure as the causes: data-side fixes (negative data via LRV-Instruction, counterfactual data via HalluciDoctor, reasoning data via REVERIE, cleaned data via ReCaption/EOS), model-side fixes (higher input resolution as in LLaVA-1.5 and InternVL, versatile vision encoders, dedicated modules like HallE-Switch, a trained "switch" that controls how much parametric knowledge enters a detailed caption), training-side fixes (auxiliary visual/contrastive supervision; automatic-metric-based, RLAIF-based, and RLHF-based reinforcement learning, including LLaVA-RLHF and RLHF-V), and inference-side fixes (contrastive/guided decoding, visual-token amplification, visual prompting, retrieval-augmented generation, ensembling, and post-hoc correction pipelines like Woodpecker and Volcano).

// does it hold up?

The eval read

The obvious critique of a fast-moving-field survey — "already dated by the time you read it" — doesn't land cleanly here, because the authors themselves refreshed it: v2 was posted a full eleven months after v1 and added 19 references published in 2025. The sharper question is what that refresh actually updated. It didn't touch the comparison tables' model roster in any way that reaches the frontier: Table 3's newest entries are Qwen-VL2, DeepSeek-VL2, Intern-VL2, and LLaVA-One-Vision — all 2024-vintage open-weight models — plus eight LLaVA-1.5-backboned mitigation methods. GPT-4o and Claude never appear anywhere in the paper's 40 pages, and Gemini is cited exactly once — a one-line architecture-category mention in Section 2, as an example of end-to-end training, with no benchmark numbers attached beyond its own bibliography entry. None of the three shows up in Table 2 or Table 3. GPT-4V — OpenAI's late-2023 vision model — remains the only proprietary frontier system in either table. So the survey's bibliography is current to April 2025, but its own "mainstream MLLMs" comparison is not; a reader looking to Tables 2–3 for how today's frontier models stack up on hallucination will not find them there.

The stronger and more structural criticism is the one the paper's own captions hand you: Tables 2 and 3 read like controlled comparisons — same rows, same columns, one score per cell — but they are compilations of numbers pulled from dozens of separate papers, each evaluated under that paper's own prompt template, decoding strategy (greedy vs. sampling vs. beam search — Figure 2 lists all three as live options), and model checkpoint. A model's CHAIR score and its HallusionBench All-Acc score in the same row were very likely produced by two different research groups running two different harnesses months apart. The survey partially owns this: Section 6.4 ("Establishing Standardized Benchmarks") states outright that "there is a variety of evaluation benchmarks, but a lack of unified standards," and flags POPE's binary yes/no protocol as misaligned with how people actually use MLLMs. But that self-critique lives in the future-directions section, three sections after the comparison tables, and nothing in Section 4 itself flags to a reader skimming Table 2 that the column-to-column comparison it invites is not apples-to-apples.

On taxonomy load-bearing-ness: the four-way cause split (data/model/training/inference) does real organizing work rather than being decorative, because Section 5's mitigation review is structured to mirror it one-for-one — data causes get data fixes, inference causes get inference fixes. That symmetry is a genuine, checkable claim (Figure 1 stacks both branches as the top and bottom of the same tree), and it's the paper's stated point of differentiation from the "concurrent" 2024 survey it cites. What the taxonomy does not do is quantify which cause is empirically dominant, or how much of the mitigation literature's progress against, say, "trap visual tokens" versus "insufficient data" actually reduces hallucination on a controlled benchmark — the survey catalogues method-to-cause pairings, it doesn't rank causes by tractability or measured impact, so the taxonomy's explanatory power is structural (this fix targets this cause) rather than predictive (this cause is worth fixing first).

On whether "hallucination" is doing too much work as a single term: the survey itself splits object hallucination into category/attribute/relation and separately gestures at "detailed descriptions" and open-ended generative unfaithfulness as related but distinct problems (the "open question" label attached to detailed-description hallucination in Figure 1 is the paper's own admission that this sub-case isn't fully resolved by the rest of the taxonomy). That's a narrower and more disciplined scope than Ji et al.'s intrinsic/extrinsic split for text-only NLG hallucination, which spans open-domain dialogue, summarization, and translation under one umbrella — the multimodal survey's tighter focus on object-level cross-modal consistency is arguably a strength, buying it a cleaner category system at the cost of not addressing longer-form generative hallucination (a full free-form caption's overall coherence or narrative fabrication) with the same granularity it brings to per-object errors.

baselines

Not applicable in the usual sense — this is a survey, not an experiment. The closer question is whether its comparison tables' model roster is current, and by the survey's own April-2025 refresh date, it is not: no GPT-4o/Claude/Gemini scores anywhere, GPT-4V (Nov 2023) remains the newest proprietary entry.

contamination

Doesn't apply to a survey directly, but the survey inherits whatever contamination exists in the underlying benchmark papers it tabulates without auditing — Table 1's "Underlying Data Source" column does record that several benchmarks (CHAIR, POPE, MME, ROPE, and others) share MSCOCO as source imagery, but neither the table nor the surrounding text draws any contamination/overlap conclusion from that shared provenance, and Tables 2–3 carry no provenance column at all, so a reader can't tell from the comparison tables whether a model's train/eval overlap differs across rows.

what's missed

Tables 2–3 read as controlled comparisons but are compilations under different prompts/decoding/checkpoints per source paper — the survey's own caption says so, and Section 6.4 admits the field lacks unified standards, but that caveat isn't surfaced next to the tables themselves.

would raise confidence

A single column in Tables 2/3 marking which cells share an identical evaluation harness (same prompt template, same decoding config) versus which are cross-paper compilations — or a re-run of a small model subset under one fixed protocol — would let a reader tell a real ranking from an artifact of who got evaluated by whom.

// bottom line

The taxonomy is the real contribution here, and it holds together: a four-way cause split that's mirrored one-for-one by a four-way mitigation review, and a disciplined three-way object-hallucination category system that Figure 3 demonstrates cleanly. The comparison tables are the weaker part of the package — not because the numbers are wrong, but because presenting cross-paper compilations in a same-row, same-column format invites exactly the kind of controlled-comparison reading the paper's own caption disclaims, and an eleven-month refresh that added 19 references didn't extend that comparison past 2023-era GPT-4V on the proprietary side.

// takeaways

What to remember

  1. MLLM hallucination is framed as a cross-modal consistency problem, distinct from text-only LLM hallucination's factuality/faithfulness split, and the survey traces it to one of four stages: data, model, training, or inference — with mitigation methods reviewed in the same four-way structure.
  2. Object hallucination — the survey's primary focus — splits into category (wrong/nonexistent object class), attribute (right object, wrong property), and relation (right objects and properties, wrong relationship); Figure 3 demonstrates all three on one image.
  3. Tables 2 and 3's "mainstream MLLM" comparisons are compilations from each benchmark's own paper, not a controlled experiment the survey ran — the caption says so directly — and despite an April-2025 (v2) refresh, no GPT-4o/Claude/Gemini scores appear anywhere in either table.
