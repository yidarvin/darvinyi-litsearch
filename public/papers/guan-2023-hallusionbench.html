HallusionBench: An Advanced Diagnostic Suite for Entangled Language Hallucination and Visual Illusion in Large Vision-Language Models

A 346-image, 1129-question diagnostic suite built from control pairs — a hand-edited twin, the untouched original, or no image at all — so a fixed decision tree — not a human's judgment call — can classify a wrong answer as language hallucination (trusting prior knowledge over the picture) or visual illusion (misreading the picture itself). GPT-4V leads all 15 tested models at a 31.42% question-pair accuracy; every one of the 12 open-source models tested scores below the 15.60% random-chance baseline.

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

By late 2023, LVLM hallucination benchmarks had converged on one narrow failure: object-existence hallucination in image captions or yes/no probes (POPE, M-HalDetect, GAVIE — all listed in the paper's own Table 1). Each collapses to a single accuracy or F1 number over whether an object is "present." That framing conflates two mechanistically different failures the paper's introduction names directly: Language Hallucination, where "the model makes false prior assumptions about the input and image context based on its parametric memory" and answers as if it never looked at the picture, and Visual Illusion, "the misinterpretation of accurate visual information" — the model does attend to the image but reads it wrong. A single accuracy score over a static image set can't tell which one produced a given wrong answer, because there's no counterfactual: you never see what the model would have said about a slightly different picture, or about no picture at all.

A separate, smaller literature had studied classical optical illusions in VLMs (the paper cites Grounding Visual Illusions in Language directly), but only that one axis, on a handful of famous illusions. No existing benchmark, per Table 1's eight-way comparison against Lynx-Bench, SciGraphQA, MathVista, MME, POPE, M-HalDetect, GAVIE, and Bingo, combined both axes with a construction that could actually attribute a failure to one cause or the other. HallusionBench's gap-filling move is to build every question as a control pair: the same question (or a same-topic question) asked against an original image, a human-edited version of it, and sometimes no image at all — spanning far more topics (food, math, geometry, statistics, geography, sports, cartoons, famous illusions, movies, memes) and formats (logos, posters, charts, tables, maps, OCR text, consecutive video frames) than any of the eight comparison benchmarks — only one of which, Lynx-Bench, also mixes images and video, and even Lynx-Bench reports zero human-edited images and no control pairs (✗), unlike HallusionBench's 181 edited images and ✓.

// how it works

The method

HallusionBench sorts every question into one of two families. Visual Dependent (VD) questions have no answer without the image at all — "Is the right orange circle the same size as the left orange circle?" is meaningless without a picture. Visual Supplement (VS) questions are answerable from parametric knowledge alone, and the image only confirms or corrects it — "Is New Mexico state larger than Texas state?" has a fixed factual answer a model may already know. The dataset holds 591 VD questions (illusion, math, video, poster, and other figures) and 538 VS questions (chart, table, map, OCR), for 1129 questions total over 346 images (165 original, 181 human-edited — 52% of the image pool). By question rather than by image, the split is 16% with no visual input at all, 39% asked against the unedited original, and 45% asked against a hand-edited twin (178/447/504 of 1129, Fig. 2).

Every image belongs to a small family: an original I₍ᵢ,₀₎, one or more edited variants I′ᵢ (image flipping, order reversal, masking, character/object/color editing), and — for VS questions only — an empty "no visual" placeholder I₀. A question is put to every image in that family, and all responses are parsed by GPT-4 into "yes," "no," or "uncertain" and scored against the ground-truth label with a text-only GPT-4 judge: the judge sees only the question, the reference answer, and the model's text response (never the image), and outputs Correct / Incorrect / Uncertain. Because the judge itself has sampling variance even at temperature 0, the authors run it three times per response and average. The paper validates this judge against human labels, but only for two of the fifteen evaluated models (GPT-4V and LLaVA-1.5), reporting the gap between "Human" and "GPT4-Assisted" rows in Table 2 as evidence of alignment (differences of roughly 0.2–4.3 accuracy points across the five metrics for those two models).

The attribution mechanism is a fixed decision tree, not a judgment call: given the correctness of the two questions in a control pair and whether their answers match or differ, the tree routes every incorrect response to exactly one of three labels — Language Hallucination, Visual Illusion, or Mixed/Uncertain — following rules the paper states explicitly (§4.3.4): a wrong answer to a question that does have visual input is provisionally Visual Illusion; a wrong-but-certain answer to a no-image VS question is Language Hallucination; and a response that stays identical after the image is edited — i.e. the model's answer tracks its own prior instead of the new picture — is also scored as Language Hallucination, "contrary to common sense."

Three families of metrics come out of this construction. Correctness is reported three ways: Question Pair Accuracy (qAcc, full credit only if a question and its edited-image twin are both answered correctly), Figure Accuracy (fAcc, full credit for a given image only if every question asked against that specific image is correct, averaged over all images), and All/Easy/Hard Accuracy (aAcc, per-question accuracy, split by whether the image was edited). Yes/No Bias is measured independently of correctness with two diagnostics borrowed in spirit from POPE's finding that some LVLMs answer "yes" regardless of the question: Yes Percentage Difference (how far a model's yes-rate departs from the ground truth's own yes-rate) and False Positive Ratio (what fraction of a model's *wrong* answers were "yes").

dy = Σ 1[M(I,q)="yes"] − Σ 1[y(I,q)="yes"]|V| // Yes Percentage Difference, range [−1, 1] — a model is more "yes"-biased as |dy| → 1; note the ground truth's own yes-rate is subtracted out, so dy is a bias measure relative to the answer key, not raw yes-frequency

Finally, the Diagnostic Test applies the decision tree above to break every model's wrong answers into %Language Hallucination / %Visual Illusion / %Mixed. Because qAcc and fAcc require every member of a control pair (or image family) to be correct, a model that just answers "yes" to everything cannot pass a pair whose two ground-truth labels differ — a structural defense against constant-answer inflation that a single yes/no accuracy number (like POPE's) doesn't have by itself, though HallusionBench doesn't balance its own ground-truth yes/no ratio the way POPE explicitly does for its three sampling strategies; the paper never states what that ratio is.

// what they found

The results

Fifteen models were evaluated (GPT-4V, LLaVA-1.5, Gemini Pro Vision, Claude 3, MiniGPT4, MiniGPT5, GIT, InstructBLIP, Qwen-VL, mPLUG-Owl-v1, mPLUG-Owl-v2, LRV-Instruction, BLIP2, BLIP2-T5, Open-Flamingo) plus a Random Chance baseline, on the text-only GPT-4 judge (Table 2). GPT-4V leads the qAcc column at 31.42% under human evaluation (28.79% under the GPT-4 judge) — the number the abstract highlights. The next-best model on this metric is Claude 3 at 21.76% qAcc (Claude 3 also places second on fAcc, Easy aAcc, and All aAcc, though not on Hard aAcc — see below); every one of the other 12 open-source models tested falls below Random Chance's own qAcc of 15.60% (LLaVA-1.5 9.45–10.55%, BLIP2-T5 15.16%, Qwen-VL 5.93%, Open-Flamingo 6.37%, MiniGPT5 10.55%, MiniGPT4 8.79%, InstructBLIP 9.45%, BLIP2 5.05%, mPLUG-Owl-v2 13.85%, mPLUG-Owl-v1 9.45%, LRV-Instruction 8.79%, GIT 5.27%) — a sweep the paper itself doesn't state in these terms, though it does call out two of these twelve (Qwen-VL, MiniGPT4) by name as sub-random.

GPT-4V's lead does not hold on every metric: on Hard Accuracy — accuracy restricted to the edited-image questions — several smaller open models score higher than GPT-4V's 37.67–38.37%, including InstructBLIP (45.12%), BLIP2-T5 (43.49%), and BLIP2 (40.70%); even Random Chance's Hard Accuracy (39.06%) edges GPT-4V out. On the Hard Accuracy column itself, the paper's own explanation is brief — "the poor accuracy demonstrates the challenges of our image manipulations for GPT-4V and other open-source LVLMs" (§5.2) — though elsewhere, discussing specific illusion case studies (Fig. 5), it suggests a mechanism that plausibly extends to the Hard column too: "the reason behind this might be that GPT-4V tends to generate answers based on its parametric memory instead of analyzing the images."

Breaking wrong answers down by cause (Table 3): GPT-4V's failures are Visual-Illusion-dominant, not evenly split — roughly 46% Visual Illusion (45.66–46.17%) against roughly 22% Language Hallucination (21.86–22.19%), a skew of about 2:1, with the remaining ~32% Mixed. LLaVA-1.5 skews the same direction, more sharply (25.63–26.71% LH, 51.09–51.42% VI). The extremes are more informative: Qwen-VL's failures are almost entirely Visual Illusion (88.06%, only 0.87% Language Hallucination) — a pattern LRV-Instruction (76.47% VI, 4.49% LH) and mPLUG-Owl-v1 (78.36% VI, 3.95% LH) both share almost exactly, all three models failing mostly by misreading the picture rather than overriding it with prior knowledge. At the other extreme, BLIP2-T5 has the most balanced split of any model in the table — 41.64% Language Hallucination against 40.44% Visual Illusion, a 1.2-point gap, next-closest is BLIP2 at 4.3 points and every other model is roughly 18 points or more apart (Open-Flamingo the closest of the rest, at 17.99) — a genuinely mixed failure profile rather than one dominant cause, unlike GPT-4V, LLaVA-1.5, Qwen-VL, LRV-Instruction, and mPLUG-Owl-v1 above.

// does it hold up?

The eval read

The decision-tree attribution is genuinely checkable — it's a deterministic function of two correctness labels and an agreement flag, stated explicitly in §4.3.4 and diagrammed twice (Fig. 3 and Fig. 6) — which is more transparent than most "failure taxonomy" papers that classify errors by eyeballing them. But its output is only as trustworthy as the correctness labels feeding it, and those come from a GPT-4 text judge validated against human annotation for two of the fifteen evaluated models. The authors themselves note "outputs of GPT-4 still exist variance, although the temperature is set as 0," which is why they average three judge calls — a tacit admission that the underlying labels the entire decision tree runs on are noisy, for a benchmark whose central contribution is attributing that noise to a specific cause.

Three headline claims on the page don't fully check out against the paper's own tables, all three checkable from the numbers quoted above without leaving the PDF:

  • Table 1's uniqueness claim is narrower than it reads. The caption's "first and the only" is scoped by its own final clause — "that focuses on control-group analysis by carefully editing each image in the database manually." Table 1 does mark Bingo with the identical ✓ in the "Control Pair?" column, so the "only" rests entirely on that manual-image-editing distinction — but Bingo's "#H-Edited Img." cell reads N/A, which the same caption defines as "the information is not provided," so the table itself neither confirms nor refutes whether Bingo's images are hand-edited; it only leaves the question open. (§3.4 states the paper's own narrower scoping directly: "We are also the first work to include human-edited images to assess the robustness of current LVLMs.") The honest reading: the uniqueness claim is unverified by Table 1 rather than contradicted by it — but Bingo sharing the identical ✓ under "Control Pair?" means "the only" is leaning on a qualifier the paper's own comparison table can't confirm.
  • The abstract's "below 16%" claim. "Notably, all other evaluated models achieve accuracy below 16%" is false against the paper's own Table 2: Claude 3 scores 21.76% qAcc. This isn't inherited from the original Oct 2023 preprint — arXiv v1's abstract carries no leaderboard numbers at all (no "31.42%," no "15 different models," no "below 16%" sentence). The line was written later, evidently for a model line-up that didn't yet include Claude 3 (released Mar 2024), and was never revised once Claude 3's row was added to Table 2.
  • The "outperform Random Choice" claim. §5.2 states "GPT-4V, BLIP2-T5, and mPLUG-Owl-v2 outperform Random Choice in both question pair accuracy, figure pair accuracy, and question level accuracy." On qAcc specifically, Random Chance scores 15.60% while BLIP2-T5 scores 15.16% and mPLUG-Owl-v2 scores 13.85% — both below, not above, the baseline the sentence says they beat. (Both do clear Random Chance on fAcc and aAcc, so the error is scoped to one metric of three, not the whole sentence.)

None of these are transcription slips on the reader's end — all three are checkable straight from values the paper itself prints. Two are direct contradictions (the abstract's "below 16%" against Claude 3's row; §5.2's "outperform Random Choice" against BLIP2-T5 and mPLUG-Owl-v2's own qAcc); the Table 1 uniqueness claim is narrower — a qualifier the paper's own comparison table can't verify, rather than an outright contradiction. Even so, the construction's real strength — deterministic, auditable failure attribution — is undercut by a paper that didn't fully audit two of its own summary sentences against its own tables.

baselines

15 models spanning three tiers (GPT-4V/Claude 3/Gemini Pro Vision closed-source; LLaVA-1.5/InstructBLIP/Qwen-VL/mPLUG-Owl/BLIP2 family open-source instruction-tuned; GIT/Open-Flamingo smaller pre-instruction baselines) plus Random Chance — a reasonably current and broad snapshot for late-2023/early-2024, though 13 of the 15 are evaluated only by the GPT-4 judge, never cross-checked against human labels.

contamination

Not directly addressed. The images are hand-sourced/hand-edited from the internet by the authors' team rather than drawn from a held-out or newly-collected corpus with documented provenance, and no near-duplicate or web-crawl-overlap check is reported for the 165 original images.

what's missed

A raw accuracy number here isn't a general VQA-competence score — this is a diagnostic suite explicitly built to surface failure, by the paper's own framing: "the focus of our benchmark is on hallucination and illusion, not the span of knowledge" (§4.2), and its Visual-Supplement construction guidelines ask directly whether "the model (still) hallucinate[s] about the images" (§3.1.2) rather than how well it performs in general. So GPT-4V's 31.42% should be read as "how often it survives a control pair built to elicit exactly this failure," not "how often it answers visual questions correctly" in general. The paper frames itself this way, but the headline number travels without that context.

would raise confidence

Human validation of the GPT-4 judge across more than 2 of 15 models; a stated inter-annotator agreement or annotator count/qualification for the "human experts" who wrote and edited the questions (never specified beyond that phrase); and a reconciliation pass fixing the two page-internal contradictions above (and a qualifier on the Table 1 uniqueness claim), which as written could mislead a reader who trusts the prose over the tables.

// bottom line

The taxonomy and decision-tree attribution mechanism are the paper's real contribution — a mechanically checkable way to say why a VLM got a question wrong, not just that it did — and the finding that every open-source model tested falls below random chance on question-pair accuracy is a genuinely damning, well-supported result. But the paper's own summary sentences don't all check out against its own tables: the abstract's "below 16%" and §5.2's "beats Random Choice" lines are direct contradictions of Table 2, and Table 1's "first and the only" uniqueness claim leans on a manual-image-editing qualifier the table itself leaves unverified for its closest comparator, Bingo — a bad look for a benchmark whose whole pitch is rigor in attributing what a number means.

// takeaways

What to remember

  1. HallusionBench pairs 1,129 hand-crafted yes/no questions across 346 images (181 human-edited) and uses a fixed decision tree — not a judgment call — to sort every wrong answer into Language Hallucination (over-trusting prior knowledge) or Visual Illusion (misreading the image). Of 15 models tested, GPT-4V leads at 31.42% question-pair accuracy, and every one of the 12 open-source models tested scores below the 15.60% random-chance baseline.
  2. Read the leaderboard as a diagnostic-difficulty score, not a general-capability score: the benchmark is deliberately built to pit prior knowledge against altered images, and GPT-4V's edge disappears entirely on Hard (edited-image-only) accuracy, where several smaller open models — and even Random Chance — score higher.
  3. Check a benchmark paper's tables before trusting its prose: the abstract's "below 16%" line is broken by Claude 3's 21.76% qAcc, the §5.2 claim that BLIP2-T5 and mPLUG-Owl-v2 "outperform Random Choice" on question-pair accuracy is broken by their own 15.16% and 13.85% against Random Chance's 15.60%, and Table 1's "first and only" control-pair claim leans on a qualifier (manual image editing) the table's own N/A cell for its closest comparator, Bingo, leaves unverified.
