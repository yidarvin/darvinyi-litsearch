Evaluating Real-World Robot Manipulation Policies in Simulation

SIMPLER swaps expensive, hard-to-reproduce real-robot trials for a suite of simulated Gym environments whose measured success rates track real-world success rates closely enough to serve as a scalable proxy — validated across seven distinct policy checkpoints spanning two robot embodiments (six on Google Robot tasks, plus Octo-Small added for the WidowX + BridgeData V2 setup) over the paper’s stated “∼1500 evaluation episodes”.

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

Generalist robot manipulation policies — RT-1, RT-2, Octo, and the growing family of models trained on large cross-embodiment datasets — are meant to be "policies that can perform a wide range of tasks across many environments and even robot embodiments." Characterizing whether one actually does that means running it on a wide sweep of tasks and conditions, and that sweep has to happen on a real robot, with a real human resetting the scene between trials. As the authors put it, "real-world evaluation of such policies is not scalable and faces reproducibility challenges, which are likely to worsen as policies broaden the spectrum of tasks they can perform." Two robots running nominally the same real-world evaluation can still produce different numbers, because lighting, camera mounting, object instances, and operator habits are never quite identical.

The robotics community has tried to standardize real-world evaluation before — shared object sets like YCB, fixed competition sites (the Amazon Picking Challenge, the DARPA Robotics Challenges), and shared-hardware platforms like TOTO and RB2 — but every one of these still requires physical infrastructure and ongoing maintenance, which is exactly what doesn't scale as the number of policies, tasks, and labs grows.

Pure-simulation benchmarks (ManiSkill, RLBench, CALVIN, Habitat, BEHAVIOR, robosuite, Meta-World, and others) sidestep the reproducibility problem, but almost all of them both train and evaluate in simulation — so a good score doesn't tell you how a policy trained on real robot data will actually behave once it's deployed on hardware. The inverse question — given a policy already trained on real data, can a simulator tell you how it will do in the real world? — had barely been asked for manipulation. It had been studied for navigation (Kadian et al.'s Sim2Real Predictivity work, RoboThor), where Kadian et al. found the sim-to-real gap can come from a simulator's dynamics letting policies exploit simulator-specific quirks, and Deitke et al. showed visual mismatches alone can throw off even artist-crafted assets. But manipulation's "tight interaction loop between policy and environment" — dynamic contact, precise grasp geometry, articulated objects — makes it a harder version of the same problem, and SIMPLER is squarely aimed at it: "how can we build systems for evaluating manipulation policies trained on real data in simulated environments?"

// how it works

The method

The paper's framing decision is to lower the bar: rather than building a pixel- and physics-perfect "digital twin" of each real setup — expensive, and still an open research problem for deformable materials, lighting, and precise physical properties — SIMPLER only needs a simulator "merely realistic enough" that relative policy rankings transfer. If policy A beats policy B in the real world, the goal is for SIMPLER to rank A above B too, not to reproduce A's exact success rate.

To get there, the paper identifies two separate sources of real-to-sim mismatch and closes each with a different technique.

Closing the control gap: offline system identification

A simulated robot arm executing the exact same recorded action sequence as its real counterpart won't trace the same end-effector trajectory unless its controller's stiffness and damping (PD) parameters are tuned to match. The paper samples a handful of trajectories from existing real demonstration datasets (the RT-1 dataset and BridgeData V2 — no new data collection needed), replays each action sequence open-loop in simulation, and scores candidate PD parameters by how closely the simulated 6D end-effector pose and rotation track the real one:

Lsysid(p, d) = Ltransl(p, d) + Lrot(p, d) // translation + rotation tracking error between real and open-loop-replayed simulated trajectories

Three rounds of simulated annealing progressively narrow the PD search range around the best-scoring parameters. On the Google Robot, this is the difference between the simulated arm missing a coke-can grasp entirely and successfully replaying the pick-up motion (Fig. 4 in the paper).

Closing the visual gap: Visual Matching vs. Variant Aggregation

The paper's primary technique, Visual Matching, minimizes the appearance gap directly: (1) "green-screening" overlays a real background photo behind the simulated foreground, composited using ground-truth simulation segmentation masks; and (2) texture matching projects real object/robot textures onto the simulated meshes (segment the object in a real image with SAM, align the simulated pose, unproject the RGB texture onto the mesh, and — for less common viewpoints — synthesize the remaining views with the diffusion model Zero123++). Because a real robot arm's color can shift across a video (lighting, wear), the paper tunes multiple robot-arm-color variants and averages results over them for the Google Robot setup.

As a deliberate alternative — testing the opposite instinct of domain randomization rather than gap-minimization — the paper also builds Variant Aggregation: instead of matching the real scene, it randomizes background, lighting, distractors, and table texture into several environment "variants" (two per axis) and averages a policy's performance across all of them.

These two ingredients combine into SIMPLER — an installable (pip install), single-line, Gym-API simulated evaluation suite built on the SAPIEN physics engine, covering the Google Robot setups used by the RT-series of policies (Pick Coke Can, Move Near, Open/Close Drawer, Open Drawer and Place Apple) and the WidowX + BridgeData V2 setup (Put Spoon on Towel, Put Carrot on Plate, Stack Green Block on Yellow Block, Put Eggplant in Yellow Basket). A single environment renders at "3.5k simulation steps per second on a consumer NVIDIA 4090 GPU at 640 × 512 image resolution," which at a 500 Hz simulation frequency works out to roughly a 7× speedup over real-world evaluation.

Measuring whether it actually worked: MMRV

To judge whether a simulated-evaluation pipeline is any good, the paper argues the standard tool — Pearson's r — has two blind spots: it only credits a linear real/sim relationship (a simulator that reproduces the correct ranking but not a straight-line fit is unfairly penalized), and it is oversensitive to noise when real-world performances cluster in a narrow range. So the paper introduces the Mean Maximum Rank Violation (MMRV), a ranking-consistency metric weighted by how large the real-world performance gap is between any two mis-ranked policies:

MMRV(R, RS) = 1N Σi=1..N maxj ( |Ri − Rj| · 1[(RS,i < RS,j) ≠ (Ri < Rj)] ) // range [0,1], lower is better — averages each policy's worst-case rank violation, scaled by the real-world margin it violates

Most correlation numbers in the paper are reported as an (MMRV, Pearson r) pair, but the population each pair is computed over varies by table: a fixed set of paired real-and-sim policy evaluations for a given task in Figs. 6-7 and Table I, versus a fixed set of distribution-shift factors within a single policy for Fig. 8 and Table VI. Tables II and III report MMRV alone, with no paired Pearson r.

// what they found

The results

The headline experiment pairs six Google Robot policy checkpoints — RT-1 (Converged), RT-1 (15% of training), RT-1 (Begin), RT-1-X, RT-2-X, and Octo-Base — across four task groups, each run in both the real world and in SIMPLER-Visual Matching. Per task, the (MMRV, Pearson r) pairs are: Pick Coke Can 0.031 / 0.976, Move Near 0.111 / 0.855, Open/Close Drawer 0.055 / 0.915, and Open Drawer and Place Apple 0.000 / 0.969. Policies with high real-world performance — RT-1 (Converged) and RT-2-X — also obtain high performance in SIMPLER; policies with low real-world performance, such as RT-1 (Begin), similarly obtain low performance in SIMPLER.

On the WidowX + BridgeData V2 setup (three policies: RT-1-X, Octo-Base, Octo-Small, across four tasks with both a partial-credit "grasp" success and a final-task success), the paper reports MMRV = 0.014 and Pearson r = 0.890, described as estimated per task and then averaged. This is a much smaller sample (three policies per task) than the Google Robot experiments, and the paper itself notes that "for all but one task... all policies are ranked correctly" — of the four final tasks, only Put Carrot on Plate has a nonzero MMRV (0.111) and the lowest Pearson r (0.575): the real world ranks Octo-Small below Octo-Base on that task, but SIMPLER ranks them the other way around.

Comparing evaluation protocols directly (Table I, averaged over Pick Coke Can / Move Near / Open-Close Drawer for the same six policies): using held-out validation-set action MSE as a real-world performance proxy gets MMRV = 0.375, r = 0.308 — a weak signal, consistent with the common robotics intuition that offline imitation-learning loss doesn't track deployed success. SIMPLER-Variant Aggregation does much better (MMRV = 0.143, r = 0.778), and SIMPLER-Visual Matching does best of all (MMRV = 0.056, r = 0.924) — which is why the paper recommends Visual Matching as the default SIMPLER configuration.

Beyond ranking policies against each other, the paper tests whether SIMPLER reproduces a single policy's behavior under distribution shift. Comparing an RT-1 trained without image augmentation against one trained with it, across five shift axes (background, lighting, distractors, table texture, camera pose, averaged over Pick Coke Can and Move Near), SIMPLER's simulated performance drop tracks the real-world drop closely for both policies: camera pose causes by far the largest real-world performance drop for both (−0.46 without augmentation, −0.38 with it), table texture is the next-largest factor without augmentation (−0.39, tied with background at −0.17 once augmentation is added), while lighting and distractor changes stay comparatively minor throughout — and SIMPLER ranks the five shift factors almost identically to how they rank in the real world (MMRV = 0.000, r = 0.831 without augmentation; MMRV = 0.016, r = 0.970 with it — the one factor SIMPLER gets out of order in the augmented case is a near-tie between lighting and distractors, not the large camera-pose/table-texture effects). A more granular real-world finding — that both policies tolerate unseen solid table colors much better than unseen patterned textures (a 4% vs. 25% performance drop) — is reproduced in simulation almost exactly (2% vs. 24%).

The most striking test is a genuinely held-out prediction. During simulated evaluation, the authors noticed Octo-Base's success rate on Pick Coke Can swings from 0% with an untuned simulated robot-arm texture to 29.3% with one of the tuned arm textures, while RT-1-X (trained on the same Open X-Embodiment data) barely moves. To check whether this sensitivity is a simulation artifact or a real policy property, they ran a new real-world test never used to build SIMPLER — wrapping the physical robot arm in gift-wrapping paper — and found the same pattern: Octo-Base's real success rate falls further (0.293 → 0.000) than RT-1-X's (0.760 → 0.520).

Removing system identification hurts both trajectory tracking and MMRV. Ablating the visual-matching components on the Drawer tasks (Table III) shows a similar all-or-nothing pattern: applying green-screening or texture matching in isolation leaves MMRV unchanged at 0.087 — no better than doing nothing — and it only drops to 0.050 once green-screened background, drawer texture, and robot texture are all applied together. Finally, the whole pipeline reproduces on a second physics engine: SIMPLER-Variant Aggregation gets MMRV = 0.082, r = 0.923 on SAPIEN (the default) and MMRV = 0.058, r = 0.919 on Isaac Sim for the same Pick Coke Can / Move Near tasks.

// does it hold up?

The eval read

The paper is unusually careful about what it is and isn't claiming — it explicitly frames SIMPLER as targeting relative ranking correlation rather than 1:1 real-world reproduction, introduces a new metric (MMRV) precisely to avoid overclaiming from Pearson r alone, and is transparent about its own edge cases (it flags, in a footnote, that the RT-1 (Begin) drawer-task real evaluation was cut short after 2 trials for robot-safety reasons, so the resulting Variant Aggregation MMRV for that cell is likely an overestimate). That discipline is real, but a few things are worth weighing before treating "SIMPLER correlates with reality" as a fully general result.

The sample of policies is small and closely related. The core correlation claims rest on six policy checkpoints for the Google Robot experiments (three training stages of RT-1, plus RT-1-X, RT-2-X, Octo-Base) and only three policies (RT-1-X, Octo-Base, Octo-Small) for the WidowX + BridgeData V2 experiments. The appendix does stress-test this a little further — adding a seventh policy, an RT-1 trained solely on Pick-Coke-Can demonstrations, still yields a low MMRV (0.027) and high Pearson r (0.959) on that task (Table XIII) — but every policy tested, including that one, is still an RT-1/RT-1-X/RT-2-X/Octo checkpoint. A Pearson r computed over three, six, or seven points is sensitive to any single outlier, and — more importantly — every one of these policies comes from one of two closely related model lineages (the RT-series and Octo), several sharing training data through the Open X-Embodiment dataset. The paper doesn't test whether the same real-to-sim correlation would hold for a policy built on a substantially different architecture or training recipe (e.g., a diffusion-policy or a newer VLA foundation model outside this lineage).

The visual-matching recipe is fragile, by the paper's own ablation. Table III shows that almost every partial application of the recipe — green-screening alone, texture-matching alone, or any other pairing of the three components except one — leaves MMRV pinned at the same 0.087 as doing nothing at all. The one exception is worse, not neutral: green-screened background plus drawer-texture matching, without robot-texture tuning, actually raises MMRV to 0.142. A different partial combination (drawer texture + robot texture, no green screen) makes the real-sim success gap worse too (0.328 vs. the untouched baseline's 0.272). Only the full three-part combination gets the reported MMRV = 0.050. That means SIMPLER's advertised correlation is not a robust property of "simulate carefully" in general — it required getting every piece of a specific recipe right, tested on one task family (the drawer tasks) with three policies.

The comparison baseline is a strawman rather than a competing simulated-evaluation method. Table I's alternative to SIMPLER is offline validation-set action MSE — a genuinely weak proxy that few practitioners would seriously propose as a simulated-evaluation replacement. There's no head-to-head against, say, a partially-matched simulator, a generic domain-randomized simulator without the paper's specific texture-tuning pipeline, or the navigation-domain sim2real-predictivity approach (Kadian et al.) applied to manipulation. The paper's strongest internal comparison — Visual Matching vs. its own Variant Aggregation alternative — is a comparison between two of its own designs, not against an external method.

Manual effort is still real, and its cost isn't quantified. The paper itself notes that its "articulated object modeling process takes the most human effort among the entire simulation environment construction process," and building each new environment still involves 3D scanning or single-view reconstruction, Blender resizing, manual density/friction assignment, and camera-pose alignment. The paper reports wall-clock speedup for running an environment (7×) but not the time cost of building one, which is the actual bottleneck for scaling SIMPLER to many more tasks — a limitation the conclusion itself names as future work: "Enabling a fully-automatic and more scalable pipeline for creating thousands of realistic simulated environments is an ambitious goal for future work."

One table disagrees with the rest of the paper. A reader cross-referencing the protocol comparison against the per-task results will hit a contradiction. For Visual Matching on Open/Close Drawer, Table I reports MMRV 0.027 and Pearson r 0.942; Tables IV and XII and Fig. 6 all give 0.055 and 0.915 for what reads as the same quantity. The same split appears on the other two protocol rows (Variant Aggregation 0.235/0.486 vs. 0.321/0.737; validation MSE 0.306/0.231 vs. 0.346/0.264). Column misalignment is ruled out — Table I's own Average reproduces exactly from its three printed cells ((0.031 + 0.111 + 0.027)/3 = 0.056), and its Pick Coke Can and Move Near cells match Table XII exactly. Only the drawer column differs, which suggests Table I's drawer figures are computed over a different population than the per-task tables'. The paper never says which, or that the two disagree at all.

baselines

Compares against offline validation-set action MSE (a weak strawman) and its own Variant Aggregation alternative — no external simulated-evaluation method or full digital-twin baseline is benchmarked head-to-head.

contamination

Not applicable in the usual training-data-leakage sense — this is a simulated-evaluation methodology paper, not a benchmark built from scraped or public text/image corpora. The closer analogue (whether policies were ever trained inside SIMPLER's own simulated environments) doesn't arise, since every evaluated policy is trained purely on real-world data.

what's missed

All six/three evaluated policies come from two closely related model families (RT-series, Octo), some sharing training-data lineage through Open X-Embodiment; the correlation claim is untested against architecturally distinct future policies. The visual-matching recipe's correlation gains are shown to be all-or-nothing on one task family (Table III), not demonstrated as robust across task types.

would raise confidence

A broader policy sample spanning genuinely different architectures/training data; a documented time-and-labor cost for building a new SIMPLER environment (to assess real scalability, not just runtime speed); and a repeated real-world evaluation of the same policy (to establish a real-eval-vs-real-eval noise floor that would bound how high any real-to-sim correlation could even be).

// bottom line

The correlation numbers SIMPLER reports are genuinely strong and the paper is honest about the two-part engineering effort (system identification plus visual matching) it took to get there — but the evidence is a small, related-model sample on a handful of tabletop tasks, and the ablations show the visual-matching gains are fragile to any partial application. Read the headline Pearson r / MMRV numbers as strong correlation demonstrated on RT-series and Octo policies across eight rigid-object tabletop tasks, not as a general property of simulated manipulation evaluation.

// takeaways

What to remember

  1. SIMPLER doesn't try to build a perfect "digital twin" — it deliberately targets just enough real-to-sim correlation in relative policy ranking (via Pearson r and a new metric, MMRV, that weighs rank violations by their real-world margin) rather than 1:1 absolute reproduction, which is a weaker and more scalable bar than full-fidelity simulation.
  2. Getting there took two separate engineering fixes — offline system identification for the control gap, and green-screening plus texture-matching ("Visual Matching") for the visual gap — and the paper's own ablation (Table III) shows partial application of the visual fix can leave correlation completely unchanged, or even make the real-sim gap worse; only the full combination reliably worked.
  3. The strongest single piece of evidence is a genuinely predictive test: SIMPLER flagged Octo-Base's sensitivity to arm texture before the authors ran a matching real-world experiment (wrapping the physical arm in gift wrap), and the real result confirmed the simulated prediction — direct evidence the correlation isn't just curve-fit to the policies used to build it.
  4. All the correlation claims rest on six (Google Robot) or three (WidowX/BridgeData V2) policies from two closely related model lineages — the RT-series and Octo — some sharing training data through the Open X-Embodiment dataset; whether SIMPLER's real-to-sim correlation generalizes to architecturally different policies, or scales cheaply to new tasks beyond rigid-object tabletop manipulation, is untested by this paper.
