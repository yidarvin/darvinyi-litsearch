Benchmarking Multi-Agent Deep Reinforcement Learning Algorithms in Cooperative Tasks

Nine deep MARL algorithms, three learning paradigms, 25 cooperative tasks, one shared codebase: a systematic head-to-head that finds no universal winner, exposes a sparse-reward environment (RWARE) where half the field never learns at all, and ships the EPyMARL codebase plus two new environments as lasting infrastructure.

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

By 2020, deep multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) had produced a "plethora of new MARL algorithms" — independent learners, centralised-critic policy-gradient methods, value-decomposition networks — but almost no shared way to tell which of them actually works, and where. Single-agent RL had already been through this reckoning: Duan et al. (2016), Henderson et al. (2018) and Wang et al. (2019) had each run systematic comparative studies showing how much reported gains depended on implementation details and evaluation protocol rather than algorithmic novelty. MARL had no equivalent. The one prior comparative study the authors could point to, Albrecht and Ramamoorthy (2012), compared classic (non-deep) algorithms in simple matrix games — not the modern deep-MARL landscape. In its absence, new algorithms were validated against whatever baselines, environments, and hyperparameter budgets their own authors chose, on tasks like the StarCraft Multi-Agent Challenge (SMAC) or the Multi-Agent Particle Environment (MPE) that were individually popular but never assembled into one consistent evaluation.

A second, narrower gap sits alongside the first: existing MARL environments cluster around dense-reward coordination (MPE) or large discrete tactical combat (SMAC), but sparse-reward exploration under partial observability — the regime that stresses credit assignment the hardest — was, in the authors' words, "underrepresented in MARL environments," despite being a well-studied challenge in single-agent RL (e.g. Montezuma's Revenge). There was no small, fast, purpose-built cooperative environment for testing whether an algorithm can learn anything at all when rewards are rare.

// how it works

The method

The paper runs a controlled comparison of nine algorithms spanning three paradigms. Independent learning (IL) — each agent trains as if the others were part of the environment: IQL (independent Q-learning, off-policy), IA2C and IPPO (independent A2C / PPO, on-policy). Centralised-training-decentralised-execution (CTDE) policy gradient — a decentralised actor paired with a critic that sees joint information during training: MADDPG, COMA, MAA2C, and MAPPO. Value decomposition — a joint Q-value factorised into per-agent utilities: VDN (linear decomposition) and QMIX (a learned, monotonic mixing network). Table 1 in the paper lays out each algorithm's off-/on-policy and value-/policy-based identity; the grouping in the text above follows the paper's own Section 2 structure.

These nine algorithms are evaluated on 25 cooperative tasks across two repeated matrix games (Climbing, and Penalty for five penalty values k ∈ {0, −25, −50, −75, −100}) and four multi-agent environments: MPE (4 tasks — Speaker-Listener, Spread, Adversary, and Tag/Predator-Prey), SMAC (5 tasks), and two environments the authors built and open-sourced for this paper — Level-Based Foraging (LBF, 7 tasks: agents must jointly load food items whose "level" exceeds any single agent's own level) and Multi-Robot Warehouse (RWARE, 3 tasks: robots must locate, deliver, and return requested shelves, rewarded only on full delivery). LBF and RWARE were purpose-built to cover the sparse-reward, partially-observable gap the paper identifies: RWARE in particular only rewards a fully-completed shelf delivery, making it the sparsest-reward environment in the suite.

Fairness constraints matter as much as the algorithm list. Because off-policy algorithms reuse samples via a replay buffer and on-policy algorithms don't, on-policy algorithms are trained for 10× the environment timesteps of off-policy algorithms in every environment (e.g. 20M vs. 2M timesteps in MPE/LBF, 40M vs. 4M in SMAC/RWARE), so the comparison is by wall-clock/sample-efficiency-adjusted training rather than raw step count. Hyperparameters are tuned per algorithm per environment, not per task: one representative task is chosen from each environment (Speaker-Listener for MPE, "3s5z" for SMAC, "15x15-3p-5f" for LBF, "Tiny 4p" for RWARE), a grid search over 3 seeds picks the best configuration there, and that single configuration is then reused, unchanged, across every other task in that environment. Every algorithm is then run with 5 seeds, evaluated at fixed intervals (41 evaluation points, 100 episodes each), and reported by two metrics: maximum return (the mean at the best-performing evaluation checkpoint) and average return (averaged over all evaluation checkpoints, rewarding faster learners). Every algorithm is also run both with and without parameter sharing across agents, a common implementation choice known to matter (Christianos et al., 2021) but rarely reported consistently in prior MARL papers.

To make all of this reproducible, the authors release EPyMARL (Extended PyMARL), which extends the widely-used PyMARL codebase (Samvelyan et al., 2019 — originally IQL/COMA/VDN/QMIX only) with five more algorithms (IA2C, IPPO, MADDPG, MAA2C, MAPPO) and configurable implementation details: parameter sharing on/off, heterogeneous observation shapes, reward standardisation, entropy regularisation, and recurrent vs. feedforward networks. The whole study cost 138,916 CPU hours (excluding hyperparameter search), run entirely on CPU; SMAC is by a clear margin the most expensive of the five environments to simulate per step (Figure 5), which is why its hyperparameter search was deliberately narrower than the other environments'.

// what they found

The results

The headline finding is negative, in the useful sense: no algorithm dominates across environments. Figure 2 plots normalised returns (each algorithm's return on a task rescaled to [0,1] using that task's own min/max across all nine algorithms, then averaged across tasks within an environment) for MPE, SMAC, LBF, and RWARE. On this normalised scale, most algorithms cluster together in MPE and SMAC, spread out more in LBF, and split sharply into two tiers in RWARE.

The raw numbers behind that split (Table 3, maximum return with parameter sharing) are stark and environment-specific — and because every environment has its own reward scale, they are only comparable within a row, never across environments. In RWARE Tiny 4p, MAPPO reaches 49.42 ± 1.22 and MAA2C 32.50 ± 9.79, while IQL (0.72 ± 0.37), MADDPG (0.54 ± 0.10), COMA (1.16 ± 0.15), VDN (0.80 ± 0.28) and QMIX (0.30 ± 0.19) are all statistically indistinguishable from failing to learn at all. The pattern repeats in Small 4p (MAPPO 27.00 ± 1.80 vs. QMIX 0.06 ± 0.08) and Tiny 2p (MAPPO 21.16 ± 1.50 vs. VDN 0.12 ± 0.07). The paper's read: value-decomposition and off-policy replay-based methods need denser reward signal than RWARE provides to successfully decompose or bootstrap a useful value function, while the on-policy actor-critic methods keep learning — except COMA, which despite being on-policy also fails to learn in RWARE, something the paper attributes to the very high variance of its counterfactual-advantage estimate.

Elsewhere the ranking inverts. On the SMAC task 3s_vs_5z, the simplest algorithm in the whole comparison, IQL, posts the highest mean return (21.15 ± 0.41) — though IPPO (19.36 ± 6.15), VDN (19.03 ± 5.77) and MAPPO (18.17 ± 4.17) are all marked as not significantly different from it, so IQL's edge is a real win only over QMIX (16.04 ± 2.87) and the much lower MAA2C/MADDPG/IA2C/COMA tail, not over MAPPO or VDN. On MMM2, VDN (18.49 ± 0.31) and QMIX (18.40 ± 0.24, not significantly different from VDN) lead, well ahead of MAPPO's 17.78 ± 0.44 and far ahead of MADDPG (3.96 ± 0.32) or COMA (6.95 ± 0.27). In LBF's easiest task (8x8-2p-2f-c), six of the nine algorithms tie at a ceiling of 1.00 ± 0.00; on the harder 15x15-3p-5f, the field splits (IA2C 0.89 ± 0.04 vs. IQL 0.17 ± 0.08 and QMIX 0.09 ± 0.04). In the matrix games, only Penalty k=0 is solved to its 250-point optimum (fixed by the payoff matrix over the 25-step episode); on Climbing and every negative-k Penalty variant, the whole field converges instead to the same sub-optimal, risk-free equilibrium — eight of the nine land on exactly 50.00 ± 0.00 (20% of the 250-point optimum) at k=-25, -50, -75 and -100, with MADDPG a hair below at 49.97–49.98, and the modal Climbing result is 175.00 (64% of the 275-point optimum), with only IQL's 195.00 ± 67.82 showing one seed in five reaching the true optimum. That is exactly the failure mode the matrix games were built to expose — Table 2 lists their Main Difficulty as "Sub-optimal equilibria." Within that shared equilibrium, MADDPG is the one consistent laggard, coming in marginally below the rest in every penalty variant (e.g. 249.98 ± 0.04 vs. 250.00 ± 0.00 for k=0). MADDPG's underperformance is much larger in the discrete grid-world environments (LBF, RWARE), which the authors do attribute to bias from MADDPG's Gumbel-Softmax discretisation trick — the same mechanism it uses to act in discrete action spaces at all, since MADDPG's underlying DPG formulation assumes continuous actions.

Parameter sharing (Figure 3) improves normalised returns on average in every environment except the matrix games, though the paper is explicit that the environment-level averages in that figure are not themselves statistically significant — the effect shows up reliably only when you look at individual algorithm–task pairs in the full tables, and it correlates with reward sparsity: bigger, sparser-reward tasks benefit more from sharing than small, dense-reward ones.

// does it hold up?

The eval read

As a benchmarking study rather than a method paper, the interesting question isn't "does the headline claim hold" — there isn't a single headline claim to defend — but whether the evaluation protocol itself is trustworthy enough to license the comparative conclusions readers actually cite this paper for.

The biggest methodological soft spot is hyperparameter reuse across tasks. Section 4.3 states plainly: hyperparameters are tuned once, on one representative task per environment (e.g. "3s5z" stands in for all five SMAC tasks, "15x15-3p-5f" for all seven LBF tasks), and then applied unchanged to every other task in that environment. That's a defensible cost-saving compromise given the 138,916-CPU-hour budget, but it means an algorithm whose best configuration happens to transfer well across tasks within an environment gets an advantage over one whose best configuration is more task-specific — a confound the paper doesn't quantify or control for. It's the closest thing to an "evaluation leakage" concern this kind of RL benchmark has, and it's worth remembering whenever a single-task result (like IQL's win on 3s_vs_5z) is read as evidence about IQL's hyperparameters specifically, rather than about the shared SMAC configuration.

Second, the paper runs two-sided t-tests (α = 0.05) between the best algorithm and every other algorithm, per task, and marks non-significant differences with an asterisk in Table 3 — good practice on its own. But across 25 tasks × 8 pairwise comparisons per task (200 tests total), the paper does not mention any multiple-comparison correction. With no correction, some of the asterisks (and their absence) are less trustworthy than a single α = 0.05 test on its own would suggest, especially given only 5 seeds per cell — several RWARE and SMAC confidence intervals are wide enough (e.g. IPPO on RWARE Tiny 4p: 31.82 ± 10.71) that significance calls there carry real uncertainty.

Third, on SMAC specifically, the paper reports return rather than the field's usual win-rate metric as its headline number, arguing (reasonably) that win-rate can diverge from return and is less informative about what the algorithms are actually optimising. That's a fair methodological choice, but it means Table 3's SMAC numbers are not directly comparable to the win-rates reported throughout the broader SMAC literature (including the original SMAC paper) — the win-rate table is relegated to Appendix G, and a reader who only sees Table 3 could not reconstruct the field-standard comparison without also reading the appendix.

Finally, the paper is upfront about scope, which is a point in its favour, not against it: it covers only fully-cooperative tasks with shared reward, explicitly excludes competitive/mixed-motive settings, communication, and opponent modelling, and says so directly in the conclusion — so absence of those settings is a stated boundary, not an omission to fault the paper for.

baselines

Nine algorithms across all three major deep-MARL paradigms current at the time (2020–21) — a genuinely broad sweep, not a strawman comparison. It is not exhaustive within value decomposition itself, though: the study only implements VDN and QMIX, so other factorisation variants in the wider literature are outside the nine-algorithm set and a reader shouldn't assume the value-decomposition paradigm is fully represented by just these two.

contamination

Not data contamination in the LLM sense, but the closest analogue — per-environment (not per-task) hyperparameter reuse — is a real, paper-acknowledged shortcut that could bias which algorithm looks best on tasks other than the one hyperparameters were tuned on.

what's missed

Normalised, per-task-rescaled aggregates (Figure 2) are the only cross-environment signal offered; raw Table 3 numbers span wildly different scales (fractions in LBF, tens in SMAC/RWARE, hundreds in matrix games) and are only legally compared within a single task/row. No correction for the ~200 pairwise significance tests run across the full table.

would raise confidence

Per-task (not per-environment) hyperparameter search where compute allows; a stated multiple-comparison correction, or wider confidence intervals reported alongside the raw asterisk calls; more than 5 seeds in the highest-variance cells (several RWARE and SMAC rows have confidence intervals comparable in size to the gap between algorithms).

// bottom line

The comparison is honest about its own limits in a way many benchmark papers aren't — no algorithm is crowned a winner, RWARE's failure mode for value-decomposition and off-policy methods (plus COMA, despite being on-policy) is reported plainly rather than tuned away, and the win-rate-vs-return distinction on SMAC is flagged in the text. The main things a careful reader should discount for are the per-environment (not per-task) hyperparameter reuse and the lack of a stated correction across ~200 significance tests — neither invalidates the qualitative conclusions, but both mean the exact asterisks in Table 3 deserve a slightly wider error bar than printed.

// takeaways

What to remember

  1. There is no single best deep-MARL algorithm across environments: independent learning is competitive under full or near-full observability (matrix games, most MPE/LBF tasks), centralised critics earn their keep specifically under partial observability with heavy coordination demands (harder SMAC, RWARE with 4 agents), and value decomposition (VDN/QMIX) leads in dense-reward SMAC and ties the best on the small 8x8 LBF tasks, but falls behind on LBF's larger, sparser 15x15 tasks and collapses to near-zero learning in RWARE's sparse-reward regime alongside COMA, MADDPG and IQL.
  2. The two artifacts this paper ships — the EPyMARL codebase (extending PyMARL with five more algorithms and configurable implementation details like parameter sharing) and the two new environments, Level-Based Foraging and Multi-Robot Warehouse — turned out to be the paper's most durable contribution, becoming standard tools in later MARL work independent of any specific Table-3 ranking.
  3. Cross-environment comparisons are only valid on the paper's own normalised [0,1] scale (Figure 2); the raw returns in Table 3 use environment-specific units (a [0,1] completion fraction in LBF, a raw shelf-delivery count in RWARE, unit-based combat reward in SMAC, payoff-matrix values in the matrix games) and cannot be compared as numbers across rows from different environments.
