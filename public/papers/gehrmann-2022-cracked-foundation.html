Repairing the Cracked Foundation: A Survey of Obstacles in Evaluation Practices for Generated Text

A 244-citation taxonomy of what's broken in NLG evaluation — flawed metrics, undocumented human studies, and datasets built for other tasks — backed by an audit of 66 recent papers that follow the fixes about 27% of the time.

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

By early 2022, NLG systems had improved to the point that the metrics built to grade them had stopped discriminating: BLEU and ROUGE measure lexical overlap with a reference, a proxy that was already rough and gets rougher as outputs get more fluent. The paper names the actual failure mode as an incentive problem, not a knowledge problem — "almost none of the suggestions are followed or new techniques used" — because there's a circular dependency: evaluation practice only improves if models are evaluated with it, but authors have little incentive to adopt evaluation infrastructure that costs effort and doesn't win the leaderboard.

Each of the three legs of NLG evaluation carries its own well-documented but rarely-connected failures. Automatic metrics reward "human-likeness" — similarity to a reference — even though Belz and Gatt (2008) found that "a system's ability to produce human-like outputs may be completely unrelated to its effect on human task-performance," and both Kryscinski et al. (2019) and Fabbri et al. (2021) find ROUGE doesn't significantly correlate with either the content quality or the linguistic quality of a summary. Human evaluation is inconsistent by construction: Howcroft et al. (2020) coded 165 NLG papers and found generated text evaluated along 204 distinct quality dimensions that map to just 71 different criteria — and of the 478 specific evaluation questions in that sample, more than half (279/478) never define the criterion being measured and 65% (311/478) never report the exact question given to raters. And the datasets underneath both are quietly broken: over 70% of XSum's reference summaries contain information not present in the source article (Maynez et al., 2020), and CNN/DailyMail's reference "summaries" are homepage bullet points assembled for a reading-comprehension task, not a summarization one — human raters given both actually prefer the article's own first three sentences to CNN/DM's reference summary (Stiennon et al., 2020).

No earlier survey had put all three failure classes — metrics, human evaluation, and datasets — into one connected argument along with a way to check whether a given paper is actually following the fix. That's the gap this paper closes: not a new metric or a new dataset, but a synthesized diagnosis of why evaluation research keeps producing better tools that modeling papers keep not using, plus a concrete alternative to the single leaderboard number (see "how it works").

The taxonomy, and the proposal

// which version this page follows

This page follows the arXiv v1 preprint (submitted 14 Feb 2022) — the version source ↗ above resolves to. The paper was later published as JAIR 77 (2023): 103–166 (submitted 02/2022, published 05/2023); every number, table, and figure this page cites is identical between the two versions, checked cell by cell. Two things differ. Structurally, the JAIR camera-ready drops Appendix A entirely — 64 published pages that end on the bibliography — while its own body text still says "the exact instructions in Appendix A," a dangling reference in the version a reader following source ↗'s eventual publication would land on. Substantively, it also adds a subsection that doesn't exist in v1 at all — "NLG Evaluation in times of large language models" (pp. 136–137) — covering GPT-3/Chinchilla/PaLM-era models, BIG-bench and HELM, and scratchpad/chain-of-thought prompting. The camera-ready’s bibliography grows from v1’s 244 entries to 292; the new subsection accounts for 13 of those, so most of the other 35 were added elsewhere. That single added subsection is also, tidily, where Semantic Scholar's reference list for this paper picked up PaLM, HELM, BIG-bench, scratchpads, chain-of-thought, and GPT-3 as things a "February 2022" paper supposedly cites — every one of those false edge leads traces back to it. Every Appendix A citation below (the checklist documentation, the A.1 selection detail, A.2's 29 instructions, and A.3's limitations) is drawn from v1 and is labelled as such where it appears.

This is a survey, not an experiment: its "method" is a structured literature synthesis, drawing on 244 cited studies published over roughly twenty years, organized into three sections that mirror the actual pipeline a model goes through on its way to a leaderboard number — automatic metrics (§3), human evaluation (§4), and the datasets underneath both (§5). Figure 1 states the paper's thesis visually before a single citation is given: train/test data, model hyperparameters, automatic metrics, human judgments, and qualitative/quantitative analysis all funnel through a single "Evaluation" block that most of the field treats as an opaque means to one end — a number proving a model beats its predecessor — rather than a process worth reporting on its own terms.

For each of the three sections, the survey works through second-order questions the field usually skips rather than just cataloging complaints. On metrics: is similarity-to-reference even a valid target ("The Myth of the Single Reliable Number"), and do learned/embedding metrics actually generalize better than lexical ones — empirically, not reliably: Bhandari et al. (2020b) find no distributional metric outperforms ROUGE on TAC/CNN-DM segment-level correlation. On human evaluation: what's actually being measured, how a question's framing biases the rating, who is doing the rating (crowdworkers vs. experts), and whether the resulting numbers are even statistically powered — Wei and Jia (2021) show that detecting a 1-point difference on a 1–100 WMT scale would require roughly 10,000 perfect annotator judgments. On datasets: whose language and whose data practices get represented, whether references are themselves faithful, and whether test-set construction (i.i.d. splits, contemporaneous with training data) actually reflects deployment.

The constructive counterpart is §6's "evaluation report," sketched in Figure 3: rather than one number, a model's evaluation should ship as part of its model card, bundling a data card per dataset, a benchmark matrix scoring the model on multiple datasets with multiple automatic metrics and documented human evaluation side by side, an error analysis broken into categories like hallucination and grammaticality rather than one aggregate quality score, and a model-audit "evaluation suite" of targeted stress tests reported as signed deltas with uncertainty rather than folded into the headline number. The mock example in Figure 3 shows a model handling 1% spelling noise and OCR-derived input reasonably well (−9±3 and −5±3 points on metric M1) but degrading sharply on an unseen topic or a 2018→2020 time shift (−29±8 and −23±5 M1). This isn't hypothetical framing: Figure 2, discussed under "what they found," is the survey's own re-analysis of freshly collected test data showing exactly the kind of time-shift audit the evaluation report calls for.

This proposal is also where the paper's own prior work shows up on the reference list. Gehrmann is a co-author of the GEM benchmark (Gehrmann et al., 2021), cited here as one of the "projects that standardize parts of the process" of human evaluation and as the source of an improved, filtered version of XSum built on a faithfulness classifier trained on Maynez et al.'s hallucination annotations. GEM is this survey's constructive sibling, not a paper it's critiquing from outside — a working attempt at some of what §6 argues every NLG paper should be doing.

// what they found

What the literature — and the survey's own audit — actually shows

Most of what the survey "finds" is a synthesis of numbers already reported elsewhere, made comparable for the first time. On metrics: 100% of papers introducing new summarization models at *CL conferences in 2021 report ROUGE, and 69% report only ROUGE — a persistence set against a string of independent critiques the survey walks through: Rankel et al. (2013) find that rarely-used ROUGE configurations outperform the commonly-used ones; Graham (2015), testing all 192 ROUGE configurations against BLEU, finds that none beat it and that the best-performing configuration was the precision variant of ROUGE-2; and Kryscinski et al. (2019) and Fabbri et al. (2021), evaluating the F1 variants of multiple ROUGE versions across several rating dimensions, are the ones who find the conventional ROUGE-1/-2/-L configuration strictly worse than less commonly used variants like ROUGE-3/-4/-WE-1. On datasets: a sample of 20 summarization papers from 2021 evaluates on 27 different datasets in total, with CNN/DailyMail and XSum the most common (used 5 and 4 times respectively) and only 2 of the 27 non-English.

The survey's own empirical contribution is the analysis in §7: 66 generation papers pulled from ACL, EMNLP, and INLG 2021 (selected by title, then filtered to those reporting actual modeling results), each hand-coded by one of the three authors against the 29-item checklist condensed in Table 1 (documented in full in Appendix A, arXiv v1 only — see the version note above). Coding is deliberately generous — a paper only needs to state that a metric was chosen for some reason to count "yes" on motivating its metric choice, regardless of whether that reason is good — so the authors describe the results as an upper bound on compliance, not a strict audit. Even so: per-paper compliance scores range from 6.5% to 58.1%, averaging 27.3% (median 25.8%, SD 0.11) — no paper in the sample follows more than about three-fifths of the 29 recommendations, and the histogram in Figure 4 shows most papers clustered between 15% and 35%.

Table 1's row-level numbers are where the specific gaps show up. The basics are common — 83.9% of applicable papers (47/56) evaluate on multiple datasets and 72.7% (48/66) run some human evaluation — but the documentation behind those numbers is thin: only 38.2% (21/55) motivate why they picked a given dataset and only 30.3% (20/66) motivate their metric choice. Dataset self-critique almost never turns into dataset repair: 28.8% of papers (19/66) point out issues with the data they use, but only a single paper in the entire sample (1.7%, 1/59) contributes to that dataset's documentation, and of the 13 papers for whom releasing a fix was even applicable, only 3 (23.1%) actually released an updated version. Rigor around human evaluation results is close to absent: not one of the 48 applicable papers (0.0%) estimates an effect size or runs a power analysis before collecting judgments, and only 25.0% (12/48) run any significance test at all. And of what a paper can actually release, releasing the underlying evidence is the rarest: 1.5% (1/66) release validation-set outputs, 3.1% (2/65) release test-set outputs, and 2.1% (1/48) release the human evaluation annotations themselves — among the least-observed practices in the whole checklist, just above the 0.0% power-analysis rate noted a sentence earlier.

// does it hold up?

The eval read

As a taxonomy, the survey is careful about its own limits in a way surveys often aren't. Appendix A.3 (arXiv v1 only — dropped from the JAIR camera-ready, so this self-disclosure doesn't survive into the published version) flags, unprompted, that each of the 66 papers was coded by only one of the three authors — "there could be misunderstandings of the different dimensions" — and that phrasing several checklist items as recall-oriented prompts means real practice could be worse than the reported percentages, not better (a paper is marked "yes" for documenting a human study's setup even if it never defines its rating criteria, exactly the sloppier practice Howcroft et al. (2020) separately measured and found much lower rates for). The paper is also explicit about what it leaves out — interpretability methods, extrinsic/task-embedded evaluation, dialogue-specific metrics, and any non-textual or multimodal generation — rather than silently generalizing past its stated scope of conditional, text-to-text NLG.

One number in the survey's own headline result doesn't reconcile, though. §7 reports that "36.7% of our 2046 judgments were positive." But the paper states, in two independent places, that the coding instrument has 29 dimensions — once in the introduction ("66 recent EMNLP, INLG, and ACL papers along 29 dimensions") and once implicitly in Table 1, whose 29 rows match Appendix A.2's 29 bulleted instructions exactly (14 bullets on one page, 15 on the next; arXiv v1 only, per the version note above). With 66 papers and 29 dimensions, the absolute maximum number of judgments — even if every applicable cell were filled and none were marked N/A — is 66 × 29 = 1,914; the actual applicable count summed directly from Table 1's own Yes+No columns is 1,649. Neither reaches 2,046. What does divide evenly is the multiplicand: 2,046 = 31 × 66, pointing to a 31-item instrument rather than the 29-item one described everywhere else. Independent corroboration: §7's own per-paper range and median are exact 31ths, not 29ths — 6.5% is 2/31, the median 25.8% is 8/31, and 58.1% is 18/31, and no fraction k/n with n ≤ 29 rounds to either 6.5% or 58.1%. The 36.7% figure itself only reconciles against the 2,046 denominator, too: 36.7% of 2,046 is roughly 751 positive judgments, but Table 1's own Yes column sums to 534 — a positive rate of 32.4% (534/1,649) against the table's own applicable count, not 36.7%, and 36.7% is only reachable as 751/2,046. Taken together, this looks like a reconciliation failure between §7 and Table 1 — the summary statistics in §7 were computed over an instrument with 31 dimensions, while Table 1 and Appendix A.2 both document 29. The paper doesn't say what the other two dimensions were or why Table 1 doesn't carry them, and this page doesn't guess. It's a small inconsistency in a supporting statistic, not in the headline 27.3%-average number (which does check out against Figure 4's histogram, whose visible bars sum to exactly 66 papers) — but the "2046" figure, and the 6.5–58.1% range and 25.8% median built on it, shouldn't be taken at face value against Table 1.

Beyond that arithmetic wrinkle, the 66-paper sample is a self-selected snapshot: papers are chosen by generation-related title keywords from three particular 2021 venues (Appendix A.1, arXiv v1 only), with "10–15" machine-translation papers ending up included as the residue of a policy against specifically selecting translation papers, adopted "to avoid an over-emphasis on machine translation" — not something folded in to achieve that emphasis. So the 27.3% figure describes compliance among papers that already self-identify as NLG work at NLG-friendly venues, not NLP practice broadly. And the constructive proposal — evaluation reports, model audits, targeted evaluation suites — never engages the field's actual next move, and that isn't just a publication-date accident: the JAIR camera-ready does add a subsection on the GPT-3/PaLM era (BIG-bench, HELM, scratchpad and chain-of-thought prompting — see the version note above), so the authors had an explicit opportunity to address the LLM era on revision — though in fairness that revision closed in 2022 (JAIR’s newest citations are all that year), before LLM-as-judge crystallised with G-Eval and MT-Bench in 2023. What they propose there instead is smaller, high-quality test sets and dataset-specific learned metrics, not a model-as-evaluator — "as a judge," "LLM-as," and "GPT-4" all return zero hits in both versions. Most of NLP evaluation instead shifted toward LLM-as-judge scoring, which reintroduces exactly the single-number, correlate-with-a-black-box-rater framing that §3 spends several pages taking apart in learned metrics like BLEURT and COMET — just moved one level up the evaluation stack.

baselines

N/A in the usual sense — there's no model being benchmarked. The closest analogue is the 29-item checklist itself, which is the authors' own construction rather than an externally validated instrument, coded by a single annotator per paper with no reported inter-rater agreement.

contamination

Not applicable in the leakage sense, but the 66-paper sample carries its own selection bias: papers are drawn from three 2021 venues by title keyword, which skews the 27.3% average toward papers that already brand themselves as NLG work.

what's missed

A second annotator (or any inter-rater check) for the 66-paper coding; a reconciled count behind the "2046 judgments" figure, which doesn't square with the paper's own 29-dimension, 66-paper instrument; and any anticipation of LLM-as-judge evaluation — the direction the field actually took instead of evaluation reports, and one that even the JAIR camera-ready's added GPT-3/PaLM-era subsection (BIG-bench, HELM, scratchpad/chain-of-thought) never engages.

would raise confidence

A published inter-annotator agreement score for the 66-paper checklist coding, even on a subsample, and a corrected total-judgment denominator in §7 that actually matches Table 1's own Yes+No columns.

// bottom line

A genuinely foundational diagnosis for its moment — the taxonomy across metrics, human evaluation, and datasets holds together, the paper is unusually candid about the limits of its own 66-paper audit, and the "evaluation report" proposal is concrete enough to actually implement. But treat the "2046 judgments" figure in §7 as unreconciled arithmetic rather than a verified count, read the 27% compliance number as a description of self-selected 2021 NLG papers rather than of the field at large, and remember that the field's actual answer to "how can we fix NLG evaluation" turned out to be LLM judges — a path this paper's own critique of single-number, black-box metrics would have had plenty to say about.

// takeaways

What to remember

  1. The central diagnosis is a circular dependency, not a knowledge gap: better evaluation methods already exist in the literature this survey cites, but modeling papers have little incentive to adopt them because doing so costs effort without winning the leaderboard — evaluation only improves if models use it, and models won't use it until it's cheap and rewarded.
  2. Its concrete fix is the "evaluation report": multiple datasets × multiple metrics × documented human evaluation, wrapped in model/data cards, with a categorized error analysis and a model-audit "evaluation suite" reporting stress-test deltas (OCR noise, spelling noise, topic shift, time shift) with uncertainty — instead of one leaderboard number.
  3. The survey backs this with its own 66-paper 2021 audit against a 29-item checklist: 27.3% average compliance, 0% of applicable papers ran a power analysis before a human study, and only one paper in the sample contributed to fixing a dataset issue it had itself identified.
  4. Its own headline statistic has an unreconciled number: "2046 judgments" doesn't square with the paper's stated 29-dimension, 66-paper instrument (max possible 1,914; actual applicable count 1,649 from Table 1) — a reminder to check even a survey's own summary arithmetic against the table it's summarizing.
