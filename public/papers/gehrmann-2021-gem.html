The GEM Benchmark: Natural Language Generation, its Evaluation and Metrics

GEM packages 11 existing NLG datasets — summarization, dialogue, data-to-text, simplification — across 18 languages into a "living benchmark" that refuses a single leaderboard number, reporting instead through an interactive multi-metric exploration tool, ten kinds of adversarial challenge sets, and NLG-specific data cards. The numbers here are small-model validation-set baselines (T5-60M, BART-139M), reported ahead of the actual ACL 2021 shared task, not final test-set results.

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

By 2021, natural-language-understanding tasks had GLUE and SuperGLUE: shared datasets, a shared metric, one leaderboard number that meaningfully ranks systems. Natural language generation never got the equivalent, and GEM argues this isn't an oversight — it's structural. A generation system has to be simultaneously faithful to its input, fluent, and appropriately diverse, and these goals can conflict (the paper cites Hashimoto et al., 2019, on the diversity/quality trade-off), so no single number captures progress the way accuracy does for classification. In its place, the field settled for lexical-overlap metrics like ROUGE and BLEU that reward surface word overlap: ROUGE can be inflated just by generating longer summaries (Sun et al., 2019), and can score a fluent, unfaithful summary as "good" if it reuses enough of the reference's words (Maynez et al., 2020). Meanwhile new models are still trained and reported on divergent, mostly Anglo-centric corpora (Bender, 2019), human evaluation setups vary paper to paper without even agreeing on what "fluency" or "accuracy" mean (Howcroft et al., 2020), and the handful of prior attempts at NLG-flavored benchmarks undercut their own multi-task framing by scoring everything with one metric anyway — DecaNLP and GLGE include generation tasks but reduce them to a single number, and XGLUE folds its two generation tasks into plain BLEU-4, which the paper (citing Reiter, 2018) calls simply inadequate for NLG. GEM's bet is that a benchmark can only be useful for generation if it is explicitly built to keep changing: swap out "solved" datasets, keep adding new automated metrics, and develop reproducible human-evaluation standards, rather than freezing a leaderboard the way GLUE could.

// how it works

The method

GEM is best understood as a process for selecting and maintaining a benchmark, not a single fixed dataset drop. The 56-author consortium first elicited 35 candidate datasets from participants (listed in Appendix B), then ran a 28-response internal survey asking members to rate, on a 5-point Likert scale, how much they wanted each candidate task included, plus yes/no questions on "hard" inclusion criteria (e.g. open-access only) and "soft" criteria (e.g. preference for multi-reference data, a mix of high- and low-resource languages). The final selection then maximized rated utility under resource constraints — the paper describes it explicitly as "similar to a knapsack solver" — which the authors position as an extension of SuperGLUE's own dataset-selection process (SuperGLUE additionally used baseline-model difficulty as a filter; GEM skips that step because its challenge sets are meant to supply difficulty instead).

The result is the 11 datasets in Table 1: CommonGen, Czech Restaurant, DART, E2E (cleaned), MLSum, Schema-Guided Dialog, ToTTo, XSum, WebNLG, WikiAuto+Turk/ASSET, and WikiLingua, spanning four high-level tasks (Dialog, Summarization, Data-to-Text, Simplification) and 18 languages in total, with Czech Restaurant (Czech only) and MLSum (German/Spanish only) carrying no English at all. Four of the eleven were modified for GEM rather than used as-is: MLSum had non-Spanish/German languages dropped and 147 German / 7,417 Spanish duplicate or wrong-language items filtered out; XSum had its well-known summary/source divergence problem addressed by fine-tuning a BERT classifier on 500 hand-annotated document–summary pairs and discarding any pair the classifier wasn't confident (p(faithful) > 0.8) was faithful; Schema-Guided Dialog was reformatted to treat service-agent utterances as generation targets; and WikiLingua was re-split to remove train/test leakage across languages and extended with 13 additional training-only languages beyond the five cross-lingual test languages (es, ru, tr, vi, and the target, en).

On top of the datasets, GEM adds ten types of challenge sets (Table 2), built three ways: (I) perturbing existing test inputs — numerical-value substitution for WebNLG, attribute-order shuffling for all data-to-text tasks, typographical noise via "butter-fingers," punctuation removal, and back-translation round-trips for the text-to-text tasks; (II) slicing existing test sets into contrast subsets — ToTTo splits by subject gender/ethnicity/nationality, WebNLG splits by input shape, WikiAuto/Turk splits by syntactic complexity, and every dataset gets a training-frequency-based split (common → unseen input components); and (III) collecting genuinely new, time-shifted data — Covid-19-themed news articles for MLSum (es/de) and XSum, gathered after the original datasets were built, to probe how models handle topics and vocabulary absent from training (and, implicitly, from any pretraining corpus that predates the pandemic). Every dataset also gets an NLG-specific "data card," extending the Datasheets-for-Datasets (Gebru et al., 2018) and data-statements (Bender & Friedman, 2018) templates, and every future submitted model gets an accompanying model card (Mitchell et al., 2019).

For automated evaluation, GEM deliberately runs several metric families side by side rather than picking one: lexical-overlap metrics (BLEU, ROUGE-1/2/L, METEOR); semantic-equivalence metrics built on pretrained encoders (BERTScore using RoBERTa-large for English and mBERT for other languages; BLEURT, English-only via BLEURT-base-128); a QA-based faithfulness metric, QuestEval (published under the earlier title "SAFEval: Summarization Asks for Fact-based Evaluation" on arXiv, per GEM's own bibliography entry); NUBIA, an entailment-and-similarity-based interpretable metric; a battery of diversity metrics (MSTTR, Distinct-1/2, Shannon entropy over unigrams/bigrams H1/H2, Unique-1/2 n-gram counts); and plain system-characterization stats (parameter count, output vocabulary size, mean output length). The explicit point of stacking all of these rather than reporting one is captured in the paper's own rhetorical contrast: instead of "System Foo performs the best," GEM wants to be able to say "System Foo leads to consistent performance increases in Bar-type metrics on challenges that measure Baz while maintaining equal performance on most metrics of type Qux."

// what they found

The results

Because GEM's own test sets and final metric selection "have not been released yet" at the time of this paper, everything reported is a baseline run on validation data, using deliberately small, fast-to-reproduce models — the paper's main baselines are T5 at 60M parameters and BART at 139M parameters, chosen so that "almost all baselines can be reproduced on a GPU-based colaboratory notebook within 2-3 hours." Non-English and cross-lingual tasks instead use mT5 (small through XL) and mBART, and a handful of datasets get task-specific baselines: TGen (and its +/++ variants) for data-to-text generation from dialog acts, an LSTM sequence-to-sequence model for E2E, DialoGPT for dialogue, and PEGASUS for XSum summarization. This produces a genuinely uneven grid rather than a uniform leaderboard, and one corner of it looks like a scoring artifact rather than task difficulty: DART's BART and T5 rows both report ROUGE-2 of exactly 0.0 and a ROUGE-L that exactly equals ROUGE-1 (7.1 and 8.4 respectively), and Czech Restaurant's three TGen variants show the identical signature (ROUGE-2 0.0, ROUGE-L = ROUGE-1 at 13.6/13.8/9.7). The DART rows are the clear case: BERTScore reads 0.862 and 0.901 there, squarely normal, against ROUGE-1 of just 7.1 and 8.4. The TGen rows are not — their 0.650/0.651/0.648 are the three lowest BERTScores anywhere in Table 3 (the next lowest is 0.691, and everything else sits at 0.812 or above), so on those rows the semantic metric agrees with the lexical ones rather than contradicting them. What rules out the simpler explanation that TGen is just a weak generator is the same system on a different dataset: TGen on E2E clean scores ROUGE-1 74.7, ROUGE-2 49.6, BLEU 46.0 and BERTScore 0.949, with the highest BLEURT in that block (0.412). The system is fine; something about its Czech Restaurant output stream is not. The same dataset scores lexically healthy for a different system — Czech Restaurant/mT5-base gets ROUGE-1 48.1, ROUGE-2 28.8, ROUGE-L 44.2, BLEU 17.1 — so the failure tracks the baseline system, not the dataset. GEM's text doesn't flag this, so it reads as an unremarked tokenization or output-formatting mismatch sitting in Table 3 rather than a diagnosed limitation — but it's nonetheless a sharper illustration of the paper's own thesis than task difficulty would be: on DART the lexical metrics aren’t merely pessimistic, they disagree with the one metric that says the outputs are serviceable, and no single number would have revealed that. CommonGen shows a much smaller gap running the other way: BART edges out T5 on six of the seven reported metrics (METEOR 0.301 vs. 0.291, ROUGE-2 32.5 vs. 29.4, ROUGE-L 55.1 vs. 54.5, BLEU 27.5 vs. 26.4, BERTScore 0.943 vs. 0.942, BLEURT −0.400 vs. −0.412), with T5 ahead only on ROUGE-1 (64.0 vs. 63.5) — a reminder that which of two similarly-sized models "wins" flips per dataset even before GEM's harder challenge sets are applied. Schema-Guided Dialog shows the widest BLEURT spread of any dataset block Table 3 reports (1.364 points): BART's BLEURT is −1.355 against T5's essentially-neutral 0.009, with T5 also ahead on every lexical metric (ROUGE-L 52.6 vs. 11.3, BLEU 33.4 vs. 2.7) — the paper doesn't diagnose why, but the gap is large enough that it's plausible BART is simply less suited to a schema-driven dialog-act input format than T5's text-to-text framing. BLEURT cells are blank ("–") for every non-English row (Czech Restaurant, MLSum, WebNLG-ru) because the reported BLEURT baseline is English-only.

This tool, not a table, is presented as GEM's actual "results" interface: it lets a reader filter by brushing over any metric axis (shown here filtering by BLEURT) and highlight the matching lines, which the paper argues scales to far more metrics than a table could and makes cross-metric patterns visually obvious. The tabular baselines (reproduced in the paper's Tables 3 and 4) are explicitly a starting point, not the shared-task's actual results — those depend on submissions and the human-evaluation protocol GEM plans to run afterward, following Belz et al.'s (2020) human-evaluation-measure taxonomy and the reporting standards of Howcroft et al. (2020), using GENIE's evaluation infrastructure.

// does it hold up?

The eval read

The honest framing of this paper is that it is a shared-task announcement with baselines attached, not a completed benchmark — and reading it that way resolves most of what would otherwise look like gaps. The reported numbers are validation-split baselines from small, deliberately fast models chosen for reproducibility rather than strength, so nothing here should be read as "how good current NLG models are" — it's closer to "here is the evaluation surface future submissions will be measured against." Two things are worth flagging as more than framing, though. First, a genuine internal inconsistency: the paper states its 11 datasets range "from 5k to 500k data points" in the introduction, then a few pages later says the same 11 datasets range "from 5,000 examples to 1.5M" — but Table 1 itself tops out at 594k (WikiAuto+Turk/ASSET), and no dataset anywhere in the paper reaches even half of 1.5M. The floor (5k) is consistent everywhere; the ceiling is stated three different ways. It's a minor slip in a large collaborative document, but it's the kind of headline scale figure that gets quoted uncritically by later papers, so it's worth naming precisely rather than silently picking whichever number sounds right.

Second, the multilingual framing is real but concentrated: of the 18 languages GEM claims, the overwhelming majority live inside a single dataset (WikiLingua's cross-lingual set) plus MLSum (de/es) and WebNLG (en/ru); of the remaining eight datasets, seven (CommonGen, DART, E2E clean, Schema-Guided Dialog, ToTTo, XSum, WikiAuto+Turk/ASSET) are English-only and the eighth, Czech Restaurant, is Czech-only rather than English. The paper's own selection survey makes this tension explicit rather than hiding it — the authors report that "non-English datasets were ranked lower, with only WebNLG and MLSum among the top 15 datasets," meaning the community's stated preferences had to be partially overridden to hit any multilingual coverage at all. WikiLingua itself always outputs English regardless of input language, so it stretches "non-English" further than "non-English output." And the one semantic-equivalence metric with the strongest track record here, BLEURT, is English-only in this baseline release — so GEM's multilingual rows are evaluated with a strictly smaller metric toolkit (no BLEURT, BERTScore via mBERT instead of RoBERTa-large) than its English rows, right at launch.

baselines

Deliberately small and fast (T5-60M, BART-139M, mT5/mBART, plus task-specific TGen/LSTM/DialoGPT/PEGASUS), chosen for 2-3-hour reproducibility on a single GPU, not competitiveness — the paper is explicit these are placeholder baselines run on validation data ahead of the real shared-task submissions.

contamination

Not directly addressed (these are pre-existing public datasets, re-split in places like WikiLingua to avoid cross-language train/test leakage); the closest proxy is the Covid-19 time-shifted challenge data for MLSum/XSum, designed to probe distribution shift rather than to detect pretraining contamination.

what's missed

No human-evaluation results yet — those, and the final metric/dataset selection, are deferred to the actual ACL 2021 shared task. Reported scores are on validation, not test, splits. Challenge sets are capped at ~500 examples each, small enough to be noisy for fine-grained breakdowns.

would raise confidence

The eventual shared-task results with real, full-size submissions and the promised human-evaluation datasheet (Shimorina & Belz, 2021) — this paper alone can only be judged on its design, not on whether the design produces better model comparisons in practice.

// bottom line

GEM's real contribution isn't any number in Tables 3–4 — it's the argument, backed by a genuinely deliberate selection process and an unusual (for 2021) interactive multi-metric exploration tool, that NLG evaluation should stop pretending a single leaderboard score is meaningful. Whether that argument pays off depends on the shared task this paper announces, which is outside its own scope to demonstrate.

// takeaways

What to remember

  1. GEM is a living-benchmark proposal plus shared-task announcement, not a finished leaderboard: its Table 3/4 numbers are small-model (T5-60M/BART-139M) baselines on validation splits, explicitly run before GEM's own test sets and final metrics existed.
  2. Its dataset selection was a genuine multi-step process — 35 elicited candidates, a 28-response utility survey, then a knapsack-style final pick — yielding 11 datasets across Dialog, Summarization, Data-to-Text, and Simplification, plus ten types of adversarial/contrast challenge sets built by perturbing, slicing, or newly collecting (Covid-19) test data.
  3. The "18 languages" headline is real but lopsided: it's carried almost entirely by WikiLingua, MLSum, and WebNLG, while the paper's own internal survey shows the community ranked non-English datasets lower, and the launch BLEURT baseline is English-only — worth remembering before citing GEM as evidence of broadly multilingual NLG evaluation.
