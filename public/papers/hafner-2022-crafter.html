Benchmarking the Spectrum of Agent Capabilities

Crafter packs Minecraft-style foraging, crafting, and combat into one fast procedurally generated 2D world scored by 22 named achievements -- replacing costly per-game RL suites, yet the best 2021 baseline (DreamerV2, 10.0%) still lands far under 5 human experts (50.5%).

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

By 2021, the standard way to evaluate a "general" reinforcement-learning agent was to run it across a whole suite of narrow environments. The Arcade Learning Environment covers around 54 Atari games, but the recommended protocol -- 5 seeds per game, 200M steps per seed -- costs over 2,000 GPU-days, and Atari games are nearly deterministic, so an agent can approximately memorize an action sequence instead of generalizing. ProcGen fixes the determinism problem with procedurally generated levels, but still asks for 200M steps across 16 separate games, each testing one narrow slice of behavior. Minecraft, via Malmo or MineRL, offers the open-world generalization and crafting depth the paper wants, but is "too complex to be solved by current methods," runs slowly, needs Java and a window server, and has no agreed-upon evaluation metric. NetHack swaps in symbolic text observations and used 1B environment steps in its original paper, so it sidesteps the visual-representation-learning challenge entirely. In every case, evaluating a wide spectrum of abilities means expensive training runs spread across many separate environments -- which is itself a barrier to iterating quickly on new methods.

Crafter's answer is to fold many of these individual challenges -- generalization, wide and deep exploration, long-term credit assignment, memory, representation learning from images, and survival under real pressure (not just "do nothing and survive," which many prior environments allow) -- into a single environment that a research group can train on with a 1M-step budget, under 24 hours on one GPU, and read out through 22 separate, semantically meaningful achievements rather than one opaque scalar.

// how it works

The method

Crafter is a 2D open-world survival game, mechanically inspired by Minecraft but simplified for research productivity. Every episode procedurally generates a fresh 64×64-cell world -- grasslands, forests, lakes, mountains, and caves, laid out via OpenSimplex noise -- so an agent cannot memorize a fixed layout and must instead generalize to a new map every time. The agent observes only a small local 7×9-cell top-down pixel window (64×64×3 image) that also renders its inventory (health, food, water, rest, collected materials, crafted tools) as part of the image, and must read that state visually rather than receive it as a separate vector. It acts through a flat, 17-action discrete space covering movement, interacting with whatever is in front of it, sleeping, placing objects, and crafting.

Survival itself is a real constraint, not a free option: food, water, and rest levels decay over time and must be replenished (eating cows or grown fruit, drinking from lakes, sleeping safely), and letting any of them hit zero starts draining health; zombies spawn in grasslands and multiply at night, while skeletons in caves shoot arrows from range. Collecting resources and crafting tools follows a technology tree with real dependencies: reaching a diamond requires an iron pickaxe, which requires a furnace and a table, which requires stone and wood, and so on down the chain shown in the paper's own dependency diagram.

Three more world details matter for the difficulty curve shown later in the results: every episode ends when health hits zero (from starvation, thirst, exhaustion, a monster, or lava) or after a 10,000-step cap; there is no separate death penalty because the health-loss penalty already covers it; and Crafter exposes privileged debug state (exact inventory counts, coordinates, a semantic map) that the agent is not allowed to observe but that researchers can use to probe representations.

Evaluation runs in two parallel protocols that share everything except reward access: a reward benchmark, where the agent receives Crafter's built-in signal, and an unsupervised benchmark, where it receives none and must be driven purely by an intrinsic objective. Both grant a fixed budget of 1M environment steps -- deliberately capped, since a larger budget "would result in a comparison of compute resources rather than algorithm quality." Two numbers come out of a run: the mean episode return (Crafter's reward is the sum of +1 for each achievement's first unlock in an episode, plus ±0.1 per health point regenerated/lost, so ceiling the return recovers the achievement count) and, more importantly, a per-achievement success rate -- the fraction of training episodes (within the 1M-step budget) in which that achievement was unlocked at least once.

The paper's headline contribution is how it collapses those 22 success rates into one number. Rather than averaging them arithmetically -- which would let an agent inflate its score by polishing already-easy achievements -- it takes their geometric mean, computed in log-space, so that going from 0% to 1% on a hard achievement (say, Collect Diamond) moves the score more than going from 90% to 95% on an easy one (say, Collect Wood). A +1 offset inside the logarithm keeps achievements an agent never unlocks (0% success) from making the whole product blow up to zero or undefined:

S = exp(1N  Σi=1..N ln(1 + si)) − 1 // s_i = agent's success rate (%) on achievement i, N = 22; from Section 3.3, footnote 2

The paper is explicit that this design choice can change an agent's ranking relative to reward: "capacity-limited agents may choose to optimize reward by mastering easy tasks and ignoring hard tasks, which only slowly increases the geometric mean." The score is also meant to give unsupervised agents a meaningful target, since an intrinsic-motivation agent that explores many achievements once and then loses interest still scores well, whereas raw reward would not reward that kind of exploration once the novelty bonus fades.

// what they found

The results

On the reward benchmark, the paper trains three algorithms for 1M steps over 10 seeds each: DreamerV2 (a world model with default Atari hyperparameters and an increased model size), PPO (a CNN policy with hyperparameters tuned for Atari, left unchanged), and Rainbow (whose Atari defaults "did not work well," so its hyperparameters were specifically re-tuned for Crafter). DreamerV2 comes out ahead with a score of 10.0% ± 1.2, followed by PPO at 4.6% ± 0.3 and Rainbow at 4.3% ± 0.2. On the unsupervised benchmark, Plan2Explore (a Dreamer-based world model that plans for expected information gain) scores 2.1% ± 0.1, ahead of RND at 2.0% ± 0.1 and both ahead of a Random-action baseline at 1.6% ± 0.0. Against a dataset of 5 human experts playing 100 recorded episodes total, humans score 50.5% ± 6.8 -- roughly 5× the best learned agent.

The paper also reports episode return alongside score, and the two metrics don't always agree on ranking: Rainbow's return (5.0 ± 1.3) is actually higher than PPO's (4.2 ± 1.2), even though Rainbow's score (4.3%) trails PPO's (4.6%) -- the paper attributes this to return being less sensitive to rare, hard achievements than the geometric-mean score is.

The per-achievement breakdown (Appendix A/B) shows exactly where that 10% ceiling comes from -- with one reading caveat: the appendix tables round success rates to one decimal place, while Figures 6-7 plot the same rates on a log axis floored at 0.01%, so a 0.0% table cell means "below 0.05%," not a true zero. Every reward-based agent -- including DreamerV2 -- scored 0.0% on Collect Diamond, Collect Iron, Make Iron Pickaxe, and Make Iron Sword: none of them has any visible bar there in Figure 6 either, so nothing registers even at that figure's 0.01% floor -- matching §4.1's own summary that no agent learned to collect and use iron or to collect diamonds. DreamerV2's edge over PPO and Rainbow shows up earlier in the chain instead -- it collects coal 14.7% of the time (vs. 0.4% for PPO and 0.0% for Rainbow), reaches 42.7% on Collect Stone (vs. 3.0% and 0.2%), places a furnace 1.8% of the time (vs. PPO's 0.1% and Rainbow's 0.0%), and is the only reward agent with a non-zero stone-tool entry in Table A.1 (Make Stone Pickaxe 0.2%, Make Stone Sword 0.3%, vs. 0.0% for Rainbow and PPO) -- though Figure 6 shows PPO crafting stone tools too, just below the table's 0.05% rounding threshold (its own caption notes "PPO additionally rarely collects coal and builds stone tools"), while Rainbow has no bar there at all. Unsupervised agents (Table B.1) get only a few steps into the tech tree -- RND places a furnace 0.1% of the time, and RND/Plan2Explore each occasionally collect coal (0.1%) and build wood tools (2.5-3.3%) -- and stone tools stay at 0.0% in Table B.1 for all three (Plan2Explore does make the occasional stone sword -- §4.2's prose and a small bar in Figure 7 confirm it, at a rate too rare to round above 0.0%) and never reach iron or diamond (Collect Diamond and every iron-related achievement stay at 0.0% for all three unsupervised/random agents, with no bars at all in Figure 7 either). The human-expert breakdown (Appendix C) shows where the difficulty actually concentrates for people too: even experts only reach 12% on Collect Diamond and 8% on eating a grown plant, versus a perfect 100% on Collect Stone, Collect Wood, Make Wood Pickaxe, and Place Table -- and only 5 of the 100 recorded human episodes unlocked all 22 achievements.

Separately from the fixed 1M-step benchmark, the paper trains DreamerV2 alone for 50M steps and reports qualitative emergent behaviors: building tunnel systems and bridges, digging and sealing its own shelters against night-time zombies, dodging or blocking skeleton arrows, and maintaining plantations of saplings as a steady food source.

// does it hold up?

The eval read

Crafter's core idea -- one fast environment standing in for a whole suite, scored by a difficulty-weighted aggregate instead of one raw number -- is a genuine and influential contribution (it's now a component of later benchmarks like SmartPlay and BALROG, and a standard testbed for later world models such as DreamerV3). But a few things are worth flagging when reading the baseline numbers as a fixed reference point.

baselines

The three reward baselines were not given equal tuning effort by the paper's own account: DreamerV2 kept its Atari defaults but got "increased model size"; PPO kept Atari-tuned hyperparameters (Hill et al., 2018) unchanged; only Rainbow was specifically re-tuned for Crafter, and only because its untouched Atari defaults "did not work well." DreamerV2 is also the sole author's own prior architecture -- and so, less visibly, is the unsupervised winner: the paper's own bibliography lists Hafner as a co-author of Plan2Explore (Sekar et al., 2020), which tops the unsupervised table at 2.1%. Both comparisons are therefore won by the author's own prior work. None of this means the 10.0% vs. 4.3-4.6% gap is wrong, but it does mean the comparison isn't a clean equal-effort bake-off -- a capacity bump for one method and fresh tuning for another (but not a third) is a confound the paper doesn't control for.

contamination

Not applicable in the usual train/test-corpus sense -- Crafter is a simulator, not a static dataset. Its actual analogue is level memorization, which the paper addresses by design (a fresh OpenSimplex-noise seed per episode means an agent can't memorize a fixed map). That's a designed defense, though, not a measured one: the paper reports no held-out-seed generalization check, so whether agents are actually generalizing across the seed distribution rather than overfitting to its statistical regularities (e.g. typical resource density or terrain adjacency) isn't directly tested here.

what's missed

The single Score number mixes a near-ceiling head (Wake Up runs 92.5-93.6% for the three reward agents and Random, but only 7.8% for RND and 47.8% for Plan2Explore; Collect Wood 75-93% for the three reward agents) with a near-zero tail (every iron-related achievement and Collect Diamond at 0.0% for all six learned agents), so two very different failure modes -- "doesn't explore broadly" vs. "explores broadly but can't get past mid-tree" -- collapse into one percentage unless a reader also opens the per-achievement appendix tables. Also worth noting: the "sophisticated emergent behaviors" the abstract leads with (tunnels, bridges, plantations) come from a separate DreamerV2 run at 50M steps -- 50× the 1M-step budget the actual benchmark scores are measured on -- so they illustrate what the environment can eventually produce, not what the reported 10.0% score reflects.

would raise confidence

A same-effort tuning budget applied identically across all reward baselines; an explicit generalization check across disjoint seed ranges; a larger human-expert panel than 5 players (100 episodes total) for what functions as the paper's headline difficulty anchor -- note too that the human score's ±6.8 is not between-player variance at all, since Appendix C computes it by randomly splitting the pooled 100 games into 5 groups "that are treated as the different seeds," so it measures partition noise over one pooled sample and says nothing about how much individual humans differ; and baselines drawn from exploration methods contemporaneous with the paper's own related-work discussion (e.g. Agent57) rather than only Atari-era Rainbow/PPO/DreamerV2/RND/Plan2Explore.

// bottom line

The achievement-and-geometric-mean design is a real methodological advance for aggregating agent ability across many correlated sub-tasks, and the paper is candid about its own limits (it explicitly declines to group achievements into ability categories like memory or exploration, calling that grouping "subjective"). The baseline numbers, though, mix a same-author model with two externally tuned-to-different-degrees baselines, and the score's single-number framing is easy to over-read without the per-achievement tables behind it.

// takeaways

What to remember

  1. Crafter trades "many narrow environments" (54 Atari games at ~2,000 GPU-days, or ProcGen's 16 games × 200M steps) for one procedurally generated 2D survival game, solvable within a 1M-step budget in under 24 hours on a single GPU, read out through 22 named achievements instead of one game-specific score.
  2. The headline metric is a geometric mean of per-achievement success rates, S = exp((1/22)·Σ ln(1+s_i)) − 1, deliberately weighted so that rare, hard achievements move the score more than polishing an already-easy one -- and it doesn't always rank agents the same way raw episode return does (Rainbow out-returns PPO, 5.0 vs. 4.2, while still scoring lower, 4.3% vs. 4.6%).
  3. Even the best 2021 baseline, DreamerV2 at 10.0% ± 1.2%, sits far below the 50.5% ± 6.8% scored by 5 human experts across 100 recorded episodes -- and every learned agent tested (reward-based or unsupervised) scored a flat 0.0% on Collect Diamond and every iron-tool achievement, the deepest branch of Crafter's technology tree.
