LegalAgentBench: Evaluating LLM Agents in Legal Domain

A 300-task, tool-using agent benchmark built over 17 real Chinese legal databases and 37 tools — scored not only on final-answer success but on a keyword-based "process rate" that checks whether the answer also surfaces the intermediate facts a correct solution path would need.

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

General-purpose agent benchmarks like AgentBench and ToolBench test whether an LLM agent can plan, call tools, and recover from errors — but they do it in domains (operating systems, databases, web APIs) where getting the "gist" right is often good enough. The authors argue legal work doesn't tolerate that kind of slack: a case number parsed one digit wrong, a filing date confused with a trial date, or a court jurisdiction misattributed isn't a minor miss, it's a wrong answer with real consequences. Existing legal-domain evaluations, meanwhile, tend to stop at narrower tasks — legal case retrieval or judgment prediction — that don't require an agent to chain together multiple tools against a live knowledge base the way real legal research does.

LegalAgentBench targets that gap directly: it is, by the authors' framing, the first benchmark built specifically to evaluate LLM agents — not just LLMs — in legal scenarios, requiring models to select and sequence real tools over real Chinese legal data sources rather than answer from parametric knowledge alone. A second, related gap the paper calls out: most agent benchmarks report only whether the final answer was correct, which can't distinguish an agent that got 90% of the way there from one that never engaged with the problem at all.

// how it works

The method

LegalAgentBench is built from three pieces: an environment of 17 real-world corpora, a set of 37 tools for interacting with it, and 300 tasks that require chaining those tools together. Everything is grounded in Chinese law and Chinese legal data — 14 of the 17 corpora are tabular databases (company registrations, court directories, law-firm records, enforcement/dishonesty case lists, administrative penalty records) sourced from real, publicly available Chinese government and corporate filings, and the remaining 3 are retrieval corpora: 26,951 entries of legal knowledge (drawn from legal books), 55,347 legislatively enacted legal articles, and 2,370 officially published guiding cases. The 37 tools break down as 3 text retrievers (backed by Zhipu AI's Embedding-3), 5 arithmetic tools (sum, subtraction, multiplication, division, ranking), 28 database-lookup tools tied to the specific tabular corpora, and a single finish tool the agent calls to submit its answer.

Tasks are generated through a scalable, six-step pipeline (Figure 2 below): the authors first build a planning tree from the call relationships between tools and corpora, where each node is an entity and each edge a tool that can be applied to reach the next entity. They then perform path selection across the tree to sample tasks at controlled depths — 1-hop through 5-hop serial chains, plus parallel-path "writing" tasks that require querying several independent pieces of information at once (e.g., drafting a legal defense document, which needs the plaintiff's, defendant's, and their lawyer's information gathered in parallel alongside retrieved legal knowledge). Entity selection picks concrete entities (company names, case numbers, court names) that actually resolve along the chosen path. The resulting templated question ("What is entity3 of entity2 of entity1?") is then rewritten by GPT-4 into a more natural-sounding query that obscures the underlying solution path, and an answer is generated programmatically by walking the known toolchain. Finally, every task — question, path, and answer — goes through human verification.

An agent solves a task by iteratively picking an action at (a tool call or a final answer), receiving an observation ot from the environment, and updating its internal state st, up to a maximum of T = 10 iterations before it must submit via finish. Each of the 300 tasks carries two keyword sets used only for evaluation, never shown to the agent as gold hints during solving: a key_answer set (keywords a correct final answer must contain, extracted from the tool-call results along the gold solution path) and a key_middle set (keywords extracted the same way from the intermediate tool-call results on that path). Both sets are checked against the same thing — the agent's final-answer text — not against its trajectory; key_middle just enlarges the target vocabulary the answer is scored against. Figure 1 shows one full worked example — a query about which courts share a jurisdiction with the Beijing First Intermediate People's Court, its key_answer (the two correct court names), its key_middle (the intermediate jurisdiction the agent needs to have looked up), and its solution Path (get_court_info → get_court_info_list).

Scoring is keyword-based, not exact-match and not an LLM judge. For a task with keyword set Ki and model output Oi, the paper defines a rate

si = |Mi||Ki| // M_i = the subset of K_i that appears in the agent's output O_i

where setting Ki = key_answer gives the success rate (did the final answer contain the required keywords), and setting Ki = key_answer ∪ key_middle gives the process rate (does that same final-answer text Oi also contain the intermediate-step keywords a correct solution path would surface, not just the key_answer keywords). The paper names this metric both ways — “process rate” in the method section, “progress rate” in the appendix and abstract, the latter more often — so a reader searching for it should try both. A third metric, BERTScore, measures semantic similarity between the generated and reference answer text; the paper reports it in the appendix but notes it is less discriminative across baselines than the two keyword-based rates, so it recommends success rate and process rate as the primary metrics. The 300 tasks split into six task types — 1-hop (80), 2-hop (80), 3-hop (60), 4-hop (40), 5-hop (20), and Writing (20) — with average query lengths (Table 2 does not state the unit) ranging from 88–118 for the hop-based tasks up to 1,060 for Writing tasks, which also carry far more key_answer keywords per query (10.25 on average, versus 1.2–2.25 for the hop tasks).

Eight LLMs were evaluated — GLM-4, GLM-4-Plus, LLaMA-3.1-8B-Instruct, Qwen-Max, Claude-3.5-Sonnet (2024-10-22), GPT-3.5-turbo (1106), GPT-4o-mini (2024-07-18), and GPT-4o (2024-08-06), all at temperature 0 — each run under three agent scaffolds: Plan-and-Solve (one upfront plan, executed step by step), Plan-and-Execute (a plan that can be revised after each step), and ReAct (interleaved thought/action/observation, with no separate planning phase).

// what they found

The results

Across all 24 model×scaffold combinations, the single best result on the headline success-rate metric is GPT-4o under ReAct, at 79.08% aggregate over all 300 tasks — and it gets there while consuming noticeably fewer tokens (about 11.2M) than the next-strongest agents. GLM-4-Plus and Qwen-Max under ReAct follow at 74.99% and 74.22% respectively. At the other end, GPT-3.5-turbo and LLaMA-3.1-8B-Instruct never exceed a 29.86% aggregate success rate under any scaffold (GPT-3.5's best ALL-column score is 29.86% with ReAct; LLaMA-3.1-8B's best is 23.59%, also with ReAct) — both stay below 30% in aggregate across all three scaffolds.

Difficulty scales with hop count, though not perfectly monotonically for every single model×scaffold row: the best 1-hop success rate in the table is GPT-4o-mini/ReAct at 93.33%, while the best 5-hop success rate is GPT-4o/ReAct at 61.17% — a real difficulty gradient, even though a few individual rows buck the trend (GLM-4 under Plan-and-Solve, for instance, actually rises from an 18.06% 3-hop success rate to 37.08% at 4-hop). Choice of agent scaffold matters a great deal and interacts with model capability: ReAct is typically the strongest scaffold for multi-hop reasoning but is also the most token-hungry — Claude-3.5-Sonnet's ReAct run burns roughly 32.9M tokens versus 7.1M and 13.6M for its Plan-and-Solve and Plan-and-Execute runs on the same 300 tasks. The single largest scaffold-driven swing for one model on one task type is GPT-4o at 3-hop: 27.50% success under Plan-and-Solve versus 75.00% under ReAct, a 47.5-point spread. The Writing tasks are where ReAct's advantage reverses for several — though not most — models: for GPT-4o, ReAct's 65.41% Writing success rate trails both Plan-and-Solve (79.71%) and Plan-and-Execute (86.43%); the same pattern holds for Claude-3.5-Sonnet (65.67% under ReAct vs. 83.95%/86.10%), GPT-4o-mini, and LLaMA-3.1-8B. The other four models (GLM-4, GLM-4-Plus, Qwen-Max, GPT-3.5) actually do best or near-best on Writing under ReAct, so this is a real but partial effect, which the authors attribute to ReAct's step-by-step correction loop being a poor fit for tasks that need several pieces of information gathered in parallel.

The paper's secondary metric, process rate (which credits an agent for surfacing the right intermediate facts, not just the right final answer), tells a partly different story about which model is actually working the problem correctly. GLM-4-Plus's aggregate ("ALL") process rate beats GPT-4o's under every one of the three scaffolds — 63.04% vs. 54.74% (Plan-and-Solve), 64.16% vs. 57.93% (Plan-and-Execute), and 72.80% vs. 71.99% (ReAct) — even though GPT-4o's aggregate success rate under ReAct (79.08%) clears GLM-4-Plus's (74.99%). In other words, on this reading GLM-4-Plus's answers are relatively more likely to surface the right intermediate facts even when its final-answer accuracy trails GPT-4o's. That said, GLM-4-Plus's process-rate lead over GPT-4o does not hold in every individual hop bucket — under ReAct, for instance, GPT-4o's 1-hop process rate (93.44%) edges out GLM-4-Plus's (93.23%), and under Plan-and-Execute GPT-4o's Writing process rate (67.48%) beats GLM-4-Plus's (64.01%). The third metric, BERTScore, compresses the spread between baselines considerably (most ALL-column scores cluster in the high 0.6s–0.8s regardless of model or scaffold), which is exactly why the paper leans on success rate and process rate rather than BERTScore for its main comparisons.

Beyond the headline numbers, the paper's error analysis (§4.3) identifies recurring failure modes: agents confusing filing date with trial time, misreading structured fields inside case numbers, and generally failing to recognize when a query requires consulting the legal-knowledge corpus rather than the model's own parametric knowledge. It also catalogs four categories of trajectory-level errors: argument errors (wrong tool-call parameters), planning errors (wrong tool or wrong sequence), exceeding the length limit, and getting stuck in repetition loops until the T=10 iteration cap is hit.

// does it hold up?

The eval read

The most important framing point for any reader outside China: this is a Chinese-law benchmark through and through. Every corpus is Chinese government/corporate data (company registrations, Chinese court and law-firm directories, Chinese statutory articles and guiding cases), the task construction targets China's statutory-law system specifically, and the underlying queries and answers were originally written in Chinese before being translated for the paper's figures. The authors are explicit about this in their own limitations section: the dataset "is primarily constructed in Chinese," they plan an English-supporting version in future work, and they flag that "the task design primarily covers statutory law system, and its performance in case law systems requires further exploration." None of the paper's claims about "the legal domain" should be read as evidence about common-law systems, English-language legal tasks, or jurisdictions outside China — the benchmark is a serious, real-data instrument for evaluating agents on Chinese statutory legal work specifically, not a general-purpose legal-agent benchmark.

The keyword-based grading (success rate / process rate) is a real methodological choice worth scrutinizing rather than a neutral default. It sidesteps the well-known brittleness of exact match on free-text legal writing, but keyword recall can still be gamed by an agent that pads its answer with plausible-sounding entity names, court names, or case numbers it half-retrieved — the metric checks whether the right tokens appear, not whether the surrounding reasoning that produced them was sound. The key_middle / process-rate mechanism does not close this gap the way it might first appear to: process rate is scored against the exact same final-answer text as success rate, just against a larger keyword set (key_answer ∪ key_middle) — it is not a trajectory audit, so it cannot by itself confirm the agent actually called the intermediate tools rather than dropping plausible-sounding intermediate entity or jurisdiction names into its final answer. The paper does not report how often an agent could satisfy key_middle by chance token overlap versus by actually executing the correct tool call — there's no manual audit quantifying false-positive keyword matches on either rate.

On baselines: eight LLMs across three agent scaffolds is a reasonably broad sweep for a first benchmark paper, and it does include both a leading closed-source frontier model (GPT-4o) and a small open-weight model (LLaMA-3.1-8B-Instruct) as bookends, which is useful for showing the benchmark actually discriminates capability. But all three scaffolds — Plan-and-Solve, Plan-and-Execute, ReAct — are 2022–2023-era prompting strategies; there's no comparison against a fine-tuned or specifically legal-domain-adapted agent, nor against more recent planning/tool-use methods, so it's unclear how much headroom remains above the reported 79.08% ceiling versus how much is an artifact of using relatively simple, generic scaffolds.

One descriptive note on Table 3 itself, since it's the paper's central evidence: the paper's own prose (§4.2) states that "the performance gap between different reasoning methods for the same LLM can reach 65%," but the largest such gap actually present in the table is 47.5 points (GPT-4o at 3-hop: ReAct's 75.00% versus Plan-and-Solve's 27.50%) — no cell-to-cell reading of Table 3 reaches 65 percentage points.

Contamination risk is addressed structurally rather than empirically: the underlying corpora are described as real-world data that "can evolve over time, mitigating the risk of LLM overfitting," and the tasks are synthetically assembled by the authors' own pipeline (with GPT-4 used only for cosmetic question rewriting, and a real toolchain used to derive gold answers) rather than scraped from a source a model could have memorized wholesale. That's a reasonable structural argument, but the paper reports no direct contamination check (e.g., no measurement of whether frontier models can answer a nontrivial fraction of tasks with tools disabled, which would indicate memorization of the underlying source documents rather than genuine tool-mediated reasoning).

baselines

Eight models × three prompting scaffolds (Plan-and-Solve, Plan-and-Execute, ReAct) — all generic, pre-2024 agent scaffolds; no legal-domain-tuned or more recent planning method is compared.

contamination

Argued structurally (real, evolving source data; synthetically assembled tasks) rather than measured directly — no reported no-tool-access control to check for memorization of the underlying legal documents.

what's missed

Keyword-overlap scoring cannot fully distinguish genuine tool-mediated reasoning from an answer that happens to contain the right entity names; the paper reports no audit of false-positive keyword matches on either success rate or process rate.

would raise confidence

A no-tools memorization control, a human/LLM-judge spot-check of keyword-matched answers for actual correctness, and a comparison scaffold from 2024–2025 rather than only 2022–2023 prompting strategies.

// bottom line

A well-constructed, real-data agent benchmark for Chinese statutory legal work, with a genuinely useful two-tier (success/process) evaluation design — but it is a Chinese-law-specific instrument, not a general legal-agent benchmark, and its keyword-based grading is validated structurally rather than audited empirically.

// takeaways

What to remember

  1. LegalAgentBench is a 300-task, tool-using agent benchmark over 17 real Chinese legal corpora (14 tabular databases, 3 retrieval corpora spanning 26,951 knowledge passages, 55,347 legal articles, and 2,370 guiding cases) and 37 tools, built entirely around Chinese statutory law.
  2. Grading is keyword-based, not exact-match or LLM-judged: a success rate checks the final answer against a gold key_answer keyword set, while a process rate checks that same final answer against an expanded key_answer ∪ key_middle set — giving credit for surfacing the right intermediate facts even when some key_answer keywords are missing.
  3. GPT-4o under ReAct posts the best success rate (79.08% aggregate, with comparatively low token use), but GLM-4-Plus's process rate exceeds GPT-4o's under all three scaffolds tested — a reminder that scoring the answer against the final keywords alone and scoring it against the intermediate keywords too don't always rank models the same way.
