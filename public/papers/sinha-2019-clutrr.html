CLUTRR: A Diagnostic Benchmark for Inductive Reasoning from Text

A procedurally generated kinship-reasoning benchmark that trains on short backward-chained stories and tests on longer ones, isolating systematic generalization from pattern matching — a Graph Attention Network fed the symbolic story graph reaches 1.0 accuracy at the training clause length, while the best text model at that length, BERT-LSTM, peaks around 0.9 at that length and, along with plain BERT (weaker throughout, peaking near 0.5), degrades to roughly 0.15-0.3 by ten reasoning steps.

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

By 2019 the NLU benchmark landscape was crowded with reading-comprehension and inference datasets — SQuAD, NewsQA, MCTest, SNLI, MultiNLI — that had already been shown to reward the wrong thing. Gururangan et al. (2018) found NLI models exploiting annotation artifacts rather than reasoning about entailment; Jia and Liang (2017) showed adversarial insertions collapse SQuAD performance; Kaushik and Lipton (2018) argued several "reading comprehension" datasets could be solved without reading much of the passage at all. Meanwhile BERT and ELMo were topping leaderboards on exactly these benchmarks, which the authors read as evidence that the datasets mostly test a model's grip on natural-language statistics rather than its capacity for systematic, compositional inference.

The specific failure mode the paper targets is systematic generalization: can a model that has learned individual logical rules compose them correctly on combinations it has never seen, and does its accuracy hold up as the required reasoning chain gets longer? Existing knowledge-graph QA benchmarks (Das et al. 2018; Xiong et al. 2017, 2018) were closer in spirit but almost all transductive — reasoning over a fixed, already-known set of entities — rather than requiring inference over a fresh, previously unseen set of entities every time, which is what CLUTRR calls inductive reasoning. The gap, concretely: no existing benchmark let researchers dial the exact number of reasoning steps a test example requires, hold out specific rule combinations from training, and add controlled, taxonomized noise — all while keeping the surface form natural language rather than a synthetic template language like the bAbI tasks.

// how it works

The method

CLUTRR (Compositional Language Understanding and Text-based Relational Reasoning) is a semi-synthetic data generator, not a fixed dataset — its benchmark suite ships the generator code plus a set of concrete datasets produced by it. Each example asks a model to infer an unstated kinship relation between two people mentioned in a short story. Generation follows four steps (Figure 2): (1) sample a random "kinship graph" of entities and family relations via a stochastic backbone process, then close it under a small, deterministic knowledge base of resolution rules (16 rules total, covering grandparent/child/sibling/in-law/aunt-uncle-niece-nephew relations, given in Appendix 1.2); (2) sample a target fact HC — the relation to be predicted; (3) run a fixed-iteration variant of backward chaining for k steps from HC, uniformly sampling a subgoal and a resolving KB rule at each step, to collect a set of k supporting facts BC that imply HC (crucially, the chain runs for exactly k iterations rather than stopping once a proof is found, which is what lets the authors dial reasoning-chain length precisely); (4) convert the sampled facts into a natural-language story via Amazon Mechanical Turk paraphrasing.

Because paraphrasing every possible fact combination for large k is combinatorially infeasible, the authors crowd-sourced paraphrases only for base clauses of length k = 1, 2, 3 (6,016 unique paraphrases total, peer-reviewed by other Turkers with a 79% pass rate — Table 1), then reused and stitched these template paraphrases together with placeholder substitution to synthesize longer stories for k up to 10. The task itself is posed as K-way classification over relation labels (not free-form question answering), specifically to avoid leaking answer information through a generated question, per Kaushik and Lipton's critique of overly informative questions. Entity names are drawn from a pool of 300 gendered English names and Cloze-style anonymized (replaced with @entity-k placeholders, following Hermann et al. 2015) so that a model cannot exploit fixed name-to-answer correlations.

The generator's modularity gives it two independent diagnostic axes. Systematic generalization is tested three ways: holding out a subset of paraphrases so test stories use unseen linguistic templates; holding out a subset of logical clause combinations (for k>2) so test stories require unseen rule compositions; and, most aggressively, training only on clause lengths up to some k and testing on strictly longer clauses the model has never encountered in any form. Robustness is tested by injecting extra, unnecessary "noise" facts into a story, taxonomized by how they relate to the target path pC in the kinship graph (Figure 4): supporting facts share both endpoints with pC (and so, unlike the other two types, introduce a cycle and an alternative — if longer — proof path); irrelevant facts share exactly one endpoint (a pure distractor attached to one of the two target entities); and disconnected facts share no endpoint at all.

Seven baselines are evaluated: a bidirectional LSTM (with and without attention pooling), Relation Networks (Santoro et al. 2017), MAC (Hudson and Manning 2018, the then-state-of-the-art model on CLEVR), vanilla frozen BERT, a BERT-LSTM hybrid (a trainable LSTM on top of frozen BERT embeddings), and a Graph Attention Network (Veličković et al. 2018) that — unlike every other baseline — is given the story's underlying kinship graph directly as structured input rather than having to parse it out of text. All non-BERT models are trained from scratch (no pretrained word embeddings), for 100 epochs with Adam at lr=0.001, on 5,000 training stories per clause length and evaluated on 100 test stories per length, averaged over 10 runs with different randomly generated stories (so the reported ± is a standard error across regenerated datasets and re-training, not just re-seeding one fixed dataset).

// what they found

The results

The headline systematic-generalization experiment (Figure 5) trains every model on clause lengths k=2,3 (or, in a second regime, k=2,3,4) and tests on k=2 through 10. GAT is the only model that is near-perfect at the training-distribution length: it reaches 1.0 accuracy at k=2 and stays close to it through k=3, whereas every text-based model is already well below 1.0 at k=2 — BERT-LSTM is the strongest of the six at roughly 0.9 accuracy at k=2, with plain BERT the weakest at roughly 0.5. As clause length grows past the training distribution, GAT degrades the most steeply in relative terms but starts from the highest point and remains the top performer through about k=5-6 in the k=2,3 training regime before the text models' curves cross above it; by k=10, all models converge into a similar low band, with BERT-LSTM among the group still sitting around 0.3 and BERT around 0.15. Training on the wider k=2,3,4 regime lifts every model's generalization curve, and the paper notes GAT gains the most from the expanded training distribution of the seven.

The robustness results (Table 2) train and test on k=2,3 combined, under seven Training→Testing noise-type pairings (Clean/Clean, Clean/Supporting, Clean/Irrelevant, Clean/Disconnected, Supporting/Supporting, Irrelevant/Irrelevant, Disconnected/Disconnected), each averaged over 10 runs:

GAT reaches the highest average across these seven conditions (0.77 ± 0.09); among the six text-based baselines, BiLSTM-Attention and MAC tie for the best average at 0.61 each (bold in the paper's own table). But the pattern is not simply "GAT wins everywhere": when supporting noise is added, GAT's Clean→Supporting accuracy collapses to 0.24 — the worst score of all seven models in that row — because a supporting fact closes a cycle in the kinship graph and creates an alternative, longer proof path that a model trained only on clean, acyclic graphs has never seen. The paper attributes this specifically to GAT's message-passing being confused by the new cycle. GAT recovers sharply once it is trained with supporting noise present (Supporting→Supporting reaches 0.98), so the failure is about distribution mismatch, not an inherent GAT weakness. By contrast, on Clean→Disconnected, GAT's score (0.80) is its second-highest among the four Clean-trained rows specifically (behind only Clean→Clean's 1.0) — disconnected facts sit in an isolated clique that message-passing never has to traverse, so they cost GAT comparatively little; across all seven conditions in the table, though, that same 0.80 ranks only fifth, well below the three noise-matched training rows (0.98, 0.96, 0.93).

The paper describes this pattern in two sentences that need to be read together, not in isolation: "All the text-based models excluding BERT actually perform better when testing on examples that have supporting or irrelevant facts added" than on Clean→Clean, immediately followed by "In contrast, the BERT-based models do not benefit from the inclusion of this extra content" — plural, and the appendix defines two BERT variants, vanilla BERT and BERT-LSTM. Checked cell-by-cell against Table 2's Clean-trained row: BiLSTM-Attention (0.58→0.76 Supporting, →0.70 Irrelevant), BiLSTM-Mean (0.53→0.64, →0.76), RN (0.49→0.58, →0.59), and MAC (0.63→0.71, →0.69) all improve on both noise types. BERT-LSTM does not: it goes from 0.67±0.03 on Clean→Clean to 0.66±0.06 on Clean→Supporting (a 0.01 gap that sits entirely inside the printed error bars) and 0.55±0.03 on Clean→Irrelevant. But that is exactly what the paper's own second, more careful sentence already predicts — both BERT-based models, not just plain BERT, fail to benefit from the extra content, which the authors attribute to their already-strong frozen pretrained representations (BERT itself: 0.37 → 0.28/0.24/0.24). Read as the two sentences intend, the claim holds for all six non-GAT baselines; only a literal single-model reading of "excluding BERT" makes BERT-LSTM look like a counterexample.

Appendix human-performance data (Table 5) puts the task's ceiling in perspective: time-limited Mechanical Turk annotators score 0.848 and 0.773 accuracy at k=2 and k=3 respectively, dropping to 0.477/0.424/0.406 at k=4/5/6 — but expert annotators given unrestricted time solve 100% of examples at every tested length (2 through 6), taking an average of about six minutes per puzzle. Turker-rated perceived difficulty (1-5 Likert) rises from 1.49 at k=2 to 4.46 at k=6 (with a small, likely noise-level dip from 3.81 at k=4 to 3.78 at k=5). So the degradation the neural models show as k grows is not purely an artifact of the task becoming unsolvable — a human with enough attention and time gets every example right regardless of length.

// does it hold up?

The eval read

CLUTRR's central design choice — an infinite, parameterized generator rather than a fixed test set — is exactly the property that makes it hold up well against contamination concerns that plague static benchmarks: a new sample with a new random kinship graph, new random entity names, and a new random paraphrase composition can be drawn at any time, so a leaderboard can't simply be memorized from a frozen released split. The paper is explicit that the released datasets in Table 6 (a handful of concrete "data <hash>" variants at fixed k combinations) are just one sampling from a generator whose code is public — that is unusually forthright for 2019, predating the "dynamic evaluation" framing that later papers like DyVal make explicit.

That said, several caveats limit how far the headline claim ("neural language models lack systematic generalization; a graph-structured model doesn't") should be read. First, the comparison between GAT and the text-based models is not apples-to-apples in what information each model receives: GAT is handed the ground-truth symbolic kinship graph directly, while every other model must first parse that structure out of a natural-language story — the paper's own follow-up experiment (Appendix 1.3, "modified train and test splits") confirms this by re-running the generalization test with the same paraphrases shared between train and test, at which point the text-based models become "competitive with the GAT model," confirming that most of the measured gap is a language-parsing bottleneck rather than a reasoning bottleneck per se. That's a genuinely useful ablation, but it also means the paper's most quoted result (Figure 5's cross-model gap) partly measures parsing difficulty on unseen phrasing rather than reasoning-composition ability in isolation.

Second, the baselines are dated even for their own moment: MAC and Relation Networks were reasonable 2017-2018 choices for "models with an inductive bias toward relational reasoning," and BERT-base was the frontier pretrained LM in mid-2019, but none of the seven baselines were purpose-built for symbolic multi-step inference the way later reasoning-focused architectures would be, and the paper itself flags that a further baseline it tried — the Relational Recurrent Memory Core (Santoro et al. 2018) — performed "significantly less across all the tasks" and was dropped from the reported results because the authors could not determine whether that was a bug or a real result. That is an honest disclosure, but it also means one candidate result was excluded from the paper on grounds the authors themselves could not verify, which a reader can't independently audit.

Third, a genuine paper-internal numerical inconsistency: Table 2's Average row lists BERT's mean accuracy as 0.30, but the seven individual BERT cells that row is supposed to average (0.37, 0.28, 0.24, 0.24, 0.32, 0.25, 0.17) sum to 1.87 and divide to 0.267, which rounds to 0.27, not 0.30. Every other column's printed average reconciles with its own row (or is off by at most 0.01, plausibly from cell-level rounding) — BERT's is the one exception, off by a full 0.03. The same pattern recurs in Table 4 (the toy-placeholder robustness table): BERT's printed Average is 0.58, but its 12 cells (0.48, 0.75, 0.56, 0.27, 0.53, 0.42, 0.61, 0.41, 0.68, 0.54, 0.59, 0.49) sum to 6.33 and divide to 0.5275, which rounds to 0.53, not 0.58 — while every one of the other six columns in that same table reconciles to within 0.01 (checked cell-by-cell: BiLSTM-Attention 0.834→0.83, BiLSTM-Mean 0.818→0.82, RN 0.828→0.83, MAC 0.845→0.84, BERT-LSTM 0.603→0.60, GAT 0.82→0.82). Table 3, by contrast, has no such gap: its BERT column (0.19, 0.32, 0.2, 0.2, 0.24, 0.27, 0.25, 0.26, 0.22, 0.22, 0.17, 0.17) sums to 2.71 and divides to 0.2258, which rounds to the printed 0.23. So the discrepancy is specific to BERT's column, and specific to two of the paper's three noise-robustness tables, not a one-off rounding slip. Separately, and independent of any of that arithmetic: the Average row's seven printed standard errors are byte-identical across all three tables — Table 2's ±0.08/±0.08/±0.07/±0.06/±0.07/±0.05/±0.09, Table 3's, and Table 4's are the same seven values in the same column order, despite the three tables reporting entirely different means in all six text-model columns. The seventh column corroborates rather than undercuts this: GAT’s Table 3 and Table 4 columns are byte-identical across all twelve cells (0.92, 0.98, 0.92, 0.92, 0.77, 0.93, 0.85, 0.75, 0.78, 0.56, 0.96, 0.82), which is exactly what should happen — GAT reads the graph and never sees the placeholder substitution Table 4 varies. So the ablation moved the text models and left GAT alone, and it is the text columns’ identical spreads that have no such explanation. This is an observation about the printed tables, not a claim about how they were produced — the paper offers no account of it either way. Taken together, BERT's Table 2 and Table 4 averages specifically should be read with a discrepancy noted, not taken as internally verified the way the other columns are.

Finally, on the noise/robustness side, the reported gender-role simplification ("Kinship and gender roles are oversimplified in our data compared to the real world, to maintain tractability" — footnote 2) and the small, fixed pool of 300 entity names mean the benchmark's linguistic diversity, while measured (Jaccard unigram overlap of only 0.201 between paraphrases of the same clause), is still bounded by what 6,016 crowd-sourced templates and simple family vocabulary can express — a model that memorizes the finite template inventory rather than parsing genuinely novel phrasing has a much smaller space to memorize than a natural corpus would offer.

baselines

Reasonable and diverse for 2019 (LSTM variants, RN, MAC, BERT, BERT-LSTM, GAT), but the star baseline (GAT) receives privileged symbolic input the others don't, and a further tried baseline (RMC) was dropped from the paper on unverified grounds.

contamination

Structurally resistant — the generator can produce fresh graphs, entities, and paraphrase compositions indefinitely, and the paper releases the generator code, not just a frozen split.

what's missed

Most of the touted GAT-vs-text gap is shown by the paper's own ablation to be substantially a language-parsing gap rather than a pure reasoning gap once train/test paraphrases are shared; the paper is honest about this but the headline framing doesn't foreground it.

would raise confidence

A text-based baseline given the same symbolic graph as auxiliary input (an apples-to-apples reasoning-only comparison), reconciliation of BERT's unreconciled Average cells in Tables 2 and 4 (and of the identical printed error bars across all three noise tables), and reporting whether the withheld RMC result was ever resolved.

// bottom line

CLUTRR is a well-constructed, contamination-resistant diagnostic for systematic generalization whose central number (GAT's near-perfect in-distribution accuracy vs. text models' partial performance) is real but partly conflates "lacks access to symbolic structure" with "lacks reasoning ability" — a distinction the paper's own appendix ablation partially untangles but doesn't fully resolve in the main results.

// takeaways

What to remember

  1. CLUTRR is a generator, not a fixed dataset: it procedurally samples kinship graphs, backward-chains a controlled number of supporting facts, and crowd-sources natural-language paraphrases (6,016 collected, Table 1), which makes held-out reasoning-chain length a first-class, dial-able test of systematic generalization.
  2. The headline gap is stark but not clean: GAT (given the symbolic graph directly) reaches 1.0 accuracy at k=2 and averages 0.77 across Table 2's seven noise conditions versus a best text-based average of 0.61 (BiLSTM-Attention and MAC, tied) — but the paper's own train/test-paraphrase-sharing ablation shows most of that gap is a language-parsing bottleneck, not a pure symbolic-reasoning one.
  3. Robustness to noise is not monotone for any model: GAT collapses to 0.24 accuracy (its worst score in the entire table) when an untrained-for "supporting" noise fact introduces a graph cycle, yet recovers to 0.98 once trained on that same noise type. The paper's own two-sentence description of the noise pattern holds up cell-by-cell: BiLSTM-Attention, BiLSTM-Mean, RN and MAC all improve with supporting/irrelevant noise added, while both BERT-based models — plain BERT and BERT-LSTM — do not, exactly as the paper's own follow-up sentence says ("the BERT-based models do not benefit from the inclusion of this extra content"). BERT-LSTM's dip is small (0.67±0.03 → 0.66±0.06, inside the printed error bars, and → 0.55 on Irrelevant).
