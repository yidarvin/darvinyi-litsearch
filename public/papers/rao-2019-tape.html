Evaluating Protein Transfer Learning with TAPE

Five biologically-curated protein tasks — spanning local structure, evolutionary remote homology, and protein engineering — evaluated on splits designed to test genuine generalization, with supervised architectures the authors explicitly size-matched to GLUE's, so that self-supervised sequence models could finally be compared on one shared, leakage-conscious benchmark instead of five incompatible ones.

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

Proteins are linear chains of amino acids — the paper encodes them in a 25-symbol alphabet (20 standard amino acids plus a few rare or ambiguous ones) — that fold into three-dimensional structures. New sequencing technology had made unlabeled protein sequence cheap and abundant: Pfam, the pretraining corpus TAPE uses, already held roughly 31 million protein domains, with the total sequence census "doubling every two years." Labels are the opposite of cheap. Determining a protein's secondary structure or 3D contacts experimentally requires Cryo-EM or X-ray crystallography that, per the arXiv v1 preprint's appendix, "can take between weeks and years and can cost over $200,000" (a figure not in the NeurIPS camera-ready's main text, which carries no appendix — see the version note in the next section). That growing gap between huge unlabeled corpora and scarce labeled data is exactly the setup self-supervised NLP had already exploited — ELMo's bidirectional language modeling, BERT's masked-token prediction, GPT-2's next-token prediction — which raises the paper's central question: does the same trick transfer to protein sequences, which are discrete tokens like text but governed by evolution and biophysics rather than grammar?

Nobody could actually tell, because nobody was measuring the same thing. A few groups had already built self-supervised protein embeddings — Bepler & Berger's structure-supervised LSTM, Alley et al.'s UniRep (a unidirectional mLSTM), Rives et al.'s large-scale transformer — but, as the authors put it, "both Rives et al. and Bepler et al. report transfer learning results on secondary structure prediction and contact prediction, but they differ significantly in test set creation and data-splitting strategies," while "other self-supervised work such as Alley et al. and Yang et al. report protein engineering results, but on different tasks and datasets." Every group built its own benchmark, so no result was comparable to any other, and — more importantly — nothing forced a check that a model's "good" test score wasn't just memorizing near-duplicates of its training proteins. The one genuinely shared benchmark, CASP (packaged for ML use as ProteinNet), tested structure prediction alone, leaving evolutionary understanding and protein engineering completely unaddressed. Quantifying how much two sequences overlap — "sequence identity," the percentage of exact amino-acid matches between aligned proteins — is the tool TAPE leans on to keep training and test proteins from being near-duplicates ("preventing undesired information leakage between data splits"), and its core claim is that a benchmark has to build that check in at the level of the splits themselves, not just report accuracy on a held-out set drawn from the same distribution as training.

// how it works

The method

// which version this page follows

TAPE has two published forms that genuinely differ, and this page follows the NeurIPS 2019 camera-ready wherever the two disagree, since that is the paper of record its 1,053 citations point to. The source ↗ link above, however, resolves to arXiv 1906.08230v1 — arXiv hosts only that one, earlier version, so every reader who clicks through sees v1, not the camera-ready. The two differ on load-bearing numbers: v1's Table 1 has no Heldout-Clans column at all, and its Heldout-Families values differ from the camera-ready's (e.g. the Transformer's accuracy/perplexity/ECE there is 0.30 / 13.04 / 10.04 in v1 versus 0.35 / 11.77 / 8.87 in the camera-ready — LSTM and ResNet differ similarly). v1 also cites SuperGLUE but never cites GLUE at all; the GLUE size-matching sentence quoted below is camera-ready-only. Conversely, every appendix fact on this page — dataset costs, split construction, training details, and the detailed supplementary tables — is drawn from v1's appendix, since the NeurIPS proceedings PDF carries no appendix and the published supplement is known to renumber tables relative to v1 (e.g. v1's "Table S8" is the camera-ready's "Table S9"). Any appendix table cited below by number is v1's numbering, not necessarily the same number in the actual published supplement. The split is awkward in both directions: the camera-ready prints a Heldout Clans column but never mentions clans anywhere in its prose — the word appears exactly once in its 13 pages, in that table header — and it points readers to “Section A.5”, “Appendix A.1” and “Appendix A.3” for the split details, none of which exist in a document that ends mid-bibliography. The version that explains clans is the version that lacks the column.

TAPE (Tasks Assessing Protein Embeddings) has two parts: an unsupervised pretraining corpus and five supervised downstream tasks. Pretraining uses Pfam, whose 31 million domains are clustered into evolutionarily-related "families." The authors carve out two out-of-distribution test sets: a fully-heldout-families set (about 1% of the data) and, more aggressively, a heldout-clans set (entire "clans," sets of related families, chosen alongside the heldout families), while the remainder is split 95/5 for an in-distribution random test set. This gives three regimes for measuring language-model generalization: Random Families (in-distribution), Heldout Families, and Heldout Clans (the two out-of-distribution regimes, increasingly evolutionarily distant from anything the model trained on).

sequence identity(A,B) = exact-matching aligned positionstotal aligned positions // e.g. filtering at 25% means no train/test pair may share more than 25% identity — the mechanism behind two of the five task splits (SS and Contact); Remote Homology instead holds out entire superfamilies with no stated identity threshold, and Fluorescence/Stability use Hamming-distance neighborhoods

The five downstream tasks split into three biological themes. Structure prediction: Secondary Structure (SS) — a per-amino-acid, 3-class sequence-tagging task (Helix / Strand / Other), data from Klausen et al.'s NetSurfP-2.0, splits filtered at 25% sequence identity, scored by per-residue accuracy on the CB513 test set — and Contact Prediction — a pairwise task predicting whether each pair of amino acids sits within 8Å of the other, data from ProteinNet (itself built from CASP), splits filtered at 30% identity, scored by precision of the L/5 most likely contacts (L = protein length) restricted to medium- and long-range pairs on the CASP12 test set, "a standard metric reported in CASP." Evolutionary understanding: Remote Homology Detection — classify a protein into one of 1,195 structural folds, data from Hou et al.'s SCOP-derived DeepSF splits, which hold out entire superfamilies so a model must recognize a fold it has never seen a close relative of, scored by top-1 accuracy on the hardest (fold-level) heldout set. Protein engineering: Fluorescence — regress a green fluorescent protein (GFP) variant's log-fluorescence, training/validation confined to a Hamming-distance-≤3 neighborhood of the parent GFP, testing on variants 4+ mutations away — and Stability — regress the most extreme circumstances in which a designed protein still maintains its fold above a concentration threshold (the paper's own proxy for intrinsic stability), training on four rounds of Rocklin et al.'s designed-protein library, testing on Hamming-distance-1 neighborhoods around the most promising candidates found in that library. Both engineering splits are scored by Spearman's ρ.

Only two of the five splits are TAPE's own construction — v1's appendix is explicit about this: for Fluorescence and Stability, "we create training, validation, and test splits ourselves," using the Hamming-neighborhood design shown below. The other three (SS, Contact, Homology) reuse identity- or fold-filtered splits already established by Klausen et al., ProteinNet/CASP, and Hou et al. respectively — TAPE's contribution there is curation into one consistent suite and evaluation protocol, not inventing the generalization test itself.

Three architectures are trained from scratch with self-supervision: a 12-layer, 512-hidden, 8-head Transformer (38M parameters, masked-token prediction), a LSTM (two 3-layer, 1024-hidden LSTMs run forward and backward and concatenated, ELMo-style, next-token prediction in both directions), and a dilated ResNet (35 residual blocks, 256 filters, kernel 9, dilation rate 2, masked-token prediction). Two previously-published models are added for comparison under the same protocol: Bepler et al.'s structure-supervised bidirectional LSTM, and Alley et al.'s UniRep, a unidirectional 1900-unit mLSTM. Every self-supervised model trains on Pfam for one week on four V100 GPUs. Two non-neural baselines bound the comparison: a one-hot amino-acid encoding, and — where a comparable state-of-the-art method exists — an alignment-based featurization (e.g. HHblits transition probabilities for SS, PSSMs for Contact and Homology) concatenated to the one-hot input; alignment features are not used for the two engineering tasks, since proteins there differ by a single mutation and alignment search returns identical features for all of them.

The paper's only explicit tie to GLUE is about model size, not evaluation design — it should not be read as more than that. The authors write: "our supervised tasks are of similar size to most of those in the GLUE [benchmark]... since the models that were applied to GLUE have tens to hundreds of millions of parameters, we chose to make our models roughly the same size" (present in the NeurIPS camera-ready; the arXiv v1 preprint does not cite GLUE at all). TAPE's one stated debt to prior NLP benchmarking practice is instead to SuperGLUE, and it's about attribution, not protocol: "following similar benchmarking efforts in NLP, we describe a set of citation guidelines in our repository." Neither sentence claims TAPE's evaluation design is modeled on GLUE. That said — as our own observation, not the paper's — the resulting suite is recognisably GLUE-shaped: a fixed multi-task benchmark with per-task splits and metrics, scored across a menu of representations. Where it diverges is instructive too: GLUE collapses nine tasks into one macro-averaged leaderboard number behind privately-held test labels; TAPE reports five public, per-task numbers with no aggregate score and no leaderboard at all. Two years later, SUPERB would apply a similar template — freeze a pretrained representation, bolt on the lightest possible task head, rank a menu of encoders across a fixed task suite — to speech instead of protein sequences, without either paper citing the other.

The results

Table 1 tracks the unsupervised language-modeling metrics across three splits — camera-ready numbers, per the version note above; v1, what source ↗ actually shows a reader, has no Heldout Clans column and different Heldout Families values (Transformer 0.30 / 13.04 / 10.04 there, versus 0.35 / 11.77 / 8.87 below). Accuracy is higher-is-better; Perplexity and Exponentiated Cross-Entropy (ECE) are both lower-is-better (the paper reports both side by side without formally distinguishing them, though for the uniform-random baseline they coincide exactly, both landing at 25 — the vocabulary size — since a uniform distribution's perplexity equals its category count). The pattern is the camera-ready's own evidence that its out-of-distribution splits are genuinely harder: the Transformer's accuracy falls monotonically from 0.45 (Random Families) to 0.35 (Heldout Families) to 0.28 (Heldout Clans), and perplexity rises correspondingly (8.89 → 11.77 → 13.54) — an argument that depends on the three-way camera-ready split and cannot be made from v1's two-way table alone. Bold cells below reproduce the paper's own typesetting (verified span-by-span against the PDF's font layer, checked on both versions): every bold value is the literal best (lowest Perp/ECE, highest Acc) in its column, ties included — a convention the caption never states but that holds without exception. The Transformer isn't uniformly best: on Heldout Clans, ResNet actually posts the lowest ECE (10.62 vs. the Transformer's 10.76), the one column where the "Transformer dominates the pretraining metrics" pattern breaks.

Table 2 is the headline grid: five downstream tasks, no-pretrain vs. pretrained versions of the three from-scratch architectures, the two literature baselines (their architectures re-pretrained on Pfam by the TAPE authors under the same self-supervised protocol, not evaluated from their original papers' own pretraining), and the one-hot / alignment feature floors and ceilings. Every metric here is higher-is-better with a max of 1.0 — the paper is explicit: "we report accuracy, precision, or Spearman's ρ, depending on the task, so higher is always better." Self-supervised pretraining improves every one of the 3-architecture × 5-task = 15 headline comparisons in this table — though the paper's own prose hedges on the model side only, describing the effect as helping "almost all models on all tasks" (abstract) and improving performance "across almost all models and all tasks" (results) — the task-level exceptions surface not in Table 2 but in the finer-grained appendix breakdowns, which this page's critique section takes up below. The largest jump is the Transformer's Stability ρ, from −0.06 (no pretrain) to 0.73 (+0.79), and ResNet's Fluorescence goes from −0.28 to 0.21. But architectures diverge sharply in where pretraining helps: "our Transformer, which performs worst of the three models in secondary structure prediction [0.73 vs. 0.75 tied for LSTM/ResNet], performs best on the fluorescence and stability tasks. Where alignment beats self-supervision — and where it doesn't — is exact and exhaustive across the three tasks alignment features are even defined for: alignment wins Secondary Structure (0.80 vs. the best self-supervised 0.75, tied LSTM/ResNet) and Contact (0.64 vs. 0.40, Bepler et al.'s pretrained model, the actual best non-alignment cell — not the from-scratch LSTM's 0.39). Self-supervision wins the third: Remote Homology, where the alignment baseline manages only 0.09 against the best self-supervised model's 0.26 (LSTM, pretrained) — a nearly 3× gap. The paper itself offers no explanation for this reversal (its Discussion only notes that current SOTA methods for all three tasks take alignment-based inputs); the standard reading — alignment struggles precisely where sequence similarity is too low to find good alignments, which is the whole point of "remote" homology — is ours, not a claim the paper makes, though it fits the paper's own §2 observation that "homologous proteins may have very different sequences if they diverged in the distant past."

Fluorescence is bimodal — a "bright" mode and a "dark" mode — and v1's appendix breaks this down further: the pretrained Transformer's Spearman's ρ is 0.60 on the bright mode alone (up from 0.08 without pretraining) but only 0.05 on the dark mode alone (up from 0 without pretraining). The authors' own reading: "low Spearman's ρ for the dark mode suggests that models are not able to rank proteins within this mode" — pretraining does teach the model to rank variants meaningfully within the bright mode and to separate bright from dark overall, but within the dark mode specifically it stays close to uninformative, which the paper states plainly rather than papering over.

Pretraining visibly helps the LSTM capture more long-range contact structure in Figure 4, but v1's appendix (Table S5 in that numbering) shows this doesn't hold uniformly by architecture or by range: in the detailed long-range contact breakdown, ResNet's AUPRC is unchanged under pretraining (0.06 vs. 0.06) and its precision-at-L actually drops slightly (0.11 → 0.10); only its precision-at-L/5 improves (0.15 → 0.17). That's a real, specific place pretraining does not help — even though ResNet's aggregate, main-table Contact score (which blends medium- and long-range and is identical in both versions) does rise, 0.20 → 0.29.

// does it hold up?

The eval read

TAPE's central methodological claim is that its splits "test biologically relevant generalization" rather than in-distribution memorization. That claim is on solid, demonstrated footing for the unsupervised pretraining task — Table 1's monotonic degradation from Random Families to Heldout Families to Heldout Clans (camera-ready numbers, see the version note above) is direct empirical evidence the splits get harder, and it isn't asserted, it's measured. It's demonstrated for Remote Homology too: v1's appendix (Table S6 in that numbering) reports the pretrained LSTM's top-1 accuracy climbing from 0.26 (Fold, the split the headline table reports) to 0.43 (Superfamily) to 0.92 (Family) as test proteins get evolutionarily closer to training — "reflected in the accuracies in Table S6, which increase drastically as the test sets get easier," in the paper's own words. That leaves Secondary Structure and Contact on weaker footing: both borrow identity-filtered splits wholesale from prior, separately-validated benchmarks (Klausen et al., ProteinNet/CASP) — reasonable curation, but TAPE itself runs no analogous ablation (e.g., comparing its filtered split against an i.i.d. random split of the same data) to show the difficulty gap for these two tasks the way it does for language modeling and remote homology. The other two (Fluorescence, Stability) are TAPE's own invention, and their harder-generalization claim rests mostly on the authors' description of the Hamming-neighborhood design plus the indirect evidence that dark-mode fluorescence stays near-unrankable (ρ≈0.05) even after pretraining — suggestive, but not the same as a controlled comparison against a random split.

There's a narrower, still-real leakage gap — not an absence the paper is blind to, but an inconsistency in how far it goes. The 31-million-sequence Pfam self-supervised pretraining corpus is never stated to be filtered against any of the five downstream test sets (CB513, CASP12, the SCOP fold-holdout set, the GFP variants, or Rocklin et al.'s designed proteins); the heldout-family/heldout-clan splits check only whether the language model itself generalizes to unseen Pfam families, which says nothing about whether a specific downstream test protein sat inside the pretraining corpus. But the authors demonstrably knew this failure mode and closed it for a different pretraining stage: v1's appendix (A.5 in that numbering) describes identity-filtering the Bepler-style supervised pretraining set against the downstream SS, contact, and homology test sets — "we perform this same sequence identity filtering on the supervised pretraining set. This reduced the supervised pretraining dataset size by 75% which likely reduced the effectiveness of the supervised pretraining." That same discipline is never stated for the much larger Pfam corpus feeding the three from-scratch self-supervised architectures, which is arguably the sharper gap for being demonstrably solvable — they did it once and didn't say they did it twice.

There is also a genuine, unreconciled internal inconsistency in the paper's own Discussion. Comparing to published state-of-the-art, it writes: "RaptorX achieves 0.69 precision at L/5 on CASP12 contact prediction, compared to our best model's 0.49." But Table 2's Contact column — precision at L/5 for medium- and long-range contacts, the metric Task 2 itself defines — tops out at 0.40 (Bepler et al.) among non-alignment models; no cell in that column is 0.49. The number 0.49 instead matches exactly v1's short-range contact table (Table S3 in that numbering), where the pretrained LSTM scores precision-at-L/5 = 0.49 — a categorically easier setting than the medium-/long-range metric the comparison claims to be making, and one the paper's own task definition explicitly excludes from the headline Contact metric. Whether this is a transposed number or a genuine like-for-like error, it understates the actual SOTA gap: the best self-supervised long-range P@L/5 in v1's appendix is only 0.27, versus RaptorX's 0.69.

baselines

Alignment-based features are hand-picked per task to match whatever each task's own prior state-of-the-art method used (HHblits transition probabilities for SS, PSSMs for Contact and Homology) rather than one uniform alignment representation — a reasonable choice for a strong-baseline ceiling, but it means the "alignment vs. learned" comparison isn't apples-to-apples across the three tasks it covers. The authors also state plainly, in v1's appendix, that they "did not perform hyperparameter tuning or significant architecture optimization," so Table 2's per-architecture gaps shouldn't be read as a tuned head-to-head.

contamination

No stated filtering of the Pfam self-supervised pretraining corpus against any of the five downstream test sets — even though the authors identity-filtered a different (supervised) pretraining set against those same test sets elsewhere, so they knew the check and didn't state doing it for Pfam. The heldout-family/-clan split only tests LM generalization, not pretrain→downstream-test overlap.

what's missed

Dark-mode fluorescence ranking stays near-zero even after pretraining (the paper says so itself); ResNet's long-range contact precision is flat-to-worse under pretraining even as its aggregate Contact score improves; and the Discussion's RaptorX comparison quotes a short-range number where the paper's own metric definition calls for medium-and-long-range.

would raise confidence

An explicit dedup of Pfam against every downstream test set; a random-split ablation for Secondary Structure and Contact to show their filtered splits are actually harder, the way Table 1 already demonstrates for language modeling and Table S6 demonstrates for remote homology; and a corrected, like-for-like contact-prediction comparison to RaptorX.

// bottom line

TAPE predates AlphaFold2, ESM-scale protein language models, and everything the field now considers strong — its own Table 2 numbers (0.39–0.40 contact precision, 26% fold-level homology accuracy) are a historical floor, not a target, and a 2026 reader shouldn't benchmark against them. What holds up is the protocol: five biologically distinct tasks, honest about which splits it invented versus borrowed, reported without an aggregated leaderboard score, in a format later protein-language-model papers kept reporting scores against. The paper is unusually candid about its own weak spots (the dark-mode fluorescence caveat, the "no hyperparameter tuning" admission) — which makes the unaddressed pretrain-corpus leakage question and the uncorrected contact-prediction citation stand out more, not less.

// takeaways

What to remember

  1. TAPE packages five protein tasks — secondary structure, contact prediction, remote homology, fluorescence, and stability — under one shared evaluation protocol whose supervised architectures the authors explicitly size-matched to GLUE's (the paper never claims to model its evaluation design on GLUE, though the resulting suite is recognisably GLUE-shaped), so self-supervised protein representations could be compared on the same splits and metrics instead of each paper inventing its own.
  2. Self-supervised pretraining improved all 15 architecture×task comparisons in Table 2, but unevenly by architecture (the Transformer wins fluorescence/stability, the ResNet ties SS but lags badly on fluorescence) and unevenly against hand-built alignment features — which still beat every learned representation on secondary structure (0.80 vs. 0.75) and contact prediction (0.64 vs. 0.40), while losing decisively on remote homology (0.09 vs. 0.26).
  3. Only two of the five splits (Fluorescence, Stability) are TAPE's own invention; the rest are curated from prior benchmarks. The paper predates AlphaFold2 and large protein language models, so its own numbers are now a floor, not a target — its lasting contribution is the protocol and splits that later protein-LM papers kept reporting scores against.
