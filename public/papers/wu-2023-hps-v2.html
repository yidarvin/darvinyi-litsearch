Human Preference Score v2: A Solid Benchmark for Evaluating Human Preferences of Text-to-Image Synthesis

A 798,090-comparison human-preference dataset spanning nine text-to-image generators plus real photos, and a CLIP model fine-tuned on it to score images the way people actually rank them — built to fix the single-generator, style-word-biased prompts baked into HPS v1, ImageReward, and Pick-a-Pic.

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

By 2023 there was general agreement that Inception Score, Fréchet Inception Distance, and CLIP Score do not correlate well with what humans actually think of a generated image, and a first generation of learned preference models — HPS v1 (the same authors' earlier work), ImageReward, and Pick-a-Pic — had appeared to fill the gap by fine-tuning a vision-language model directly on human pairwise choices. The paper's argument is that this first generation carries two specific, fixable biases rather than being simply "wrong."

The first is image-source bias. HPS v1, ImageReward and Pick-a-Pic were built almost entirely from Stable Diffusion (and its variants) outputs, so a model tuned and tested on them has no evidence it generalizes to images from a GAN, an autoregressive model, or any generator with different failure modes. The second is prompt bias. A large share of their training prompts come from Discord channels or DiffusionDB, where users pile on style tokens: the paper counts roughly 28.5% of DiffusionDB prompts containing "artstation" and 15.0% containing "Greg Rutkowski." These tokens only work if a generator was trained on data using the same tokens, so scoring different generator families on the same style-word-heavy prompts is not a level test — and the prompts are sometimes internally contradictory ("renaissance oil painting" next to "realism" and "hyper realistic" in the same string), which the paper argues confuses annotators judging image-text alignment as much as it confuses the generative models. On top of both, there was no agreed evaluation prompt set: Imagen's DrawBench has only 200 prompts, which the paper says “only supports the purpose of evaluation” rather than data collection — too few, on this page’s own reading of the per-style spread, for a stable per-model score — Table 7 later reports DrawBench alongside HPS v2's own 800-prompt style sets purely for reference, and DrawBench's reported standard deviation is larger than any of the four style sets' for every one of the 20 benchmarked models.

// how it works

The method

The paper ships two artifacts. Human Preference Dataset v2 (HPD v2) is the data: 798,090 binary pairwise preference choices over 433,760 images, generated from 107,515 prompts. Human Preference Score v2 (HPS v2) is a scoring model — an OpenCLIP ViT-H/14 checkpoint (pre-trained on LAION-2B) fine-tuned on HPD v2 — that turns a (prompt, image) pair into a scalar preference score. Figure 1 lays out the four-stage pipeline that builds the dataset and trains the model.

Fixing the prompt bias first

Every prompt in HPD v2 comes from one of two sources: COCO Captions (real-image captions, used as-is) or DiffusionDB, run through ChatGPT to strip style words and contradictions and rewrite the result as one plain sentence — e.g. a DiffusionDB prompt reading "queen goddess isis, high fantasy, by john berkey, by peter mohrbacher, renaissance oil painting, realism, master study, hyper-realist" becomes "The image depicts a regal depiction of the Egyptian goddess Isis." Figure 3 shows what this actually removes: the frequency of prompts containing "artstation," "greg rutkowski," and "mucha," and of prompts ChatGPT itself judges self-contradictory, all drop sharply after cleaning (the largest single drop is "artstation," from roughly 28% of raw DiffusionDB prompts to under 5% after cleaning).

Fixing the image-source bias

HPD v2 draws images from nine generators plus real COCO photos: CogView2, DALL·E 2, GLIDE, Stable Diffusion v1.4, Stable Diffusion v2.0, LAFITE, VQGAN+CLIP, VQ-Diffusion, and FuseDream, spanning autoregressive, diffusion, and GAN architectures from 0.35B to 24B parameters. Crucially, the training split uses only four of these models plus COCO images, while the test split uses all nine plus COCO — the five models absent from training (GLIDE, LAFITE, VQGAN+CLIP, VQ-Diffusion, FuseDream) exist specifically to measure whether the trained preference model generalizes to generators it never saw label data from.

57 contract annotators (50 labelers, 7 quality checkers) ranked the images: at least 10% of each labeler's work is spot-checked against a checker's own ranking (recall@2), and the overall hiring rate from a qualifying test was 61%. The test split's 400 prompt-groups are each labeled by 10 distinct annotators (153,000 binary comparisons total), which is what makes Figure 5's per-pair agreement analysis possible later; the training split's 107,515 groups get one annotator each (645,090 comparisons). 153,000 + 645,090 = 798,090, the headline dataset size.

Training the scoring model

HPS v2 fine-tunes CLIP's cosine-similarity score between a prompt p and image x. For a pair of images with preference label y, the two similarity scores are turned into a softmax over the pair, and the model minimizes the KL divergence between that softmax and the human label — i.e., a Bradley-Terry-style pairwise preference loss on top of CLIP's own scoring function. The last 20 layers of the image encoder and the last 11 layers of the text encoder are unfrozen and trained for 4,000 steps with AdamW (learning rate 3.3×10⁻⁶, weight decay 0.35, batch size 128, 500-step warm-up, cosine schedule); the layer counts and learning rate were chosen by Bayesian optimization against HPD v2 test accuracy.

To turn the same score into a benchmark number, the raw CLIP cosine similarity is simply rescaled by 100:

sθ(p, x) = Enctxt(p) · Encimg(x) × 100 // Eq. 4 — the number every HPS v2 score on this page (e.g. Table 7, Figure 11) is read directly off

Every reported HPS v2 score in the paper — and on this page — is this quantity, on a scale that lands mostly in the low-to-high 20s for real generator outputs, not the raw 0–1 cosine similarity CLIP starts from. Figure 5, discussed below, is the one place this rescale doesn't reach: its x-axis is still plotted on the pre-rescale, raw cosine scale.

The results

Table 6 compares HPS v2 against zero-shot CLIP, ImageReward, an Aesthetic Score Predictor, HPS v1, and PickScore on two held-out test sets: ImageReward's own test set (built entirely from Stable Diffusion images) and HPD v2's test set (the nine-generator-plus-COCO set described above). Two things stand out in that table beyond the headline win. First, HPS v2 (65.7 / 83.3) slightly beats "Single Human vs. Single Human" (65.3 / 78.1) on both test sets — trained on averaged preference from many annotators, it predicts an individual's choice slightly better than another individual does, which is a real, non-trivial result for a model trained purely on binary labels. Second, it does not reach "Single Human vs. Averaged Human" (85.0 on HPD v2), the closest thing the paper has to a ceiling — human preference itself is diverse enough that even one person's judgment doesn't fully predict the group average.

Table 7 is the actual benchmark: 20 recent text-to-image models scored on four 800-prompt style sets — "Animation," "Concept-art," "Painting," "Photo" — each split into 10 groups of 80 for a mean ± std, plus DrawBench included for reference only. Several publicly-known community fine-tunes of Stable Diffusion (Dreamlike Photoreal 2.0 at 28.24, Realistic Vision at 28.22, Deliberate at 28.13 on Animation) sit at the top of the table, ahead of both official Stable Diffusion releases (27.26–27.48) — the paper's own §5 reads this as "popular models from the community are consistently out-performing models from the academic," though it does not label each row academic/community/industry itself. Figure 11 gives two qualitative examples from the community-checkpoint side of that comparison, with HPS v2 numbers printed next to each image — it doesn't include an academic-model render, so it illustrates the scores' granularity rather than the academic-vs-community gap itself.

Appendix E's pairwise breakdown (Table 9) shows where the model's separating power is weakest: across all 45 pairwise combinations of the nine test-set generators and real COCO photos, HPS v2's pairwise agreement with human choice runs 67.5–98.8% for every pair except two, but VQGAN+CLIP is a consistent outlier — 51.5% against both FuseDream and VQ-Diffusion, barely above chance.

// does it hold up?

The eval read

The core methodological improvement over HPS v1 / ImageReward / Pick-a-Pic is real: testing against five generator families never seen during training, and reporting accuracy on an external benchmark (ImageReward's own test set) rather than only the paper's own test set, is exactly the right instinct for a preference model whose whole premise is "generalizes across generators." But the margins on the two test sets in Table 6 aren't symmetric, and that asymmetry is worth sitting with. On HPD v2's own test set — sharing 4 of its 9 generators with the training split (the other 5 are held out specifically to test generalization) and drawn from the same ChatGPT-cleaned-DiffusionDB/COCO prompt recipe the model was fine-tuned on — HPS v2 leads the next-best model (PickScore) by 3.5 points, 83.3 vs. 79.8. On ImageReward's external test set, HPS v2 leads ImageReward's own model by 0.6 points, 65.7 vs. 65.1 — a margin small enough that "HPS v2 generalizes better than previous metrics" rests almost entirely on the benchmark it was purpose-built to match.

That points at a deeper property of the evaluation design: the images vary across generators — 4 + COCO in training, 9 + COCO at HPD v2 test time, and 20 models on the 3,200-prompt Table 7 benchmark — but the prompts never do. Every prompt anywhere in this pipeline — training, HPD v2 test, and the four-style benchmark used to rank the 20 models in Table 7 — is either a COCO Caption or a DiffusionDB prompt run through the same ChatGPT-cleaning instruction. The paper's own Limitations section (§6) names the gap directly: "there are indeed some overlooked topics, such as logo and graphic design," which "play a crucial role in design industries and have unique criteria." A scoring model fine-tuned exclusively on this prompt population has no evidence its ranking transfers to a materially different one — the paper solved the generator-diversity half of the original bias problem and left the prompt-diversity half only partially addressed (cleaning DiffusionDB's tokens, not diversifying its topics).

Figure 5 gives a second, sharper worry — and it comes with a paper-internal wrinkle worth flagging on its own. It plots inter-annotator agreement against the absolute HPS v2 gap between two models, but its x-axis (0.000–0.040) is left on the raw, pre-rescale 0–1 cosine-similarity scale, while every score elsewhere on this page and in the paper — Table 7, Figure 11, Eq. 4 — is that same quantity ×100; nothing in the paper flags the difference. Converted to Table 7's scale, the trend is unambiguous — agreement clusters around 60–80% when the gap is below roughly 1.5 points (0.015 raw), and only climbs reliably past 85–90% once the gap exceeds about 2.0 points (0.02 raw). That means the benchmark's finest distinctions are drawn from its noisiest ground truth. In Table 7, several adjacent models sit inside that low-agreement band — Epic Diffusion (27.57) and Openjourney (27.85) on Animation are 0.28 apart on the Table 7 scale (0.0028 raw), well inside where Figure 5 shows agreement dropping toward 60–80%. The paper's reported ± figures (e.g. ±0.163, ±0.145) measure dispersion across 10 prompt subsets, which is a real and useful stability signal, but it is not the same thing as annotator disagreement, and Figure 5 shows the two can diverge exactly where a reader is most likely to compare two close scores and declare a winner.

One more internal wrinkle, small but worth flagging: the abstract, §3, and §3.4's own arithmetic (153,000 test comparisons + 645,090 training comparisons) all agree the dataset totals 798,090 comparisons, but Appendix H.2's datasheet states "789,090 instances" — reusing the same 433,760-image, 107,515-prompt figures. The three independent, arithmetic-checked mentions all say 798,090; the datasheet's 789,090 looks like a transposition. Not consequential to any result, but a reader citing "dataset size" from the datasheet section specifically would get it wrong.

baselines

Fair and contemporary (CLIP, HPS v1, ImageReward, Aesthetic Score Predictor, PickScore) — but HPS v2's win margin drops from 3.5 points on its own test set to 0.6 points on ImageReward's external test set, so the "generalizes better" claim is much better supported on the benchmark HPS v2 itself was built to match.

contamination

Not leakage in the classic sense — the paper is careful to hold out five image generators — but train/test/benchmark prompts are drawn from one unvarying recipe (ChatGPT-cleaned DiffusionDB + COCO Captions); the paper's own Limitations section names logo/graphic-design prompts as an untested topic.

what's missed

Figure 5's own agreement-vs-gap plot — plotted on the raw, pre-×100 cosine scale, not Table 7's scale — shows inter-annotator agreement sagging to 60–80% for gaps under ~1.5 points on the Table 7 scale (0.015 raw) — exactly the regime many Table 7 model-to-model comparisons fall into — and the reported ±std (dispersion across prompt subsets) doesn't surface that noise floor.

would raise confidence

A held-out prompt-distribution test (non-DiffusionDB/COCO topics), an agreement-weighted accuracy figure alongside the flat one, and reconciling the 798,090-vs-789,090 dataset-size mismatch between the abstract and the datasheet.

// bottom line

HPS v2 is a genuine, carefully-built upgrade over HPS v1, ImageReward, and Pick-a-Pic on the axis that matters most for a preference benchmark: it tests generalization across generator families it never trained on, not just within one. But its own figures — Table 6's much thinner margin on an external test set, and Figure 5's agreement-vs-gap plot — say the benchmark is most trustworthy exactly where its rankings are least interesting (large, obvious gaps between generators) and shakiest exactly where a user would most want a tiebreaker (small gaps between similar models).

// takeaways

What to remember

  1. HPD v2 is 798,090 pairwise preference comparisons over 433,760 images from nine text-to-image models plus real COCO photos, ranked by 57 trained contract annotators — built specifically so the training set (4 models + COCO) and test set (9 models + COCO, 5 held out) can measure generalization across generator families, not just within Stable Diffusion variants.
  2. HPS v2 (OpenCLIP ViT-H/14 fine-tuned on HPD v2 via a pairwise KL-divergence loss) is the best of six compared models on both test sets — 83.3% on HPD v2's own test (vs. PickScore's 79.8%) and 65.7% on ImageReward's external test (vs. ImageReward's own 65.1%) — but that second margin is thin enough to temper how far the "generalizes better" claim should be pushed.
  3. The paper's own Figure 5 shows inter-annotator agreement dropping toward 60–80% exactly when two models' HPS v2 scores are close together — worth remembering before reading a small gap in a Table-7-style leaderboard as a confident ranking.
