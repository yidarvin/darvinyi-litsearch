SmartPlay: A Benchmark for LLMs as Intelligent Agents

Six games -- from Rock-Paper-Scissors to Minecraft -- wrapped in one text-based Gym API, scored against nine named agent capabilities (planning, randomness, spatial reasoning, learning from mistakes, and more). Even GPT-4 lands 10 to 70 points below the human baseline depending on which game is asked.

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

By late 2023, LLM evaluation had two well-worn tracks: static knowledge/reasoning suites (MMLU, HELM, BIG-bench, AGIEval) and helpful/harmless dialogue evaluation (HH-RLHF, MT-Bench/Chatbot Arena, AlpacaFarm). Neither track asks the question that was starting to matter most as people wired LLMs into AutoGPT-, BabyAGI-, and AgentGPT-style loops: can this model act as an agent that perceives an environment, keeps a running strategy, and recovers when its plan goes wrong?

The authors name four capabilities they say prior LLM benchmarks miss entirely: long-horizon planning for multi-step real-world tasks, understanding the odds in probabilistic environments, spatial reasoning for 3D worlds, and learning from interactions or mistakes when the agent hits an unseen situation. Their proposed fix leans on a much older idea -- using games as intelligence benchmarks -- because well-designed games bundle exactly these properties (calculation of odds, spatial reasoning, escalating difficulty, quantifiable outcomes) and, crucially, some are procedurally generated, which the paper argues makes them harder to solve by memorization than a fixed-answer QA set.

That contamination argument isn't just asserted -- it's demonstrated with a concrete anecdote the paper returns to in its results: for the 3-disk Tower of Hanoi, a puzzle whose textbook solution is almost certainly in every LLM's pretraining data, models can recite the standard recursive solution starting from the canonical all-disks-on-rod-A configuration, yet "get confused quickly after a few moves" once play has scattered the disks across all three rods -- a state combination far less likely to appear verbatim in training corpora. The authors read this as evidence that games (or at least their less-rehearsed intermediate states) probe something closer to live reasoning than memorized answers.

// how it works

The method

SmartPlay ships 6 games: Two-Armed Bandits, Rock Paper Scissors, (3-disk) Tower of Hanoi, Messenger (Hanjie et al., 2021, 3 difficulty levels), Crafter (Hafner, 2021, a 2D open-world survival game with 22 achievements across a tech tree of depth 7 and 17 discrete actions), and a simplified Minecraft "find-the-biome" creative-navigation task built on MineDojo (Fan et al., 2022). Together the six expose up to 20 distinct evaluation settings. Because most LLMs at the time had no vision channel, every environment -- including the two 2D/3D ones -- is converted to text: Minecraft's raw voxel/lidar observation is first grouped by a connected-component pass over same-type blocks, then each group is reported as a directional, distance-annotated line like "grass, 3.87 blocks away to your bottom, taking 38% of screen" (Figure 4; the visual-descriptor scheme is adapted from the authors' own earlier SPRING work, Wu et al., 2023c).

Every game is exposed through one OpenAI-Gym-style interface (Brockman et al., 2016): text observation, a text manual whose components vary by game (background only for Bandits; background + rules for Rock Paper Scissors; background + rules + worked examples for Hanoi; background + rules + an "advice" string for Messenger and Crafter; just an objective for Minecraft), a rolling text history of past actions/observations up to a fixed history length, and a flat categorical action space. At every turn the agent is prompted twice, following the two-step pattern from the authors' prior SPRING paper: first a free-form "What is the next action to take, let's think step by step." with the manual, history, and current observation as context, then a forced-choice follow-up, "Choose the best executable action from the list of all actions. Write the exact chosen action.", which maps the reasoning back onto one concrete environment action.

SmartPlay reports three metrics: reward (matching each game's original RL reward, for Bandits/RPS/Messenger/Crafter), completion rate (Hanoi, Messenger, Minecraft), and a score defined per game so every environment has a comparable number -- for Bandits and RPS, the count of times the agent's action matches the environment's optimal action; for Hanoi, the number of disks successfully moved to the goal peg; for Messenger, the same value as its reward; for Crafter, the number of unlocked achievements summed across the whole episode; for Minecraft, a binary indicator of whether the "find" objective was completed.

Section 2 defines 9 capabilities an intelligent agent needs -- long text understanding, reasoning, instruction/rule following, planning, generalization, understanding the odds, learning from interactions, error/mistake handling, and spatial reasoning -- each with its own 3-4 point degree scale (e.g. spatial reasoning: 1 = no/1D, 2 = 2D, 3 = 3D). Appendix Table 3 hand-assigns a degree to every game/capability pair (Minecraft rates a 3 on spatial reasoning and a 3 on generalization; Crafter alone rates the maximum 4 on learning from interactions since two of its recipes -- 2 wood for a crafting table, 8 stone for a furnace -- aren't stated anywhere in its own instruction manual and must be discovered by trial and error). Those per-game degree weights dgc feed directly into a per-capability score used to build the Figure 3 radar charts:

The results

To keep query costs down, the paper narrows its quantitative run to 7 evaluation settings out of the possible 20 -- Bandit, RPS, Hanoi, MessengerL1, MessengerL2, Crafter, and Minecraft (MessengerL3 is left out of Table 2 entirely) -- and evaluates the 8 LLMs that appear in Table 2 (the paper's own text says "9 recent popular open-source and proprietary LLMs," but only 8 model rows plus the human baseline show up in both Table 2 and Table 4) against a human baseline: GPT-4-0613, GPT-4-0314, text-davinci-003, Claude, Bard, and three open-source LLaMA-family models (llama-2-13b, llama-13b, vicuna-13b). The paper's own framing of the gaps: a 10-point gap on 3-Disk Hanoi (GPT-4-0314 hits 0.90 against the 1.00 human baseline), roughly a 40-point gap on Minecraft creative navigation (the single best score in that column, 0.61, is shared by GPT-4-0613 and llama-2-13b -- "all LLMs behave similarly in Minecraft creative tasks, with the best model at 60% of human baseline performance"), and about a 70-point gap on Crafter (GPT-4-0314's 0.32 is the column's best score, still far under 1.00). Table 2's other proprietary models -- Claude, Bard, text-davinci-003 -- trail the GPT-4 variants by more than 20 normalized points on most games (e.g. RPS: GPT-4-0613's 0.91 vs. Claude's 0.47, Bard's 0.30, text-davinci-003's 0.40), and on Crafter specifically GPT-4 variants score roughly 3× higher than any other proprietary model (0.26/0.32 vs. 0.04–0.07).

One number breaks the "human ceiling" framing outright: text-davinci-003 posts 1.04 on Bandit -- literally above the 1.00 human baseline. Bandit's Table 2 entry is not a reward figure; the paper defines "score" for Bandit and RPS as the count of rounds where the LLM's action matches the environment's optimal action, and the arm payoff probabilities are hidden from the agent and re-shuffled every episode specifically to prevent exploitation. Table 4's raw scores explain the >1.00: over the 50-round rollout, text-davinci-003 matched the optimal arm 46.92 times on average against the three-person human baseline's 45.

Fine-tuning doesn't uniformly help: vicuna-13b, an instruction-tuned descendant of llama-13b, scores lower than its own un-tuned base model on 6 of the 7 settings -- most starkly on Hanoi (0.07 vs. llama-13b's 0.33) and MessengerL1 (0.00 vs. 0.16) -- which the paper summarizes as "the fine-tuned Vicuna-13b model performs much worse than the base LLAMA-13b."

The capability radar (Figure 3, built from the pcLLM formula above) adds texture the aggregate score hides: both GPT-4 variants score comparatively lower on learning from interactions, error/mistake handling, and spatial reasoning than on the other six axes. Among the mid-tier proprietary models, Claude "demonstrates overall better performance than Bard, especially in planning, reasoning, instruction following," while text-davinci-003 is described as biased toward learning-from-interaction and randomness but "particularly weaker at instruction following, planning and reasoning." Among the open-source models, llama-2-13b beats llama-13b on planning, reasoning, and error handling but is worse at learning-from-randomness/interactions, and vicuna-13b "loses a lot of reasoning, planning, long text understanding, and error/mistake handling capabilities after fine-tuning" relative to llama-13b.

Two qualitative observations round out the paper's Section 5.2: in Minecraft, LLMs are reported to often issue "a bunch of 'move north' followed by a bunch of 'move south,' undoing a lot of its own efforts at exploration" -- a concrete symptom of the spatial-reasoning gap the radar chart shows numerically. And in Bandits/RPS, the proprietary models are observed to shift from an exploratory strategy early on to exploiting the (initially hidden, revealed-through-play) biased opponent -- the one place the paper highlights genuinely promising in-context learning-from-interaction behavior.

// does it hold up?

The eval read

SmartPlay's per-game qualitative findings (the Hanoi mid-puzzle confusion, the Minecraft direction-reversal, Vicuna's post-tuning regression) are concrete and well-supported by the transcripts the paper describes. The single aggregate "human-normalized score" that those findings get folded into deserves more scrutiny than the paper gives it.

baselines

Every score in Table 2 (and every axis of the Figure 3 radar) is normalized against a human baseline drawn from just 3 players, "including the authors" (Appendix D.1) -- not blinded, not independent of the benchmark's design. Worse, the number of rounds each human played is wildly uneven: 3 rounds each of Bandit/RPS, but only 1 round each of Hanoi, Crafter, and Minecraft -- exactly the three games the paper reports the largest LLM gaps on. A single human playthrough is a high-variance ceiling to be dividing every model's score by, especially for Crafter's 22-achievement, 10,000-step episode.

contamination

The paper's one contamination probe -- LLMs reciting Hanoi's canonical opening but losing the thread once disks are scattered mid-game -- is a genuinely clever, well-documented test. But it covers exactly one of six games. The claim that "games could be more robust to dataset contamination" generally rests on that single anecdote plus the (untested) assumption that procedural generation in Messenger/Crafter/Minecraft is itself sufficient; no seed-overlap or memorization check is run on the other five environments.

what's missed

The per-game "score" compresses four genuinely different task shapes -- RPS's opponent-modeling, Hanoi's exact combinatorial planning, Crafter's 22-item achievement count, Minecraft's binary find/no-find outcome -- into one [0,1] number, and the capability radar then re-weights those numbers by a 1-4 "degree" label (Table 3) that the four authors assigned to each game/capability pair themselves, with no independent annotation or inter-rater check. The capability breakdown looks precise (nine named axes, numeric weights) but is built on a subjective rubric from the same team that designed the games.

would raise confidence

A larger, blinded human baseline with equal trial counts across all seven settings (not 3 rounds for the easy games and 1 for the hard ones); an explicit contamination check (seed novelty / string-overlap test) extended to Messenger, Crafter, and Minecraft, not just Hanoi; and either an independently validated capability-weighting scheme or, short of that, releasing the raw per-trial transcripts so a reader can re-derive the radar without trusting the authors' own degree labels.

internal consistency

Appendix D.2 prints the normalization as sg = (shuman − sraw)/(shuman − smin), but that isn't the formula the tables actually use. Plugging in Table 4's Crafter raw scores for GPT-4-0613 (sraw=700, shuman=2680, smin=0) gives (2680−700)/(2680−0) = 0.739 -- but Table 2 reports 0.26. The formula that actually reproduces Table 2 is the complement, (sraw − smin)/(shuman − smin): (700−0)/(2680−0) = 0.261. The same swap holds on MessengerL1 (GPT-4-0613: raw 0.8, human 1, min −1 -- the corrected formula gives 1.8/2 = 0.90, matching Table 2; the printed formula gives 0.10). As printed, the formula would also assign the human baseline itself a score of 0.00 on every game, which is inconsistent with Table 2's human row of all 1.00s. A minor copyediting slip, but it means the one equation in the paper that's supposed to let a reader independently re-derive Table 2 doesn't.

// bottom line

SmartPlay is a well-built, genuinely diagnostic stress test -- its qualitative catches (Hanoi's memorization boundary, Minecraft's directional flip-flopping, Vicuna's regression relative to LLaMA) are the kind of finding static QA benchmarks structurally cannot produce. But the single normalized "human-1.00" score and the nine-axis capability radar built on top of it rest on a 3-person, unevenly-sampled human baseline and a self-assigned capability rubric -- treat those aggregate numbers as illustrative, and the per-game transcripts as the real evidence.

// takeaways

What to remember

  1. SmartPlay wraps 6 games (Bandits, Rock Paper Scissors, Tower of Hanoi, Messenger, Crafter, Minecraft) behind one OpenAI-Gym-style text API and scores agents against 9 named capabilities, so a benchmark result can be traced to a specific skill (planning, spatial reasoning, learning from mistakes, ...) instead of one leaderboard number.
  2. Even the strongest model tested falls well short of the human baseline once the task gets hard: GPT-4-0314 scores 0.90 on 3-Disk Hanoi (a 10-point gap), the best Minecraft score is 0.61 -- shared by GPT-4-0613 and llama-2-13b -- against a 1.00 human ceiling, and GPT-4-0314's best Crafter score is 0.32.
  3. Instruction-tuning isn't automatically an upgrade: vicuna-13b scores below its own un-tuned base model, llama-13b, on 6 of 7 settings (Hanoi: 0.07 vs. 0.33; MessengerL1: 0.00 vs. 0.16) -- a concrete case where instruction fine-tuning cost this agent's reasoning and planning ability, not just its style.
