2018 · AAAI · Benchmarks & Evals

Complex Sequential Question Answering: Towards Learning to Converse Over Linked Question Answer Pairs with a Knowledge Graph

IBM Research, IIT Madras, and MILA introduce CSQA — 200K dialogs and 1.6M turns, each conversation a coherent walk over a 21.2M-tuple Wikidata subgraph that demands logical, quantitative, and comparative reasoning plus coreference, ellipsis, and clarification handling — then show that a state-of-the-art hierarchical dialog model spliced to a key-value memory network manages only 15.83% recall and 6.7% precision overall, with entity-retrieval quantitative-reasoning questions (Quantitative Reasoning (All)) near zero at 0.29% recall / 0.44% precision, even as their count-producing sibling (Quantitative Reasoning (Count) (All)) scores 40.2% F1 — the largest number in the table.

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

By early 2018, factoid question answering over knowledge graphs and multi-turn dialog systems had mostly been studied in isolation. The dominant factoid QA benchmarks of the day — WebQuestions (Berant et al. 2013), SimpleQuestions (Bordes et al. 2015), WikiQA (Yang, Yih, and Meek 2015) — are all built around Simple Questions: each is answerable from a single tuple in the knowledge graph (KG), with no need to combine facts, apply logical operators, or track what was asked three turns earlier. Meanwhile the popular dialog corpora of the day — Twitter Conversations (Ritter, Cherry, and Dolan 2010), the Ubuntu Dialogue Corpus (Lowe et al. 2015), Movie-DiC (Banchs 2012) — have no knowledge graph behind them at all, so they can model conversational form (turn-taking, coreference, context) but never test whether a system can actually retrieve a correct fact. The one KG-grounded dialog dataset that existed, Bordes and Weston's (2016) goal-oriented restaurant-reservation bAbI tasks, used a KG with fewer than 10 cuisines, locations, and other slot values and dialogs with very few states; the Movie Dialog dataset of Dodge et al. (2015) paired QA with recommendation but its KG held only about 75K entities and 11 relations, and — critically for the authors' framing — its questions are not coherently linked into a conversation the way a person's follow-up questions are.

The paper's core claim is that a real assistant answering questions from a large-scale KG has to clear four hurdles simultaneously that no existing dataset tests together: (i) parse questions requiring logical, quantitative, and comparative reasoning over a larger subgraph than a single tuple — e.g. "Which rivers flow through India and China?" requires intersecting two sets of tuples, not looking up one; (ii) use conversational context to resolve coreferences and ellipsis — a later question may only make sense given what was asked and answered before it; (iii) recognize when a question is ambiguous and ask for clarification rather than guessing; and (iv) do all of this against a KG that is orders of magnitude larger than the toy KGs used in prior QA-meets-dialog work. No existing dataset combines a large, realistic KG with dialog-linked, complex (non-single-tuple) questions — that combination is what CSQA is built to supply, and the paper's second contribution is showing that combining today's best QA and dialog components does not come close to solving it.

// how it works

The method

Building the dataset

The knowledge graph is a filtered Wikidata dump from 14-Nov-2016, which as released contains 5.2K relations, 12.8M entities, and 52.3M facts. The authors keep only 330 "meaningful" relations (dropping obscure ones like "ISO 3166-1 alpha-2 code") and 642 entity types out of 30.8K (keeping types in the top-90th-percentile by tuple count for at least one retained relation), leaving 21.2M tuples over 12.8M unique entities, of which 3.8M appear in at least 3 tuples.

Question construction happens in stages, with in-house Computer-Science-graduate annotators identifying templates that crowdworkers on Amazon Mechanical Turk then instantiate and paraphrase at scale. Simple questions come in two of three possible forms the annotators identified — object-based ("Who is the CEO of Google?" → Sundar Pichai) and subject-based ("Which company is Sundar Pichai the CEO of?" → Google); the third, relation-based form, was dropped as unnatural. Across the 330 relations this yielded 1,531 subject-based and 1,450 object-based question templates (including paraphrases). Complex questions add four families of reasoning on top of these simple templates (Table 2 in the paper): logical (union/intersection/difference over one or multiple relations, e.g. "and", "or", "but not"), verification (a yes/no answer about whether a single or multiple entities satisfy a relation, e.g. "Does Ganga flow through India?"), quantitative (count, min/max, at-least/at-most/approximately-equal-to-N, each over a single or multiple entity types, plus "count over" variants combining counting with logical operators), and comparative (more/less between entities, plus "count over" comparative variants). After instantiation, only questions with fewer than 1,000 answers are retained. Finally, linked sequential QA stitches individual QA pairs into a coherent conversation: consecutive questions are connected only if they share a relation, subject, or object (never linked to arbitrary earlier turns), and separate templates convert simple/complex questions into "indirect" conversational ones using coreference ("that one"), ellipsis, and clarification devices. The end result is 200K dialogs, 1.6M turns, split by disjoint tuples so validation/test dialogs never reuse a tuple seen in training questions (Table 3): 152,391 train / 16,413 validation / 27,797 test dialogs, averaging 15.9 / 15.65 / 19.44 utterances per dialog and 1.2M / 0.13M / 0.27M question-bearing utterances respectively, with a 0.1M-word vocabulary (frequency ≥ 10).

The proposed model

Because CSQA sits at the intersection of dialog and QA, the authors propose a hybrid of the two era-leading architectures for each: the HRED hierarchical dialog encoder (Serban et al. 2016a) and the key-value memory network for QA (Miller et al. 2016). The model (Figure 1) has three parts. First, a hierarchical encoder: a lower-level RNN encodes each utterance, and a higher-level RNN encodes the sequence of utterance representations into a dialog-context vector q₁. Word inputs concatenate a GloVe embedding (for ordinary words) with a TransE knowledge-graph embedding (Bordes et al. 2013) learned offline for KG entities, using zeros for whichever half doesn't apply — this sidesteps blowing up the vocabulary with a separate trainable embedding for every entity name. Second, candidate generation and a key-value memory network: since attending over the full 21.2M-tuple KG per question is infeasible, tuples are pre-filtered by longest n-gram match to the question's entity mentions (the paper notes the average candidate set the model must attend over is still around 10K tuples). Each candidate tuple becomes a key (concatenated relation + subject TransE embedding) and value (object TransE embedding) pair; the model makes H passes over this memory, at each pass computing a softmax attention over keys and using it to update the query representation:

qj+1 = Rj( qj + Σᵢ Softmax(qjA·φK(khᵢ))·A·φV(vhᵢ) ) // A ∈ Rᵈˣᴰ and R₁…H ∈ Rᵈˣᵈ are learned; H = 2, following Miller et al. (2016)

Third, a decoder — an RNN sequence generator over a ~1,500-word shortlisted vocabulary of counts, yes/no tokens, and entity types, plus a special KG_WORD placeholder token. Whenever the decoder needs to emit a KG entity, it produces KG_WORD and a separate copy mechanism (Softmax(qH+1BφV(vhᵢ))) fills in the actual top-scoring entity or entities from the candidate set — avoiding a softmax over all 12.8M entities. Training minimizes the sum of cross-entropy loss over vocabulary tokens and over the copied KG entities. Hyperparameters (learning rate ∈ {1e-3, 4e-4}, hidden/embedding size ∈ {256, 512}, batch size ∈ {32, 64}, dialog context size 2) are tuned on the validation set with Adam.

// what they found

The results

Because CSQA turns fall into structurally different answer types, the paper evaluates them with three different metrics, reported per question category in Table 4 — and these categories and metrics do not mix. Questions whose answer is one or more KG entities (simple questions, logical-reasoning questions, entity-retrieval quantitative/comparative questions, and clarification requests) are scored by Recall and Precision over the entities produced. Questions whose answer is a count or a yes/no sequence — verification (Boolean), quantitative-reasoning "count" questions, and comparative-reasoning "count" questions — are scored by F1 on the predicted count/Boolean sequence, since the paper treats these as needing exact-match style accuracy over a short output rather than partial-credit set overlap. Clarification questions additionally have a natural-language-generation side, scored separately by BLEU-4 on the generated clarifying utterance.

On the Recall/Precision block, the model reaches 15.83% recall and 6.7% precision overall. Broken out by category: Simple Question (Direct) reaches 27.9% recall / 7.77% precision — the easiest category, as expected, though even this "solved" case is far from solved. Performance drops once context resolution is required: Simple Question (Coreferenced) falls to 12.31% recall / 3.84% precision, and Simple Question (Ellipsis) to 19.45% recall / 3.96% precision — both below the Direct case, most visibly on precision. Logical Reasoning (All) reaches 27.22% recall / 10.52% precision — comparable to or better than Direct simple questions on this metric pair, but still means fewer than three in ten relevant entities are recovered. Clarification questions reach 30.64% recall / 10.8% precision, the highest of this block. The two categories that collapse are Quantitative Reasoning (All), at 0.29% recall / 0.44% precision, and Comparative Reasoning (All), at 1.26% recall / 5.45% precision — both entity-retrieval variants of quantitative/comparative questions (e.g. "which rivers flow through at least N countries") are answered almost never.

On the separate F1 block, Verification (Boolean) (All) reaches 17.68% F1, Quantitative Reasoning (Count) (All) reaches 40.2% F1 — notably the single best number in the whole table, for the count-producing sibling of the quantitative category that scored near zero above — and Comparing Reasoning (Count) (All) reaches 11.86% F1. On the BLEU-4 block, Clarification (Natural Language Generation) reaches a score of 15.58.

The paper's discussion draws four specific lessons from this pattern. First, complex (logical/quantitative/comparative) questions expose that existing encoders have no explicit mechanism to decompose a question like "Which rivers flow through India and China?" into its component sub-queries before attending over memory — the HRED+KVmem encoder simply never learns this decomposition. Second, indirect (coreferenced/elliptical) questions score below their direct counterparts, showing the dialog-context encoder does not learn to weight named entities and relations specially within the context, even though those are exactly the words later questions depend on. Third, the n-gram-based candidate-generation step is brittle: because annotators were explicitly asked to paraphrase, simple n-gram matching against entity/relation surface forms misses paraphrased mentions, hurting recall, while for very popular entities (the paper gives U.S.A. as an example) it can pull over 100K candidate tuples into memory, hurting efficiency. Fourth, roughly 15% of questions require more than 100K candidate tuples in memory, which the paper argues a flat key-value memory organization cannot handle gracefully — both because of raw memory footprint and because the softmax over that many keys becomes prohibitively expensive at training and inference time.

// does it hold up?

The eval read

Like SimpleQuestions three years earlier, this is a dataset paper first and a method paper second, and the two need separate scrutiny.

baselines

There is, deliberately, no external baseline comparison at all — this is a brand-new task, and the paper's stated purpose is to show that the best available components (HRED for dialog, key-value memory networks for QA — both state of the art for their respective sub-problems as of 2016) are inadequate when combined, not to claim a new state of the art against prior systems. That framing is honest, but it also means every number in Table 4 is uncontextualized: there is no random-guess or majority-class row, no simpler retrieval baseline (e.g. exact tuple match without the neural encoder), and no ablation isolating how much of the failure is the encoder versus the brittle n-gram candidate-generation step the paper itself flags as a known weak point. Given that Quantitative Reasoning (All) scores 0.29% recall — a number close enough to a broken pipeline that it is hard to tell "the model doesn't understand min/max reasoning" apart from "the candidate generator rarely surfaces the right tuples for these questions" without such an ablation.

contamination

The paper explicitly constructs train/validation/test splits so that dialogs in validation and test do not contain questions built from KG tuples seen at training time (page 5), which is a real and stated safeguard against tuple-level leakage — a meaningfully more careful split discipline than many QA datasets of the era. What it does not, and cannot, guard against is template-level leakage: the crowdsourced question templates and paraphrase templates were collected once and then instantiated across all three splits, so while the specific (subject, relation, object) tuples differ between train and test, the linguistic patterns, coreference devices, and clarification phrasings a model sees at test time were, by construction, drawn from the same fixed template pool used to build the training set — the benchmark measures generalization across new facts and entities, not new question forms.

what's missed

The three-way metric split (Recall/Precision for entity-retrieval questions, F1 for count/Boolean questions, BLEU-4 for clarification text) is appropriate to the heterogeneous answer types, but it also makes the "overall" 15.83% recall / 6.7% precision headline easy to misread as a single number describing the dataset's difficulty — it excludes the count/Boolean and NLG categories entirely, so a reader who only remembers "15.83%" is missing that Quantitative Reasoning (Count) actually scores 40.2% F1, the best result in the paper, while the entity-retrieval sibling of that same reasoning category (Quantitative Reasoning (All)) scores 0.29% recall. The gap between those two numbers for ostensibly related question types is itself a finding the single overall statistic hides. Separately, the paper reports that around 15% of questions need >100K candidate tuples but does not report Table 4 broken out by candidate-set size, so it is not possible to tell from the paper how much of any given category's low score traces to candidate-generation failure versus reasoning failure.

would raise confidence

A simple non-neural baseline — e.g. exact/fuzzy tuple lookup without any learned encoder, on at least the Simple Question categories — would calibrate how much of the 27.9%/12.31%/19.45% recall spread across Direct/Coreferenced/Ellipsis is really about dialog-context modeling versus basic entity linking. An ablation replacing the brittle n-gram candidate filter with an oracle candidate set (i.e., always including the gold tuple) would isolate reasoning failure from retrieval failure, which matters most exactly where the paper's own numbers are most alarming (Quantitative Reasoning (All) at 0.29%). And reporting a template-held-out split — training and testing on disjoint template pools, not just disjoint tuples — would test genuine linguistic generalization rather than tuple-level memorization within a fixed template distribution.

// bottom line

CSQA's lasting contribution is the same as SimpleQuestions' before it: not the model, which the authors are explicit is a strawman combination meant to fail, but the dataset itself — 200K dialogs and 1.6M turns is, by a wide margin, the largest and most reasoning-diverse KG-grounded dialog benchmark that existed at the time, and its category-by-category breakdown (rather than one aggregate score) is exactly the right shape for a benchmark meant to expose specific model weaknesses. The near-zero score on entity-retrieval quantitative and comparative questions is a genuinely useful negative result — it says plainly that 2018-era memory networks have no mechanism for multi-set operations over a knowledge graph — but without an oracle-candidate or non-neural baseline ablation, a reader cannot fully separate "the model can't reason" from "the candidate generator never gave it a chance to."

// takeaways

What to remember

  1. Introduces CSQA: 200K dialogs, 1.6M turns, built over a filtered Wikidata subgraph of 21.2M tuples (330 relations, 642 entity types, 12.8M entities) — the first large-scale benchmark combining coherently linked conversational turns with logical, quantitative, and comparative reasoning questions rather than single-tuple lookups.
  2. A HRED-encoder + key-value-memory-network hybrid (H=2 hops, TransE entity embeddings, a copy mechanism for KG entities) reaches only 15.83% recall / 6.7% precision overall on entity-retrieval questions; it falls to 0.29% recall / 0.44% precision on Quantitative Reasoning (All) and 1.26% recall / 5.45% precision on Comparative Reasoning (All), while its count-style sibling, Quantitative Reasoning (Count) (All), scores a comparatively strong 40.2% F1 — the same broad reasoning category swings from near-zero to the paper's best result depending on whether the answer is a retrieved entity set or a count.
  3. The paper is explicit that its own model is meant to demonstrate inadequacy, not to be competitive: it flags a brittle n-gram-based candidate generator, an encoder with no explicit question-decomposition mechanism, and a flat key-value memory that cannot scale to the ~15% of questions needing over 100K candidate tuples, as the concrete gaps future work needs to close.
