Benchmarking Model-Based Reinforcement Learning

Eleven model-based RL algorithms, four model-free baselines, 18 environments, one shared protocol. The result isn't a leaderboard so much as a diagnosis: model-based methods learn faster early on, then, on most environments, plateau well below what model-free methods eventually reach (Hopper's SLBO is the one exception) — and the paper names three specific, reproducible reasons why.

Tingwu Wang, Xuchan Bao, Ignasi Clavera, Jerrick Hoang, Yeming Wen, Eric Langlois, Shunshi Zhang, Guodong Zhang, Pieter Abbeel, Jimmy Ba

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

Model-based reinforcement learning (MBRL) — learning a model of the environment's dynamics and planning or training a policy against it — is supposed to be the sample-efficient alternative to model-free RL (MFRL). By 2019 a run of papers (PETS, ME-TRPO, SLBO, MB-MPO) had each shown their own method matching model-free asymptotic performance with far fewer environment interactions. But as the paper puts it, "research in model-based RL has not been very standardized": authors evaluated on self-designed environments, modified reward functions and episode horizons without saying so consistently, and some codebases were never released. Single-agent model-free RL had already been through a version of this reckoning — the paper cites rllab (Duan et al., 2016), OpenAI Gym, and Henderson et al.'s "Deep Reinforcement Learning that Matters" (2018) as evidence that unstandardized comparisons in RL routinely produce results that don't replicate. No equivalent standardized comparison existed for MBRL specifically, which made it an open question — not a rhetorical one — how the growing list of MBRL algorithms actually stacked up against each other and against model-free baselines under identical conditions.

The authors' fix is a single shared benchmark: 18 continuous-control environments built on OpenAI Gym, a common training/evaluation protocol (same seed count, same reporting window, per-algorithm hyperparameter search under a fixed budget), and — the part that turns this into more than a leaderboard — a systematic look at why MBRL underperforms where it does, distilled into three named failure modes: the dynamics bottleneck, the planning horizon dilemma, and the early-termination dilemma.

// how it works

The method

The benchmark covers 11 MBRL algorithms grouped into three families the paper treats as structurally distinct: Dyna-style algorithms (ME-TRPO, SLBO, MB-MPO) that alternate between fitting a dynamics model and training a model-free policy on imagined rollouts from it; policy search via backpropagation through time (PILCO, iLQG, GPS, SVG) that differentiates the RL objective directly through a (Gaussian-process, linear-quadratic, or Gaussian-linear) dynamics model; and shooting methods (RS, MB-MF, PETS-RS, PETS-CEM) that plan by sampling and scoring candidate action sequences against the learned model at every step, executing only the first action (model-predictive control). Two additional ground-truth-dynamics variants, GT-CEM and GT-RS, swap the learned dynamics model for the simulator's real dynamics to give an upper-bound reference, and 4 model-free baselines — TRPO, PPO, TD3, SAC — are run under the exact same protocol so the model-based/model-free gap is measured, not assumed.

All 11 MBRL algorithms (10 of which learn the dynamics model; iLQG instead plans directly against the ground-truth dynamics, despite being grouped among the 11) plus the 4 model-free baselines — together with the 2 additional ground-truth-dynamics oracles (GT-CEM, GT-RS) and a Random baseline, 18 rows in the results table — are run on 18 environments spanning CartPole and Acrobot up to Humanoid, all rebuilt on top of OpenAI Gym with three deliberate, disclosed modifications: reward functions were reshaped so a gradient with respect to observations exists (needed by iLQG and GPS, which differentiate through the reward), early-termination (suffix "-ET") variants were added alongside the raw environments because early termination — standard in MFRL for faster, more directed exploration — had never been systematically tested in MBRL, and the stock Swimmer-v0 task (found to be "unsolvable for all algorithms") was re-instrumented with a relocated velocity sensor into an easier "Swimmer" variant, kept alongside the original for reference. Every algorithm is trained with 4 random seeds, and for each algorithm the authors run a grid search over its own hyperparameters (summarized per-algorithm in the appendix) and report the single hyperparameter setting with the best average performance — a "reference" hyperparameter combination that performs reasonably across all environments is reported separately, but it is not what generates the headline numbers.

// what they found

The results

At 200k timesteps (Table 1), the picture is close to a tie: "in general model-free and model-based methods are two almost evenly matched rivals when trained for 200,000 time-steps." On HalfCheetah, for instance, MB-MPO reaches 3639.0±1185.8 and TD3 reaches 3614.3±82.1 — nearly identical means, though MB-MPO's standard deviation is more than fourteen times larger. SAC is the strongest non-oracle algorithm on HalfCheetah at this budget (4000.7±202.1) — though the ground-truth-dynamics oracle GT-CEM dwarfs it at 14777.2±13964.2, and MB-MPO's own spread (3639.0±1185.8) overlaps SAC's. Averaged in rank across all 18 environments (Table 5, computed only over the 10 learned-dynamics MBRL algorithms — iLQG is excluded because it always uses ground-truth dynamics), PETS-CEM and SLBO tie for the best mean rank (4.0 of 10), while SLBO alone has the single best median rank (3.5 of 10); the worst performers by both measures are GPS (7.7 mean / 8.5 median) and PILCO (9.5 mean / 10 median, i.e. last on more than half the environments it could run on). The paper's own summary of this table is blunt: "across this very substantial benchmarking, there is no clear consistent best MBRL algorithm."

The more consequential finding shows up when training runs longer. Extending six algorithms — PETS-CEM, ME-TRPO, MB-MPO, SLBO, TD3 and SAC — to 1 million timesteps on HalfCheetah, Ant, Walker2D and Hopper (Table 4, Figure 2) reveals what the paper calls the dynamics bottleneck: on HalfCheetah, SAC climbs to 6095.5±936.1 and TD3 to 5072.9±815.8, while every learned-dynamics method plateaus far lower — MB-MPO at 4513.1±1045.4, PETS-CEM at only 2875.9±1132.2 (barely above its own 200k-step score of 2795.3), and ME-TRPO at 2672.7±1481.6. Table 4 also carries a seventh, ground-truth-dynamics CEM column at 14777.2±13964.2 — identical to the decimal to its Table 1, 200k-step value, so this reads as a re-printed reference ceiling rather than a re-run at 1M steps — and even taken that way it's evidence the environment itself supports much higher reward than any learned model captures. As the paper states plainly: "PETS's performance plateaus after 400k time-steps at a value much lower than the performance when using the ground-truth dynamics," and more data does not close the gap. The paper proposes two mechanisms: compounding one-step prediction error over long rollouts, and the coupling between policy and dynamics learning, which traps the agent in model-exploiting local minima. The pattern isn't universal, though: on Hopper, SLBO climbs to 2963.1±323.4 at 1M steps, above TD3's 2745.7±546.7 and within one std of SAC's 3020.3±134.6 — the one learned-dynamics method, on the one environment, that keeps pace with the model-free baselines rather than plateauing below them.

A second diagnosis, the planning horizon dilemma, applies to the shooting methods (Figure 3): "increasing the planning horizon does not necessarily increase the performance, and more often instead decreases the performance," with a horizon of roughly 20–40 steps working best for both ground-truth and learned dynamics. The paper attributes this to the curse of dimensionality in the search space as horizon grows, compounded by modelling error for learned dynamics. The third diagnosis, the early-termination dilemma, compares each environment against its "-ET" twin in Table 1: PETS-CEM falls from 1165.5±226.9 on Ant to 81.6±145.8 on Ant-ET, ME-TRPO from 282.2±18.0 to 42.6±21.1, MB-MPO from 705.8±147.2 to 30.3±22.3 — all learned-dynamics methods tested drop substantially. The paper reports trying several mitigation schemes for PETS specifically (padding rewards, adding "alive bonus" penalties during planning) and states plainly that "none of them were successful."

// does it hold up?

The eval read

As a reproduction study, this paper's rigor is largely in its favor: 4 seeds per run, a disclosed per-algorithm hyperparameter grid (appendix B), and — unusually for RL benchmarking papers of this era — an explicit statement that GPS's original codebase samples initial states from several fixed conditions while this benchmark instead samples from a continuous Gaussian distribution, which the authors flag themselves as making the task "harder to solve" for GPS specifically. That kind of self-reported caveat is exactly what the paper's own motivating citation, Henderson et al.'s "Deep Reinforcement Learning that Matters," argues is missing from most RL papers. Even so, three aspects of the design are worth reading carefully rather than taking the headline rankings at face value.

First, the headline numbers in Table 1 and Table 5 use, per algorithm, "the hyper-parameters producing the best average performance" — chosen per algorithm from a grid search, not a single fixed setting applied uniformly, and (per the per-algorithm appendix sections) tuned with visibility into the very same 200k-timestep evaluation window being reported. The appendix does separately report a cross-environment "reference" hyperparameter setting, which is arguably the more decision-relevant number for a practitioner picking an algorithm blind, but that reference setting is not what produces Table 1, Table 4, or the Table 5 rankings this page (and the paper's abstract) leads with.

Second, the "universal" language in the early-termination section is broader than the paper's own appendix supports for at least one case. The main text states "the early termination dilemma is universal in all MBRL algorithms we tested" (appendix G, restating the Table 1 pattern), and that holds for Ant and Hopper in the paper's own follow-up ablation (Table 21): learned-dynamics CEM drops from 1165.5±226.9 to 162.6±142.1 on Ant with ET, and from 1125.0±679.6 to 801.9±194.9 on Hopper. But the same table's Walker2D row runs the other way — learned-CEM goes from −493.0±583.7 (no ET) to 290.6±113.4 (with ET), an improvement, not a drop. That specific ablation uses a different, apparently fixed hyperparameter setting than Table 1's per-environment-tuned PETS-CEM Walker2D score (260.2±536.9 at 200k), so it isn't a direct contradiction of the headline Table 1 numbers — but it does mean "universal" oversells what the appendix itself shows once you leave the Ant/Hopper cases the prose focuses on. There's a plausible mechanical reason for the reversal the paper doesn't spell out: a fallen Walker2D that isn't terminated keeps accruing negative reward for the rest of the fixed-length episode, while early termination cuts the episode off at the fall — the same sign flip shows up in Table 1's Walker2D/Walker2D-ET pairs for RS (−2060.3→201.1), MB-MF (−2218.1→350.0), SVG (−1430.9→252.4) and SLBO (−1277.7→207.8). That makes the Walker2D reversal a return-scale artifact of how ET truncates the episode rather than an independent counterexample to the early-termination dilemma — a narrower, more defensible reading than treating it as an unexplained anomaly.

Third, look closely at which comparison a claim is actually making. The paper credits GPS with "the best performance in Ant-ET" (Section 4.3) — true only among the 10 learned-dynamics MBRL algorithms (GPS's 275.4±309.1 edges out RS's 239.9±81.7). The model-free baseline SAC scores 2012.7±571.3 on the same Ant-ET column — roughly 7.3× GPS's number, and, notably, a 4× increase over SAC's own Ant score (506.7±165.2) without early termination, the opposite direction from every model-based row in that column. But it's SAC alone that moves that way: the other three MFRL baselines drop just like the model-based rows do (PPO 321.0→80.1, TRPO 323.3→116.8, TD3 956.1→259.7), so the paper's silence is really about one algorithm out of eighteen responding oppositely to the same intervention, not about an unreconciled MFRL-vs-MBRL split. And neither GPS nor SAC is the column's true maximum: the ground-truth oracle GT-RS scores 2519.0±469.8 on Ant-ET, ahead of SAC's 2012.7 — a reader skimming for "who wins Ant-ET" could easily miss that the actual column-wide maximum is an oracle row, outside the comparison Section 4.3 is even making.

baselines

Current and comprehensive for 2019 — TRPO, PPO, TD3 and SAC cover both on- and off-policy state-of-the-art MFRL, run under the identical protocol (same seeds, same reporting window) as every MBRL method, which is the paper's main methodological contribution.

contamination

Not applicable in the usual data-leakage sense (this is a control benchmark, not a text/vision corpus). The nearest analogue is environment fidelity: all 18 environments are modified from stock OpenAI Gym in three ways (reward reshaping for iLQG/GPS, five added "-ET" early-termination variants, and a re-instrumented Swimmer), which the paper discloses but which also means none of these absolute numbers are directly comparable to numbers reported in the original per-algorithm papers (PETS, ME-TRPO, SLBO, MB-MPO) on their own environment variants.

what's missed

Statistical significance across only 4 seeds, when several headline gaps are smaller than one standard deviation (MB-MPO 3639.0±1185.8 vs. TD3 3614.3±82.1 on HalfCheetah is a coin flip's worth of separation) and some ground-truth cells carry a std nearly as large as the mean (GT-CEM HalfCheetah: 14777.2±13964.2). The paper cites Henderson et al. on exactly this reproducibility risk in its own introduction without applying that lesson to its own error bars in the text.

would raise confidence

A significance test (or at minimum a per-cell seed count check) on the closer Table 1 margins; a version of Table 5's ranking recomputed under the shared "reference" hyperparameters rather than each algorithm's per-environment best; and either resolving or explicitly flagging the Walker2D early-termination reversal in Table 21 rather than leaving "universal" as the only word attached to that finding.

// bottom line

The comparison protocol is the paper's real contribution, and it's a careful one — disclosed environment modifications, disclosed hyperparameter grids, a stated caveat about GPS's harder initial-state distribution. The three named failure modes (dynamics bottleneck, planning horizon dilemma, early-termination dilemma) are genuine, well-evidenced patterns, not just a restatement of "MBRL sometimes loses." But the paper's own appendix contains at least one exception to the "universal" framing it uses for early termination, and several of its headline rankings rest on per-environment-tuned hyperparameters and 4-seed error bars wide enough to make ties, not clean wins, the more honest reading of Table 1.

// takeaways

What to remember

  1. Under one shared protocol across 18 environments, model-based and model-free RL are "almost evenly matched" at 200k timesteps — but on most environments model-based methods stop improving with more data (the dynamics bottleneck) while model-free methods keep climbing, so the apparent parity at 200k steps mostly doesn't hold at 1M (Hopper's SLBO, which overtakes TD3, is the exception).
  2. By Table 5's own ranking of the 10 learned-dynamics MBRL algorithms across all 18 environments, PETS-CEM and SLBO tie for the best mean rank (4.0/10) and SLBO alone has the best median rank (3.5/10) — but "no clear consistent best MBRL algorithm," in the paper's own words, is the honest summary, not a single winner.
  3. The paper's three named failure modes are real and specific enough to be checkable, but two of the checks push back on the strongest phrasing: the early-termination "dilemma" has a documented exception (Walker2D, appendix Table 21), and "best performance" claims (e.g. GPS on Ant-ET) sometimes compare within the MBRL family only, not against the model-free baselines run in the very same table.
