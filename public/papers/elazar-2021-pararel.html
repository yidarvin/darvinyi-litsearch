Measuring and Improving Consistency in Pretrained Language Models

Ask a masked language model the same fact two different ways — "Homeland originally aired on [MASK]" vs. "Homeland premiered on [MASK]" — and it will often answer differently. PARAREL is 328 human-vetted paraphrase patterns spanning 38 T-REx relations, built to measure exactly that: not whether a model knows a fact, but whether it says the same thing about it twice. The best model tested, BERT-large, agrees with itself only 61.1% of the time; a proposed KL-divergence consistency loss narrows the gap by 5.8 points on held-out relations but shows no measurable benefit when carried to a real downstream task.

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

By early 2021, a substantial line of work — LAMA (Petroni et al., 2019), LPAQA (Jiang et al., 2020), AutoPrompt (Shin et al., 2020) — had been probing pretrained masked language models (BERT, RoBERTa, ALBERT) as implicit knowledge bases: fill in a cloze-style prompt like "Dante was born in [MASK]" and read off the model's top prediction as a fact. Nearly all of that work reports a single number: how often the top prediction is correct. What none of it checks is whether the model gives the same answer when the same fact is asked a different way. That gap matters specifically because the appeal of a PLM-as-KB is querying in free natural language rather than a fixed schema — but if "Homeland originally aired on [MASK]" and "Homeland premiered on [MASK]" pull different answers out of the same frozen weights, the model isn't really storing a fact, it's pattern-matching a phrasing.

Consistency itself wasn't a new concept — the paper's own related-work section cites Ribeiro et al. (2019) on consistency of QA models, Li et al. (2019) on consistency in NLI, Camburu et al. (2020) on inconsistent NLI explanations, and Kassner & Schütze (2020) on negation-induced inconsistency ("birds can fly" vs. "birds cannot fly"). What was missing was a systematic, multi-relation resource purpose-built to measure paraphrase consistency of factual knowledge across many PLMs at once, plus a first attempt at fixing it without full finetuning on a downstream task.

// how it works

The method

The paper defines a model as consistent on an N-1 (many-to-one) relation if, given two "quasi-paraphrase" cloze patterns for the same subject (e.g., "[X] was born in [Y]" / "[X] is native to [Y]"), it makes non-contradictory predictions across a large set of entities (the quasi-paraphrase notion is borrowed from Bhagat and Hovy, 2013, and deliberately looser than strict logical paraphrase). Concretely, the reported Consistency score for a relation is the share of agreeing pattern pairs: for each KB tuple, every one of the n(n−1)/2 pairs among its n patterns is checked, and Consistency is the percentage of those pairs that agree. Crucially, consistency does not require correctness — a model that confidently and consistently says the wrong city is "consistent" by this definition; correctness is tracked as a separate axis (Accuracy). Many-to-many (N-M) relations (e.g. shares-border-with, where Greece and Albania are both valid answers) are excluded from consistency scoring and instead scored for determinism — whether the model at least returns results in the same order.

Building PARAREL

PARAREL pairs 38 of LAMA's 41 T-REx relations (3 dropped as poorly defined or heterogeneous) with a total of 328 hand-vetted paraphrase patterns — an average of 8.63 per relation, ranging from 2 to 20. Construction was a four-step pipeline: (1) start from LAMA's one base pattern per relation; (2) add the subset of LPAQA's automatically mined paraphrases (back-translation and sentence extraction) that the authors judged to actually be correct paraphrases, discarding the rest; (3) mine additional patterns from Wikipedia sentences using SPIKE, a syntax-aware search engine, by searching for sentences containing a known subject-object pair and manually extracting the pattern; (4) add further paraphrases from the annotators' own linguistic judgment, then have two additional experts review every pattern and discard anything they couldn't agree on.

To check the result, the authors ran a human-agreement study: for each relation, up to five paraphrase pairs (156 candidate pairs total) plus 61 deliberately-mismatched control pairs were populated with random T-REx subject/object pairs and shown to NLP graduate students, who judged whether each pair was a genuine paraphrase. One annotation was collected per pair, and any pair that disagreed with the authors' own initial label was sent back for re-annotation to "fix random mistakes" — so the reported 95.5% (paraphrases) and 98.3% (controls) agreement rates measure agreement between an external annotator and the authors' pre-existing labels, not independent inter-annotator agreement between two blind raters.

To make PLMs behave like the KBs they're being probed as, predictions are restricted to a per-relation candidate set of valid objects pulled from T-REx (e.g., only real cities for a birthplace relation, not "tv"). The paper is explicit that this makes the task easier for the model — so any inconsistency measured here is, if anything, a floor rather than a ceiling on how inconsistent the unrestricted model would be. Objects that weren't a single token in every model's vocabulary were dropped, leaving 26,813 T-REx tuples split into 31 N-1 relations (scored for consistency) and 7 N-M relations (scored for determinism).

Improving consistency: a KL-divergence loss

Section 8 proposes a continued-pretraining objective. For each relation, all paraphrased patterns for a subject are encoded, and the model's output distributions over the candidate set are pulled toward each other with a symmetric KL-divergence term, summed over every pattern pair in the relation:

L = λ·Lc + LMLM // L_c = Σ_pairs [ D_KL(Q_n‖Q_m) + D_KL(Q_m‖Q_n) ] over the candidate-set distributions Q; L_MLM continues masked-LM training on the relation patterns themselves, to prevent catastrophic forgetting

The straightforward objective — forcing the two patterns' top-1 (argmax) predictions to match — is discrete and non-differentiable, so the paper relaxes it to matching full softmax distributions over the candidate set instead. Training uses only three relations (original-language, named-after, original-network — chosen to exclude the common location-based relations, to avoid train/test leakage into the held-out set), with 3 validation relations and 25 held-out test relations, on BERT-base, tuning λ ∈ {0.1, 0.5, 1} by validation Consistent-Acc.

// what they found

The results

All seven real PLMs tested — base and large versions of BERT, RoBERTa, and (smallest/largest) ALBERT, plus BERT-large whole-word-masking — hit a perfect 100.0% Succ-Patt (every pattern extracts the right answer for at least one entity somewhere), confirming the patterns themselves function correctly. But Succ-Objs — the share of entities any pattern gets right — tops out at 65.7% for BERT-large and bottoms at 45.8% for ALBERT-base, meaning roughly a third to a half of T-REx facts aren't recoverable from these models at all under any of the 8.63-paraphrases-per-relation on average.

Table 3's headline: Accuracy@1 (using the restricted candidate set) ranges 29.8%–48.7%; Consistency ranges 49.8%–61.1% among the real models, with BERT-large highest; and Consistent-Acc — correct and agreed-upon by every paraphrase of a relation, the paper's strictest combined measure — sits far below both, 16.4%–29.5%. Every BERT variant beats every RoBERTa and ALBERT variant on Consistency, reversing the accuracy/robustness ordering reported elsewhere for RoBERTa over BERT (Liu et al., 2019; Talmor et al., 2020) — the paper's hypothesis is that Wikipedia is BERT's dominant pretraining source but only a minor slice of RoBERTa's and ALBERT's, so more (non-Wikipedia) data doesn't straightforwardly help factual consistency. The majority row is a trivial baseline (always predict a relation's single most common object) that is perfectly consistent by construction — it's excluded from "best model" comparisons but shows the consistency floor a totally knowledge-free strategy already clears just by never changing its answer.

A pretraining-scale ablation on four RoBERTa checkpoints (Warstadt et al., 2020) trained on 1M, 10M, 100M, and 1B tokens shows Accuracy and Consistent-Acc rising monotonically with data (11.2→17.3→22.1→38.0 and 2.8→3.2→3.7→18.0), but Consistency does not: the 1M-token model scores 37.1, higher than both the 10M (29.8) and 100M (31.5) checkpoints, before the 1B model jumps to 50.6. The paper flags the 1M model's much smaller parameter count (chosen to avoid overfitting on so little data) as "a potentially crucial difference," while noting it is nonetheless striking that a model trained on far less data achieves better consistency.

Restricting to pairs whose subject–object dependency path differs (Diff-Syntax, i.e. syntax varies) or to pairs where syntax and lexical choice are both identical and only word order/tense can vary (No-Change) — both non-comparable subsets of the full pattern set — every one of the seven models is consistently more self-agreeing under No-Change (67.3%–81.1%) than Diff-Syntax (51.7%–69.7%), confirming that syntactic variation specifically, not just paraphrasing in general, degrades consistency; among those seven, RoBERTa-large is best on Diff-Syntax (69.7%) and BERT-large-wwm best on No-Change (81.1%).

A K-means clustering analysis (centroids set to the number of patterns, then separately to the number of subjects, per relation) confirms the visual read: cluster purity (V-measure) is consistently higher against pattern identity than against subject identity, and the correlation between that purity and a relation's actual Consistency score is close to zero for every model except BERT-large-wwm (Spearman 39.5%, p<0.05) — meaning representation geometry mostly doesn't explain which relations end up consistent.

Trained on just 3 relations and evaluated on 25 unseen ones, the full method (BERT-ft) improves Consistency by 5.8 points (58.2 → 64.0) and Consistent-Acc by 5.9 points (27.3 → 33.2) over untouched BERT-base, both significant by McNemar's test (p ≪ 0.01) — and generalizes past the training relations, since none of the 25 test relations were trained on. The ablation shows continued MLM training is doing real work: drop it and Consistency spikes to 80.8% — the highest value among the five actually-trained rows in the table, though still short of the majority baseline's trivial, always-agree 100.0% — but Accuracy collapses to 16.9% and Consistent-Acc to 9.1%, which the paper attributes to catastrophic forgetting — the model becomes consistent by becoming uniformly less knowledgeable, not more self-agreeing about what it knows.

// does it hold up?

The eval read

The central worry — is 61.1% Consistency measuring a real model failure, or an artifact of a badly-built paraphrase set? — gets a partial, careful answer from the paper's own design choices, but not a fully rigorous one. On the "real failure" side: Succ-Patt is a perfect 100.0% for every real PLM tested (every pattern does successfully extract the right answer somewhere — the trivial majority baseline sits slightly lower, at 97.3%, since it never predicts per-pattern at all), which rules out the patterns being simply broken or ungrammatical; and the candidate-set restriction makes the underlying task easier than open-vocabulary generation, so the paper's own framing — that true consistency is likely lower still — is defensible rather than a dodge. On the "measurement artifact" side, though, the headline 95.5%/98.3% "human agreement" numbers are weaker evidence than they look: only one external annotation was collected per candidate pair, and any disagreement with the authors' own pre-assigned label triggered re-annotation rather than being kept as a genuine second opinion — so this is agreement-with-the-authors', not blind inter-annotator agreement between independent raters, and it's reported over a 156-pair sample, not the full 328-pattern resource.

The generalization test for the improvement method is the sharpest finding on the page, and it cuts against the paper's own stated motivation. Section 9 opens by framing consistency as valuable specifically because "obtaining this skill from a pretrained model... has the potential to make specialized consistency solutions in downstream tasks redundant" — but when the authors actually finetuned the consistency-improved model on SQuAD and tested it on paraphrased SQuAD questions (Gan and Ng, 2019), they report the results "perform on par with the baseline model, both on SQuAD and the paraphrase questions." A 5.8-point gain on the paper's own intrinsic probe produced no visible transfer to the one downstream robustness test they ran — the paper is upfront about this ("more research is required to show that consistent PLMs can also benefit downstream tasks"), but it means the improvement's practical value is, on the paper's own evidence, unproven.

baselines

Only masked LMs of one architectural family and era (BERT/RoBERTa/ALBERT, all released 2019) are compared; AutoPrompt (Shin et al., 2020) is cited as accuracy-superior to manual patterns but never run through the same consistency pipeline, so it's unclear whether automatically-mined prompts are more or less self-consistent than PARAREL's curated ones. The majority baseline is trivially and vacuously 100% consistent by never varying its answer — correctly flagged by the paper, and correctly excluded from "best model" claims, but it's the only non-PLM reference point offered.

contamination

Not applicable in the usual train/test sense — the whole premise is probing what BERT/RoBERTa/ALBERT memorized during pretraining, so T-REx facts being "in" pretraining data is the point, not a leak. The one place leakage is a real risk — the consistency-improvement method's 3-relation train / 25-relation test split — is handled deliberately: location-based relations are excluded from training specifically "to prevent train-test leakage" (footnote 16).

what's missed

Consistency is decoupled from correctness by design (a model can be consistently wrong, as Table 6's iCloud→developer example shows: three different wrong answers, still scored on Accuracy=0 but contributing to the inconsistency count), and the paper is careful to report Consistent-Acc alongside Consistency for exactly this reason — but a reader who only remembers "61.1% consistent" risks forgetting that headline number says nothing about correctness on its own. The N-M/determinism relations (7 of 38) get one sentence and two numbers (52.9%/44.6% for BERT-large/RoBERTa-base) rather than the full per-model, per-relation breakdown given to the 31 N-1 relations.

would raise confidence

A second, independent round of paraphrase annotation (rather than authors'-label-plus-mismatch-correction) would put real numbers behind the 95.5%/98.3% quality claim. Running the consistency loss on a fourth architecture family (T5 or GPT-2, both name-checked in the intro but never tested) would clarify whether the MLM-specific candidate-set setup is why BERT beats RoBERTa/ALBERT, or whether the effect is more general. And a second downstream-transfer test beyond SQuAD-paraphrase — which showed no gain — would tell us whether that null result is about SQuAD specifically or about the method.

// bottom line

PARAREL is a well-built, if narrowly validated, measurement instrument, and the consistency numbers it produces (61.1% at best, on an easier-than-real-world candidate-restricted task) are a legitimate and still-cited demonstration that early-2020s masked LMs don't behave like the knowledge bases they were being pitched as. The proposed fix is real and statistically significant on the paper's own intrinsic metric, but the paper's own downstream test found no benefit from it — which is the honest, if less quotable, result.

// takeaways

What to remember

  1. PARAREL: 328 human-vetted paraphrase cloze patterns across 38 T-REx relations, built from LAMA base patterns, filtered LPAQA paraphrases, SPIKE-mined Wikipedia patterns, and expert additions — designed to test whether a PLM gives the same answer to differently-phrased versions of the same factual query, independent of whether that answer is correct.
  2. Best-model Consistency tops out at 61.1% (BERT-large) on a task made easier by restricting predictions to a per-relation candidate set of valid objects; BERT beats RoBERTa and ALBERT on consistency across every size pairing, the reverse of the usual accuracy ranking, which the paper attributes to Wikipedia's outsized share of BERT's pretraining data.
  3. A KL-divergence consistency loss (continued MLM pretraining on 3 relations) improves Consistency by 5.8 points and Consistent-Acc by 5.9 points on 25 held-out relations, significant by McNemar's test — but produced no measurable improvement when the resulting model was finetuned on SQuAD and tested against paraphrased SQuAD questions, the paper's own chosen downstream check.
