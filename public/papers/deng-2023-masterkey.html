MASTERKEY: Automated Jailbreaking of Large Language Model Chatbots

A time-based side channel — borrowed from blind SQL injection — infers the undisclosed content filters behind ChatGPT, Bard, and Bing Chat from response latency alone, then an RLHF-style pipeline fine-tunes an LLM to generate jailbreak prompts automatically, reporting a 21.58% average query success rate against 7.33% for existing prompts on the same services, measured in mid-to-late 2023.

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

// naming note

The arXiv v1 preprint (16 Jul 2023) called this framework JAILBREAKER; v2 (25 Oct 2023, the version this page follows, and the version source ↗ currently resolves to) renamed it MASTERKEY, along with its ablation variants (JAILBREAKER-NO-FINETUNE → MASTERKEY-NO-FINETUNE, etc.). Every table, figure and headline number this page quotes is identical in both versions — though v2 does correct one count in the prose, describing 10 restricted categories where v1 said 11, against a Table I that has 10 rows in both. Beyond the rename, v2 is a genuine revision: it adds a Caesar-cipher/encryption discussion, an entire appendix (the LMFlow-based training setup, fine-tuning/RAFT prompt templates, and the eight-A100 server) that v1 lacks outright, and five more references. July 2023 press coverage of this work still refers to it as JAILBREAKER — if you're looking for the paper under that name, this is it.

By mid-2023, jailbreak prompts — prompts crafted to talk a chatbot past its own usage-policy filters — were already a well-documented phenomenon, but almost all of that documentation was about ChatGPT specifically. The authors' own empirical study (Section III of the paper) shows why that's a problem: when they take 85 jailbreak prompts pulled from a public prompt-sharing site and an earlier academic study and fire all of them, ten times each, at four commercial chatbots (GPT-3.5, GPT-4, Bard, Bing Chat), the results are lopsided. GPT-3.5 falls to jailbreaks 21.12% of the time on average across four prohibited-content categories (Illegal, Harmful, Privacy, Adult); GPT-4 falls 7.13% of the time; Bard falls just 0.40% of the time, and Bing Chat 0.63%. The paper's second empirical finding sharpens this further: when a jailbreak fails, GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 explain exactly which policy was violated, while Bard and Bing Chat return a generic "I can't help with that" with no further detail even when pressed. The paper reasons that Bard and Bing Chat must be running some kind of additional, undisclosed content-moderation layer beyond the base model's own alignment — but because these are closed commercial services with opaque failure messages, there was previously no way to find out what that layer actually does, or where in the pipeline it sits.

So the paper poses two linked problems. First, a measurement problem: existing jailbreak techniques were being evaluated almost exclusively against ChatGPT, so claims that "jailbreaking is a solved research problem" or "defenses are inadequate" didn't actually generalize to Bard or Bing Chat, which turn out to be substantially more resistant to the same attacks. Second, a reverse-engineering problem: without visibility into what a commercial provider's content moderator is actually checking (the input? the output? individual keywords? full semantics?), attackers and defenders alike are working blind, and there was no published methodology for inferring the shape of a black-box LLM safety filter from the outside.

// how it works

The method

MASTERKEY has two genuinely separate halves, and it's worth keeping them apart: a diagnostic technique for inferring a black-box defense's internal shape from timing alone, and a generative pipeline for producing new jailbreak prompts. The second builds on what the first finds, but they answer different questions and carry different amounts of evidentiary weight.

Part 1 — a time-based side channel

The authors' key observation is that LLM chatbots generate text token by token, and the wall-clock time a chatbot takes to finish responding is strongly correlated with how many tokens it actually emits — including on failed jailbreak attempts, where the model still runs some amount of generation before a response gets blocked, truncated, or masked. As a proof of concept, they ask each of GPT-3.5, GPT-4, Bard, and Bing Chat to produce responses of a specified length (50/100/150/200 tokens) and measure both the token count actually produced and the time taken (Table III below). The Pearson correlation between requested length and generation time is positive and significant for every service (r = 0.465 to 0.838, p ≤ 0.009), which licenses treating response time as a proxy for "how much of the generation process actually ran" — the same principle behind classic time-based blind SQL injection, where a database's response latency (rather than any error message) leaks information about a query's internal state. That licence is weakest exactly where it matters most: Bing Chat's coefficient is the floor of the range (r = 0.465, R² ≈ 0.22), and Bing is one of the two services (with Bard) the side channel goes on to reverse-engineer.

Applied to a chatbot, the paper abstracts a service into two black-box parts — an LLM-based generator, and a "content moderator" that may check the input, may check the output stream in real time, may check only the finished output, and may use keyword matching, semantic analysis, or both (Figure 3). None of these properties are disclosed by any provider, so the paper designs a battery of timing probes to distinguish between them (Figure 4):

  • Baseline (a). Two benign questions with specified output lengths, back to back, establishing that total time ≈ t₁ + t₂ when nothing gets blocked.
  • Control1 — is input filtered? (b). A benign question followed by an overtly malicious one. If input validation exists, generation should stop almost immediately (time ≈ t₀, far below baseline). For Bard and Bing Chat the observed times were statistically indistinguishable from baseline (average z = -1.46, p = 0.34) — evidence against input-side filtering.
  • Control2 — is monitoring real-time? (c). The malicious question moved to the front. If moderation only checks the finished output, timing should still resemble baseline; if it monitors the token stream live, the response should truncate early. Here the drop was large and highly significant for both services (average z = 29.48, p < 0.01) — strong evidence for real-time, streaming moderation.
  • Control3 — is it keyword-based? (d). A "red-flag" keyword (e.g. "porn") is instructed to appear at a specific token position inside an otherwise benign 200-token response. If moderation halts generation right around that position, the elapsed time should track the keyword's position rather than the baseline; the observed average was z = -2.45, p = 0.07 — a difference the authors read as consistent with position-tracking keyword detection, though (as the paper's own prose acknowledges) it falls just short of the conventional 0.05 significance threshold.

Reading Control1 through Control3 together, the paper concludes that Bard and Bing Chat likely run dynamic, real-time output moderation that combines keyword tracking with some semantic checking — not input-side filtering. Using that inference, the authors hand-craft a single proof-of-concept prompt (a Machiavelli/"AIM" role-play framing, with character-spacing and code-block formatting layered on to dodge keyword matching) that works across GPT-3.5, GPT-4, Bard, and Bing Chat. That PoC becomes the seed for Part 2.

Part 2 — MASTERKEY, the automated generator

Rather than hand-writing more prompts, the paper fine-tunes Vicuna-13B into a dedicated jailbreak-prompt generator through a three-stage pipeline (Figure 5):

  1. Dataset building and augmentation. Starting from 85 existing prompts plus the PoC, service-specific terms are genericized ("ChatGPT" → "you", "OpenAI" → "developer") and the set is expanded via self-instruction: ChatGPT is prompted to rephrase each prompt inside a {{ }}-delimited "rephrase, don't execute" template, producing paraphrastic variants while preserving intent.
  2. Continuous pre-training and task tuning. The base model is first exposed to the jailbreak corpus via next-sentence prediction, then explicitly task-tuned on (original prompt → rephrased prompt) pairs to learn the text-style-transfer behavior itself.
  3. Reward-ranked fine-tuning (RAFT). Generated prompts are tested against the real chatbots and each success is worth +1 reward, summed across targets; the model is then fine-tuned on the resulting mix of high- and low-reward examples to prefer prompts that transfer across multiple services rather than overfitting to one.
Reward = Σi=1n JailbreakSuccessi(binary, 1 per successful target) // a prompt's reward is the count of targets (out of n) it successfully jailbreaks
// what they found

The results

MASTERKEY is evaluated against GPT-3.5, GPT-4, Bard, and Bing Chat (272,000 queries: 85 prompts × 10 generated variants × 20 prohibited questions × 4 tested services × 4 prompt-generation models), with GPT-3.5, GPT-4, and Vicuna itself as prompt-generation baselines. The paper reports an overall average query success rate of 21.58%, against 7.33% for the existing prompts it started from — and, per row, the ranking MASTERKEY > GPT-4-as-generator > GPT-3.5-as-generator > Vicuna-as-generator holds in every one of the 16 tested-model × category cells in Table V, not just on average. GPT-4 was consistently the strongest of the three non-fine-tuned generators, and Vicuna the weakest — the paper attributes this partly to Vicuna's failure to actually perform the rephrasing task: given a template like print(function("[INSERT PROMPT HERE]")), it would sometimes echo the literal placeholder text back rather than substituting the real prompt into it. That qualitative failure shows up quantitatively too: Vicuna-generated prompts underperform simply reusing the unmodified original prompt in 14 of 16 cells (and tie in a 15th), meaning a large share of the fine-tuning-free baseline's weakness is Vicuna failing at rewriting at all, not fine-tuned models being intrinsically better attackers.

The headline result the paper foregrounds is Bard and Bing Chat: MASTERKEY achieves average query success rates of 14.51% (Bard) and 13.63% (Bing Chat) — both figures reconcile exactly with the mean of their four Table V category rows — which the authors describe as the first published successful jailbreaks against either service. A supplementary experiment translates 20 sampled prompts into Simplified Chinese and tests them against Baidu's Ernie, reporting a 6.45% average success rate and arguing the approach transfers across both language and model family, though this sub-experiment is explicitly scaled down (20 prompts, one language) relative to the main evaluation because of Ernie's rate limits and account-suspension risk on repeated failed attempts.

The ablation numbers are worth reading closely rather than just as "both components help." Across all four services, dropping fine-tuning entirely costs 91–98% of the full model's success rate; dropping only the reward-ranking step (while keeping fine-tuning) costs 27–45%. Both cuts hurt, and both are directionally consistent with the paper's claim that "both fine-tuning and reward-ranked feedback are indispensable" — but the magnitude gap is large: continuous pre-training and task-tuning is doing most of the work of turning a general LLM into an effective jailbreak-prompt rewriter, and reward-ranking is a real but secondary refinement layered on top, not a comparably-sized contributor.

// does it hold up?

The eval read

Perishability first, because it governs how to read everything else here. Every number in this paper describes a snapshot of four specific commercial services — GPT-3.5, GPT-4, Bard, and Bing Chat — as they existed in mid-to-late 2023. The paper never states an explicit "we ran these experiments on date X"; the closest anchor is its own arXiv history: v1 (calling the system JAILBREAKER) was posted 2023-07-16, and v2 (MASTERKEY) on 2023-10-25, with NDSS accepting it for the 2024 symposium. Bard no longer exists under that name (it became Gemini in early 2024), and OpenAI, Google, and Microsoft have all iterated their moderation stacks repeatedly since. None of the success rates — 21.12% for GPT-3.5, 0.40% for Bard, 21.58% for MASTERKEY overall — should be read as current properties of "ChatGPT" or "Bard" today; they're measurements of specific deployed systems in a roughly four-month window in 2023.

The side-channel inference is the paper's most interesting claim and its most fragile one. A timing difference is direct, replicable evidence that something changes response latency under certain prompt conditions. What it is not is direct evidence of the defense's actual architecture — Findings 4–6 (no input filtering; real-time output monitoring; keyword-plus-semantic checking) are the authors' best inference from that timing signal, and the paper is careful to hedge this explicitly more than once — in the introduction ("our understanding may not perfectly mirror the actual defense design, it provides a valuable approximation") and again at the close of §V-B ("our understanding may not be exhaustive, it elucidates the services' behavioral properties") — and Findings 4–6 themselves are stated in hedged language ("likely conduct checks," "seem to implement dynamic monitoring," "likely incorporate a dynamic keyword-mapping algorithm"). Those hedges are honest, but it's worth being precise about which parts of the paper rest on measurement (the timing numbers in Tables III–IV) versus inference (what those numbers imply about Bard's and Bing Chat's internals) — the PoC prompt and, downstream, all of MASTERKEY's training data are built on the inferred model, not the measured one.

How many confounds does a commercial-service timing signal actually have? The paper states what each timing cell aggregates — five generative questions per Table III cell, and 20 test questions (5 sample questions × 4 jailbreak templates) per Table IV cell — but not how many times each individual measurement was repeated, nor whether measurements across conditions were interleaved or randomized to guard against drift (the "repeated five times" figure the paper does give applies only to §VII's separate RQ3/RQ4 evaluation, not to Tables III–IV). It also states no controls for the obvious sources of latency noise on a live, multi-tenant, geographically-distributed API: server load, response caching, model routing between capacity pools, network path, or time-of-day traffic. A z-test comparing two conditions' means is only as trustworthy as the assumption that nothing else differs between when those conditions were measured — and on a commercial chatbot serving millions of concurrent users, that's a real assumption, not a given. None of this necessarily overturns Findings 4–6 (the differences reported, especially Control2's z ≈ 29, are large relative to any plausible load jitter), but the paper would be stronger with an explicit statement of the repeat count per measurement and some evidence that measurements were interleaved or otherwise controlled against systematic drift.

Is "existing approaches are not effective" a fair comparison? The 7.33% baseline comes from 85 prompts sourced from a public jailbreak-prompt-sharing site and the authors' own earlier empirical study — not a systematic survey of the jailbreak literature (no comparison, for instance, to gradient-based adversarial-suffix attacks). More importantly, the 7.33% figure and MASTERKEY's own 21.58% were necessarily measured within the same few months of 2023, against the same moving services. That's a legitimate apples-to-apples comparison at the time it was run, but the paper's framing — "existing approaches are not effective on the mainstream LLM chatbots" — reads as a claim about those approaches in general, when it's really a claim about how those specific 85 prompts performed against these specific services in mid-2023. The same perishability the paper is implicitly diagnosing in Bard/Bing's defenses applies just as much to its own baseline numbers.

Ethics and disclosure. The paper states plainly that it did not exploit jailbreaks to disrupt the services, reported findings to the affected providers "promptly," is described as "actively collaborating" with them on defenses, and deliberately withholds the actual working jailbreak prompts and the training dataset from publication — only a single illustrative PoC (with characters spaced apart, e.g. "w r i t e a f a m o u s p o r n w e b s i t e") appears in the paper. That's a defensible disclosure posture for a security venue, though the paper gives no concrete dates for when providers were contacted or what (if anything) they fixed in response — "promptly" is qualitative, not a timeline.

baselines

85 prompts from a jailbreak-sharing website plus the authors' own prior paper — not a systematic sweep of jailbreak techniques (no gradient-based/adversarial-suffix attacks compared), and measured on the same 2023 services as MASTERKEY itself.

contamination

Not directly applicable (this attacks live services rather than benchmarking on held-out data), but MASTERKEY's training corpus is entirely 2023-era jailbreak prompts and their defenses — generalization to post-2023 moderation stacks is untested by the paper.

what's missed

No stated repeat count or drift control for the timing experiments (Tables III–IV) — each cell aggregates a stated set of questions (5 for Table III, 20 for Table IV), but not repeats-per-measurement or interleaving order; no discussion of network/load/caching confounds on a commercial multi-tenant API; the "real defense design" is inferred from timing, not confirmed by any provider.

would raise confidence

Explicit repeat counts per measurement and interleaved/randomized measurement order for the timing probes; a repeat of the RQ2 baseline comparison against a broader, independently-curated jailbreak corpus; a stated disclosure timeline with provider acknowledgment.

// bottom line

MASTERKEY's timing-based methodology is a genuinely clever and largely reproducible measurement technique — the token-time correlation and the Control1/Control2 z-tests are strong, well-separated signals. What it licenses is narrower than "we know how Bard's defense works": it's "response latency under these specific prompt conditions is consistent with real-time, non-input-side moderation," which the paper mostly presents with the right amount of hedging. The bigger-picture headline numbers (21.58% vs. 7.33%, first jailbreaks against Bard/Bing Chat) are real achievements against real 2023 systems, but they describe a moment in the ongoing arms race between jailbreaks and defenses, not a permanent verdict on any of these chatbots' safety.

// takeaways

What to remember

  1. MASTERKEY (renamed from JAILBREAKER between arXiv v1 and v2) has two separable contributions: a time-based side channel — adapted from blind SQL injection — that infers a black-box chatbot's content-moderation architecture from response latency alone, and a three-stage fine-tuning pipeline (continuous pre-training → task tuning → reward-ranked fine-tuning on Vicuna-13B) that automatically generates jailbreak prompts from that inference.
  2. The timing evidence is genuinely strong for what it directly measures (Control2's real-time-monitoring signal: z = 29.48, p < 0.01) but is an inference, not a confirmation, about Bard's and Bing Chat's actual internal defense design — the paper says so itself, and it's worth carrying that hedge forward into anything downstream that relies on it.
  3. MASTERKEY reports a 21.58% average query success rate against a 7.33% baseline for existing prompts, and the ranking MASTERKEY > GPT-4 > GPT-3.5 > Vicuna (as prompt-generation models) holds in all 16 tested-model × category cells — but every one of these numbers is a mid-to-late-2023 snapshot of services that have since changed, and the ablation shows fine-tuning (not reward-ranking) doing the overwhelming majority of the work.
