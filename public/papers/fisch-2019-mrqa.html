MRQA 2019 Shared Task: Evaluating Generalization in Reading Comprehension

Ten teams trained reading-comprehension systems on a fixed pool of six QA datasets and were ranked on twelve they'd never trained on — six with a peek at dev data, six completely hidden. The winning system, D-Net, beat the organizers' BERT-Large baseline by 10.7 F1 (72.5 vs. 61.8), but every leaderboard entry above that baseline belongs to an external team's submission — the organizers contribute only the BERT-Large/BERT-Base baselines to the leaderboard — and the organizers' own meta-analysis credits most of that gap to which pretrained language model a team happened to plug in, not to any of the generalization-specific tricks (adversarial training, multi-task learning) the task was designed to surface.

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

By 2019, reading comprehension had a benchmark for almost everything — SQuAD, TriviaQA, Natural Questions, HotpotQA — but each one measured the same thing: how well a system does on test examples drawn from the same distribution it trained on. The paper's introduction is blunt about the gap this leaves: "MRQA systems are still primarily evaluated on in-domain accuracy. It remains challenging to build MRQA systems that generalize to new test distributions... and are robust to test-time perturbations." A system could top every SQuAD leaderboard and still fail the moment the passages come from a textbook instead of Wikipedia, or the questions come from a domain expert instead of a crowdworker.

The organizers' fix is a shared task, not a new dataset: take 18 existing QA datasets — not all of them extractive to begin with; the paper notes it is “easier to convert multiple-choice datasets to extractive datasets than converting extractive datasets to multiple-choice” — and reformat every one of them into an identical schema (question, context, answer span), and then partition them by role rather than by content. Six datasets are for training only; six more are shown during development but scored on hidden test portions; the final six are never seen at all until scoring. Every participating team trains under the exact same six-dataset diet and is judged purely on how well that training transfers outward — which is the paper's actual contribution: a controlled, shared measurement of out-of-domain transfer, not a leaderboard for any single dataset.

// how it works

The task, the schema, and the baseline

Every one of the 18 sub-domains is converted to the same extractive format: a question, a context (concatenated and truncated to the first 800 tokens, with [DOC]/[TLE]/[PAR] separator tokens), and an answer that must appear as a literal span in the context. Datasets that weren't already extractive get adapted — multiple-choice datasets (RACE, MCTest, TextbookQA, BioProcess) keep only the correct option if it appears verbatim in the passage and drop options-dependent questions like "which of the following..."; datasets without labeled spans (DROP, DuoRC, QAMR) get every occurrence of the answer string tagged in the context. The paper is explicit that this format choice is a deliberate scope restriction: "the task does not test unanswerable, multi-turn, or open-domain question types" (footnote 2) — this is a shared task about extractive span generalization specifically, not question-answering generalization in general.

The 18 sub-domains are split into three fixed roles (Table 1):

  • Split I — training pool (in-domain, 6 datasets): SQuAD, NewsQA, TriviaQA, SearchQA, HotpotQA, Natural Questions. Participants may train on these and see their dev sets; there is no held-out test portion for them at all — Split I exists purely to constrain and standardize what every team trains on.
  • Split II — out-of-domain, dev shown (6 datasets): BioASQ, DROP, DuoRC, RACE, RelationExtraction, TextbookQA. No training data; a dev set is released — from 674 examples for RACE up to 2,948 for RelationExtraction — so teams can inspect the domain before being scored on its held-out test portion.
  • Split III — out-of-domain, fully hidden (6 datasets): BioProcess, ComplexWebQ, MCTest, QAMR, QAST, TREC. No training data and no dev data — participants never see so much as an example before the final scoring run.

To keep the held-out comparison fair across sub-domains of very different native sizes, Splits II and III are each rebalanced to roughly 1,500 test examples per dataset — though three sub-domains (BioProcess, QAST, TREC) fall well short of that target — partitioned by context so no single passage leaks across a dev/test boundary. The organizers also state explicitly that they draw from each dataset's original test split first before touching train/dev data, "by ensuring that no original test data is leaked into non-hidden splits of the MRQA dataset" (footnote 3) — a leakage control worth noting up front, since it bears directly on how much to trust the "hidden" claim for Split II/III.

The organizers' own baseline (run at both BERT-Base and BERT-Large sizes) follows a single recipe based on BERT (following the MultiQA setup of Talmor & Berant, 2019): concatenate question and passage with special tokens, encode with BERT, and train separate MLPs to predict start/end indices independently, decoding the span that maximizes p_start(i) × p_end(j). Because BERT's 512-token window is shorter than the 800-token passages, each example is chunked with a sliding window (stride 128), and training keeps only chunks that contain the answer. For multi-task training, up to 75K examples are sampled from each of the six Split I datasets, combined, and shuffled into mixed batches. Everything past this point — XLNet, ERNIE 2.0, adversarial domain discriminators, mixture-of-experts layers — belongs to the ten participating teams, not to the organizers.

Ten teams submitted final systems (Table 2); six of them wrote system-description papers, which the organizers summarize: D-Net (Baidu) ensembles an XLNet-based and an ERNIE 2.0-based model; Delphi (Apple) fine-tunes XLNet with carefully sampled negative examples; HLTC (HKUST) studies data-shuffling schemes on top of XLNet; CLER (Fuji Xerox) adds a mixture-of-experts layer on BERT with NLI multi-task training; Adv. Train (42Maru/Samsung) trains a domain discriminator adversarially against the QA model to learn domain-invariant features; and HierAtt (Alexandria University) freezes BERT-Base and trains only two attention layers on top. Systems are ranked by macro-averaged F1 across all 12 held-out datasets (Split II + III combined).

// what they found

The results

The full leaderboard (Table 3) ranks 11 rows total: 9 of the 10 participant submissions (PAL IN DOMAIN, from UC Irvine, is listed among participants in Table 2 but has no row in the results table at all) plus the organizers' own two baselines. D-Net (Baidu) takes the top spot at 72.5 F1 on the combined Split II+III test portion, 10.7 points above the organizers' BERT-Large baseline (61.8) — matching the abstract's headline number exactly. Broken down by split, D-Net's margin over BERT-Large is 11.5 points on Split II (68.9 vs. 57.4, the datasets with dev exposure) and 10.0 points on Split III (76.1 vs. 66.1, the fully hidden datasets). Five of the six teams that wrote a system paper outscored the BERT-Large baseline; the sixth, HierAtt, did not (56.1 vs. 61.8) — of the nine submissions that have a Table 3 row at all (PAL IN DOMAIN does not), two trail the baseline: HierAtt and BERT-Multi-Finetune, at 60.3. So it is not the case that every submission, or even every submission with a published system description, beat the baseline — only most of them.

A striking finding the organizers highlight themselves (Section 6.1): "there is a very strong correlation between in-domain and out-of-domain performance" — the same systems that top the Split I (in-domain) column also top the out-of-domain columns, in almost the same order. Per-dataset, the paper's own comparison (Section 6.1) says the largest gain over baseline is on DROP (43.5 → 61.5 F1) and the smallest is on RelationExtraction — the paper's own printed comparison pairs 84.9 F1 against D-Net's 89.0, but 84.9 matches Adv. Train's Table 6 cell rather than the organizers' own BERT-Large baseline (84.7), a one-digit difference that reads more like a transcription slip than a deliberate choice of comparator. Either way the substantive point holds: RelationExtraction has the smallest gap regardless (4.1 points against Adv. Train, 4.3 against the baseline) — a domain built from templated relation-extraction questions that every one of the 11 systems already clears at 81.3-89.0 F1, leaving comparatively little room for "generalization" to show up in the score.

The organizers' own meta-analysis (Section 6.2) credits the bulk of the gain to base-model choice, not technique: "The top three performing systems all use XLNet instead of BERT-Large — this isolated change in pre-trained language model alone yields a significant gain in overall in- and out-of-domain performance." D-Net's own edge specifically comes from ensembling two different pretrained models (XLNet-L and ERNIE 2.0), which the organizers describe as a general pattern — ensembling submissions "all reported modest gains," at the cost of training/inference efficiency. Of the technique-specific ideas the task was designed to test: adversarial domain-invariance training (Adv. Train) gained +1.9 F1 over its own BERT-Base run but only +0.4 F1 over its stronger BERT-Large run, and, per the organizers, "helped on most of the datasets (9 out of 12), but also hurt performance on some of them"; multi-task learning with NLI produced a "modest gain" of +0.7 F1 for CLER on the Split II dev portion, and no improvement at all for D-Net's own ablation.

Section 6.3 asks how much of the OOD gap is closeable with a little in-domain data: fine-tuning the BERT-Large baseline individually on each Split II dev set closes the gap to the best submitted result on all six datasets (Figure 1) — macro-average F1 rises from 57.4 to 62, a 4.6-point gain — but only fully catches up on one of the six, RelationExtraction (90.6 vs. 89.0), where the organizers suggest the dataset's narrow relation vocabulary makes a little in-domain data go unusually far. On the other five (BioASQ, DROP, DuoRC, RACE, TextbookQA), fine-tuning narrows the gap but the best submission still leads.

// does it hold up?

The eval read

As a shared task rather than a model paper, MRQA 2019's rigor is largely about the fairness of its protocol, and on that count it does real work: the fixed six-dataset training pool — the rules "disallowed the use of any other question-answering data for training" — is an unusually clean control on what every team could use, and the footnote-3 leakage safeguard (drawing eval examples from each source dataset's own original test split before touching train/dev) is exactly the right instinct for a task built by remixing 18 pre-existing datasets that plenty of pretrained LMs may already have seen in some form.

But the paper's own numbers cut against its stated goal of isolating "generalization" as a distinct capability. Section 6.1's finding — "there is a very strong correlation between in-domain and out-of-domain performance" — that the same models that lead on the Split I training domains also lead on the twelve held-out ones means the leaderboard mostly reproduces a ranking of overall model strength rather than surfacing a distinct skill of domain-invariant generalization. The organizers' own Section 6.2 analysis reinforces this: swapping BERT-Large for XLNet is credited with "a significant gain in overall in- and out-of-domain performance" on its own, and D-Net's win specifically comes from ensembling two different pretrained LMs. Of the submissions that pursued a generalization-specific mechanism — adversarial domain-invariance training, multi-task learning with NLI — the gains the paper itself reports are modest (+0.4 to +1.9 F1) and, per the organizers' own account, Adv. Train "helped on most of the datasets (9 out of 12), but also hurt performance on some of them." The paper is transparent about all of this; the critique is that the shared task's premise (measuring generalization) and its actual leaderboard driver (base-model quality plus ensembling) are not the same thing, and the paper doesn't reconcile them.

The three-way split (train / dev-shown / fully-hidden) also has an unexplained arbitrariness: Section 3.1 states the definitions of Splits I/II/III (Table 1 only tabulates them) but nowhere argues why, say, BioASQ and RACE were assigned dev-shown status while ComplexWebQ and QAMR were made fully hidden. Since Section 6.1 shows scores are highly correlated across splits anyway, the II/III distinction may matter less than the paper's careful three-tier design implies — a domain-shift "lottery" in the sense that which datasets end up in which tier isn't argued for on domain-shift-severity grounds, just asserted.

Reformatting 18 heterogeneous datasets into one extractive schema also throws information away by explicit design choice, not oversight: the paper states it discards HotpotQA's distractor paragraphs, drops "which of the following" and other options-referencing questions from converted multiple-choice sets, and restricts DROP to its extractive-answer subset only (numeric/discrete answers are excluded). Each of these is defensible in isolation and stated plainly in Section 3, but together they mean several of the 18 "sub-domains" are narrower slices of their original datasets than the dataset names suggest — a RACE or DROP score here is not directly comparable to a leaderboard score on the original benchmark.

baselines

Two organizer baselines only (BERT-Large, BERT-Base) — both are fine-tuned span-extraction models, no non-BERT or generative baseline. Every participant improvement is measured against this single family, which the paper's own analysis shows the top submissions mostly beat by upgrading the same family (to XLNet/ERNIE) rather than by a fundamentally different approach.

contamination

The task takes explicit steps against MRQA-internal leakage (splitting each source dataset's own original test data first, before train/dev, per footnote 3) and constrains training data to the six provided datasets only. It does not and cannot control for whether a team's underlying pretrained LM (BERT/XLNet/ERNIE) saw held-out passages during its own pretraining corpus — a 2019-era concern the paper doesn't address, since general-purpose LM pretraining corpora are outside the shared task's controllable surface.

what's missed

Macro-averaging F1 equally across 12 datasets of very different native difficulty (RelationExtraction ranges 81.3-89.0 F1 across all eleven systems; RACE ranges 37.4-53.9) means a system's rank is driven disproportionately by the hardest domains, which the paper doesn't flag. The task also explicitly excludes unanswerable, multi-turn, and open-domain question types (footnote 2) — real limits on what "generalization" is being measured here.

would raise confidence

A stated rationale for the Split II vs. Split III assignment; an ablation isolating "same base LM, technique on vs. off" for at least one generalization-specific method (the paper reports Adv. Train's and CLER's gains over their own baselines, but not a controlled apples-to-apples comparison across teams using an identical base LM); and per-dataset variance across random seeds, which none of Tables 3-7 report.

// bottom line

This is a well-run, well-documented shared task with a genuinely useful artifact (18 datasets in one schema, a clean train/dev/hidden protocol) — but its own results show that the leaderboard it produced is closer to "which team had the best pretrained LM and ensembled it" than to "which team learned to generalize." Read the 72.5-vs-61.8 headline as evidence that bigger/better LMs transfer better, not as evidence that any of the shared task's target techniques (adversarial training, multi-task learning) cracked domain generalization.

// what to remember

What to remember

  1. MRQA 2019 unified 18 existing QA datasets (SQuAD, TriviaQA, DROP, HotpotQA, RACE, TextbookQA, and more) into one extractive schema, split into 6 training-only, 6 out-of-domain-with-dev, and 6 fully-hidden domains — a shared measurement of transfer, not a new dataset with its own leaderboard.
  2. Ten teams submitted; the winner, D-Net (Baidu), scored 72.5 macro-F1 on the 12 held-out domains, 10.7 points above the organizers' BERT-Large baseline (61.8) — but D-Net's own description credits an XLNet+ERNIE 2.0 ensemble, not a generalization-specific mechanism.
  3. The organizers' own analysis found in-domain and out-of-domain scores strongly correlated, and credited most of the leaderboard's spread to base pretrained-LM choice — a caution against reading this shared task as proof that any particular generalization technique (adversarial training, multi-task learning) works, since none of those showed gains anywhere close to simply switching LMs.
