OpenEQA: Embodied Question Answering in the Era of Foundation Models

A 1636-question, open-vocabulary embodied QA benchmark scored by an LLM judge instead of a fixed answer list — and the best model tested, GPT-4V at 49.6%, still trails the 86.8% human ceiling by nearly forty points.

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

Embodied Question Answering — an agent watches or explores an environment, then answers a natural-language question about it — is an old idea (Das et al.'s original EQA, MP3D-EQA, MT-EQA, IQA all predate this paper by several years), but the authors argue the prior EQA line never grew up to match what foundation models can now do. Every one of those earlier benchmarks, per the paper's own Table 1 comparison, is closed-vocabulary: the agent picks from a fixed answer set rather than producing free text, which sidesteps the hardest part of the problem — deciding whether two different phrasings of an answer both count as correct. Several also restrict inputs to a single modality or a single frame, and none combines real (not procedurally generated) 3D scans with both a passive, memory-based setting and an active, exploration-based one.

The authors frame two concrete, separate use cases that motivate the reformulation. Episodic-memory EQA (EM-EQA) models a smart-glasses-style assistant that must answer from a history of past observations it already has — it cannot go look for more information. Active EQA (A-EQA) models a mobile robot that has no relevant memory yet and must physically explore a scanned environment to gather the observations it needs before answering. These are framed as two different tasks under a common problem statement, not two scores on the same task: EM-EQA agents receive an episode history H as input; A-EQA agents receive a simulator state S and must generate their own H through exploration.

The second, arguably harder gap the paper takes on is evaluation itself. Once you allow open-vocabulary answers, exact-match scoring stops working — "two microwaves" and "a pair of microwaves" should both score as correct, but naive string matching would fail both interchangeably. Prior open-ended QA and captioning work leaned on human evaluation, which the paper calls "prohibitively slow and/or expensive" for benchmarking at scale. OpenEQA's proposed fix — an LLM as an automatic judge — is itself the paper's second headline contribution, not a footnote to the dataset.

// how it works

The method

OpenEQA draws its 1636 question-answer pairs from two existing 3D-scan sources: 1079 questions sourced from ScanNet video walkthroughs (90 validation scenes plus 10 held-out test scenes) and 557 questions sourced from HM3D scans rendered through the Habitat simulator (87 validation scenes). Human annotators watched an episode history H — effectively a video tour of a home or office — and, playing the role of an end user, wrote two question-answer pairs per category after viewing it. That process surfaced seven question categories the paper settled on as covering the range of things a real assistant gets asked: object recognition, attribute recognition, object state recognition, object localization, spatial reasoning, functional reasoning, and world knowledge. Every question-answer pair then went through independent validation by two more annotators, who flagged and discarded anything unanswerable, ambiguous, or factually wrong. Because object-localization questions in particular can have several equally correct answers ("to the left of the fridge" vs. "to the right of the stove"), the paper collected 4 additional answers from 2 more annotators for that category specifically, giving 5 reference answers per localization question rather than 1.

The HM3D-sourced questions do double duty: because the episode history H for EM-EQA was itself generated by placing a scripted exploration policy at a fixed starting state S, that same (Q, S, A*) triple can be reused for A-EQA — the existence of a feasible exploration trajectory is the paper's own proof that the question is answerable from that starting point. ScanNet questions, by contrast, only support EM-EQA, since ScanNet supplies fixed video walkthroughs rather than an interactive simulator state. That is why A-EQA's question count (557) exactly equals the HM3D share of EM-EQA's question count.

To score open-vocabulary answers automatically, the paper introduces LLM-Match, adapting the answer-scoring protocol from MMBench to EQA. Given a question, the human-annotated ground-truth answer, and a model's generated answer, an LLM is prompted to output a similarity score σ on a 1-to-5 scale (1 = incorrect, 5 = fully correct, with 2–4 covering partial similarity). The aggregate LLM-Match score across N questions is:

C = 1N Σᵢ σᵢ − 14 × 100% // rescales the 1–5 judge score onto a 0–100% correctness axis

For A-EQA specifically, correctness alone doesn't penalize an agent that wanders aimlessly before stumbling on the answer, so the paper adds an efficiency metric modeled on SPL (Success weighted by Path Length), a standard navigation metric. It weights the same (σᵢ − 1)/4 correctness term by the ratio of the ground-truth exploration path length lᵢ to the max of the agent's own path length pᵢ and lᵢ:

E = 1N Σᵢ σᵢ − 14 × lᵢmax(pᵢ, lᵢ) × 100% // the paper notes E > 100% is theoretically possible, since ground-truth paths aren't guaranteed optimal

Four families of EQA agents are benchmarked, none fine-tuned for the task. Blind LLMs (GPT-4 or LLaMA-2) answer from the question alone, with no visual input, establishing how much can be guessed from prior world knowledge. Socratic LLMs w/ Frame Captions caption K sampled frames (K=50 for EM-EQA, K=75 for A-EQA) with LLaVA-1.5 and feed the captions plus the question to an LLM. Socratic LLMs w/ Scene-Graph Captions instead build an object-centric 3D scene graph — either ConceptGraphs or the paper's own Sparse Voxel Map variant — describing objects, their attributes, and 3D locations, and feed that structured text to the LLM. Multi-Frame VLMs, represented by GPT-4V, process up to 50 frames directly rather than going through a captioning intermediary. A separate Human Agent baseline has annotators watch the same episode video and answer the same questions, establishing a ceiling. Because non-blind agents sometimes abstained outright (up to 55% of the time for GPT-4-based Socratic agents, per the analysis section), the paper applies a "force-a-guess" protocol: an abstaining agent's answer is replaced by its underlying blind LLM's guess, since the scoring metric treats abstention as simply incorrect. All headline numbers in the main paper use this correction.

// what they found

The results

On the combined ("ALL" = ScanNet + HM3D) EM-EQA LLM-Match score, the full ranking from weakest to strongest is: blind LLaMA-2 (28.3%), LLaMA-2 with ConceptGraphs (28.7%), blind GPT-4 (33.5%), LLaMA-2 with the Sparse Voxel Map (34.3%), GPT-4 with ConceptGraphs (36.5%), LLaMA-2 with frame captions (36.8%), GPT-4 with the Sparse Voxel Map (38.9%), GPT-4 with frame captions (43.6%), and GPT-4V processing frames directly (49.6%, on a 500-question subset due to API limits). The Human Agent baseline — a genuinely separate population, not a model — scores 86.8% on that same ALL/EM-EQA column, roughly 37 points above the best model tested. That ceiling rests on a very small sample, though: §3 says the authors "enrolled two independent participants for this benchmarking exercise and found strong agreement in responses" — so half of the headline gap is anchored by an N of two.

A striking result the paper calls out explicitly (its own observation 3): blind LLMs are "surprisingly strong baselines," with blind GPT-4 scoring 33.5% and blind LLaMA-2 scoring 28.3% on the combined EM-EQA score — substantially below GPT-4V or human performance, but non-trivial for models given no visual input at all. The paper reads this as evidence that a large share of OpenEQA questions can be partially answered from prior world knowledge or plausible guessing alone, without any specific information about the actual scene — a pattern it notes echoes what early VQA work also found with blind baselines. The comparison to perceptual agents isn't uniform, though: within each LLM family, every perceptual variant does outscore its own blind counterpart (the paper's own observation 5), but across families blind GPT-4 (33.5%) actually edges out the weakest LLaMA-2 perceptual agent, LLaMA-2 with ConceptGraphs (28.7%), while trailing every other perceptual row.

A-EQA scores (restricted to HM3D scenes, since ScanNet has no simulator state to explore) are generally lower than the matching EM-EQA agent's score, as the paper itself puts it — though not uniformly: the blind agents' A-EQA HM3D scores are identical to their EM-EQA HM3D scores (GPT-4 35.5% either way, LLaMA-2 29.0% either way), since a blind agent's answer doesn't depend on the episode history at all. Among perceptual agents, GPT-4 with frame captions drops from 43.6% (EM-EQA ALL) / 40.0% (EM-EQA HM3D-only) to 38.1% on A-EQA HM3D, while GPT-4 with ConceptGraphs is essentially unchanged (34.0% EM-EQA HM3D to 34.4% A-EQA HM3D) — the paper attributes the general A-EQA drop to the exhaustive frontier-exploration policy producing longer, noisier histories than the human-like trajectories EM-EQA agents see. On the A-EQA efficiency metric, scores range from 4.3±0.3 (LLaMA-2 w/ ConceptGraphs) up to 7.5±0.6 (GPT-4V), all well under the 100% a perfectly efficient, perfectly correct agent would need.

Breaking EM-EQA performance down by category (Figure 5), functional reasoning is the category models handle best — the paper reports an average LLM-Match score of 45.6 there — with object state recognition and world knowledge also comparatively strong, which the paper attributes to these categories rewarding commonsense knowledge that large models already have. Object localization and spatial reasoning are the hardest categories across the board; notably, the paper reports that scene-graph agents are "no better than frame-captioning agents, even on spatial reasoning questions" — the structured 3D representation that in principle should help with space and geometry does not show up as an advantage in these results.

On the LLM-Match metric's own validation: the authors sampled 300 questions, drew responses from blind LLaMA-2, GPT-4V, and human annotations, and had 4 human evaluators score all 300 in a double-blind study using a prompt similar to LLM-Match's own. The resulting Spearman correlation between the LLM-Match metric and human judgment was ρ = 0.909 (bootstrap CI 0.883–0.928, N=9999 bootstrap resamples) — a number about the judge's reliability, not about any model's task performance. For context, the paper reports that human evaluators correlated with each other at ρ between 0.91 and 0.93 on the same 300-question set, which is why the paper's own framing — that LLM-Match agrees with human judgment "nearly as much as human subjects do with one another" — is a same-study, apples-to-apples comparison.

// does it hold up?

The eval read

The strongest part of this paper's evaluation design is that it doesn't just assert its LLM judge works — it runs an actual human-agreement study and reports the comparison number a reader needs (human-human agreement) alongside the number it's arguing for (LLM-human agreement). ρ=0.909 vs. ρ∈[0.91,0.93] is a genuinely informative pair, and the paper is careful to scope the claim to that specific 300-question, 4-rater, double-blind setup rather than asserting universal judge reliability. It's worth noting, though, that the validation sample deliberately drew from only two model sources (blind LLaMA-2 and GPT-4V) plus human answers — it does not include responses from every agent family the paper later benchmarks (the scene-graph and frame-caption Socratic agents aren't part of the 300-question validation set), so the judge's demonstrated reliability is strictly established for the response *distributions* actually sampled, not guaranteed to generalize identically to every row in the results table.

The blind-LLM baseline is exactly the right control to run here, and the paper does run it (rather than merely gesturing at its absence): blind GPT-4 and blind LLaMA-2 are full baseline rows in Table 2, not an afterthought, and the paper explicitly draws the conclusion a careful reader would want — that a large share of OpenEQA's questions can be "guessed" from prior world knowledge without any scene-specific visual grounding, and that agents with perceptual access still don't clear the blind-LLM floor by nearly as much as one might hope in some categories. This is a genuine strength: many embodied/VQA benchmarks skip this control entirely.

Where the paper is thinner is on what the LLM-Match metric itself might be missing. The score compresses a 1–5 similarity judgment into a single aggregate percentage; it doesn't break out how often the judge disagrees with humans in a way correlated with answer length, verbosity, or hedging language — a model that produces a long, hedged, technically-correct answer and one that produces a short wrong guess could plausibly be scored very differently by an LLM judge than by a human, and the paper's ρ figures are aggregate correlations that wouldn't necessarily surface that kind of systematic bias. The paper is also explicit that it recommends GPT-4 specifically as the scoring LLM after finding GPT-3.5 and LLaMA-2 correlate much more weakly (ρ<0.7) — which means the benchmark's reported numbers for every future model are contingent on continued access to a scoring model whose behavior OpenAI can change without notice, a dependency the paper does not flag as a limitation of its own protocol.

On the GPT-4V comparison specifically: its 49.6% figure is computed on only 500 of OpenEQA's 1636 EM-EQA questions, explicitly because of API limitations at the time — a real and disclosed caveat, footnoted directly in Table 2, but one that's easy to lose when the number gets quoted (as the paper's own contributions section, Sec. 1.1, and conclusion do) alongside the 86.8% human score, which is computed on the full question set. Both are real, correctly-reported numbers, but they are not scored on the same population, and the paper's headline "GPT-4V at 49.6% vs. human at 86.8%" framing doesn't restate that population difference at the point where the comparison is made.

Contamination is a live question this paper doesn't really engage with. OpenEQA's images and video walkthroughs come from ScanNet (published 2017) and HM3D (published 2021) — both years-old, widely used, and downloadable 3D-scan datasets. GPT-4V and GPT-4 were trained on internet-scale image and text data well after both were public, so it is possible (though the paper does not investigate this) that some of the same rooms, floorplans, or even specific captioned frames from these datasets appear in the models' training data in some form. The blind-LLM result — that GPT-4 answers a meaningful fraction of scene-specific questions correctly with zero visual input — is consistent with strong world-knowledge priors, but it is also consistent with some degree of prior exposure to these exact environments; the paper's framing favors the former explanation without ruling out the latter.

baselines

Includes a genuine blind-LLM control (both GPT-4 and LLaMA-2) and a human ceiling — stronger baseline hygiene than most embodied-QA benchmarks at the time. No fine-tuned/task-specific baseline is attempted; all agents are zero-shot foundation-model compositions.

contamination

Not investigated. Source scans (ScanNet 2017, HM3D 2021) predate GPT-4/GPT-4V training cutoffs and are public datasets; the paper doesn't test whether blind-LLM performance reflects prior scene exposure vs. pure world-knowledge guessing.

what's missed

The 300-question judge-validation set draws only from blind LLaMA-2, GPT-4V, and human answers — not from every agent family later reported in Table 2. LLM-Match's sensitivity to answer verbosity/hedging (as opposed to prompt wording, which is tested in the appendix) isn't measured.

would raise confidence

A judge-agreement study sampled across all nine benchmarked agent rows (not just two); an explicit contamination check against ScanNet/HM3D scene identifiers; and GPT-4V results on the full 1636-question set rather than a 500-question subset, so its number sits on the same population as every other row.

// bottom line

OpenEQA earns its central claim — foundation models trail humans by a wide margin on open-vocabulary embodied QA — with an evaluation design that is unusually careful for the space: a real blind-baseline control, a real human-agreement study for its judge, and explicit, disclosed caveats (the 500-question GPT-4V subset, the force-a-guess correction) rather than buried ones. Its remaining soft spots are less about what it measured wrong and more about what it didn't check: contamination exposure for two well-known public 3D datasets, and judge reliability across the full roster of agents it reports on rather than a two-model sample.

// takeaways

What to remember

  1. OpenEQA reformulates Embodied QA as two distinct tasks — episodic-memory EQA (answer from a given observation history, e.g. smart glasses) and active EQA (explore a simulated scene first, e.g. a mobile robot) — over 1636 open-vocabulary, human-authored questions across seven categories, drawn from 1079 ScanNet and 557 HM3D questions.
  2. Its LLM-Match metric replaces exact-match scoring with an LLM judge (1–5 similarity, rescaled to 0–100%), validated at ρ=0.909 against human ratings on a 300-question, 4-rater double-blind study — a judge-reliability number, not a model-performance number, and one drawn from only a subset of the paper's later-benchmarked agents.
  3. The best model tested, GPT-4V processing frames directly, reaches 49.6% on a 500-question subset of EM-EQA, versus 86.8% for human annotators on the full set and 28.3–33.5% for blind (no-vision) LLMs — leaving a roughly 37-point human gap and a surprisingly strong blind-guessing floor that the paper attributes to world-knowledge priors rather than scene grounding.
