Benchmarking Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Medicine

MIRAGE puts 7,663 zero-shot medical questions from five QA datasets through 41 corpus × retriever × LLM configurations of a new toolkit, MedRAG, spending over 1.8 trillion prompt tokens to find out which RAG recipe actually helps in medicine — and to surface a log-linear snippet-scaling law and a "lost-in-the-middle" position effect along the way.

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

By early 2024, medical LLM evaluation had mostly measured raw model knowledge — MultiMedQA / Med-PaLM (Singhal et al., 2023a), GPT-4 on medical boards (Nori et al., 2023a) — without giving the model any external documents to work with. The handful of studies that did test retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) in medicine, such as Liévin et al. (2022) and Lála et al.'s PaperQA (2023), fed the retriever the candidate answer options along with the question, which is not how a clinician or patient actually poses a question: in the real world nobody hands the search engine a multiple-choice answer key. None of the prior evaluations combined all four properties the paper argues actually matter for a realistic medical RAG test: zero-shot learning (no in-context exemplars), multi-choice evaluation (for large-scale, automatic scoring), RAG itself, and question-only retrieval (QOR — no answer options visible to the retriever). Table 1 of the paper lays this out directly: Nori et al. (2023a) and Singhal et al. (2023a) test ZSL+MCE with no RAG at all; Liévin et al. (2022) and Lála et al. (2023) add RAG but not QOR. MIRAGE is, in the authors' words, the first benchmark to adopt all four settings simultaneously.

Beyond the evaluation protocol, there was also no controlled way to compare the actual components of a medical RAG pipeline — which corpus, which retriever, which backbone LLM — against each other on equal footing. Prior medical-RAG systems (Almanac, PaperQA, BioReader) each bundled their own idiosyncratic choice of corpus and retriever, making it impossible to tell whether a reported gain came from the retriever, the corpus, or the LLM. That is the second gap MIRAGE + MedRAG is built to close.

// how it works

The method

MIRAGE is a fixed, 7,663-question test set assembled from five existing multi-choice medical QA datasets, split into two families: three medical examination sets — MMLU-Med (1,089 questions, a 6-subject biomedical subset of MMLU following Singhal et al.'s selection), MedQA-US (1,273 four-option USMLE-style questions), and MedMCQA (4,183 questions, the dev split, since MedMCQA's test set has no released labels) — and two biomedical literature sets — PubMedQA* (500 yes/no/maybe questions, built by stripping the supporting PubMed abstract context out of PubMedQA's expert-annotated test split) and BioASQ-Y/N (618 yes/no questions, the most recent five years, 2019–2023, of BioASQ Task B). Every ground-truth supporting document is deliberately removed from the input; only the bare question (and, at answer time, its options) is given to the system, and retrieval must work from the question text alone.

To actually run RAG systems against MIRAGE, the paper also builds MedRAG, a toolkit with three swappable component types. Corpora (five): PubMed (23.9M documents / 23.9M snippets, biomedical abstracts), StatPearls (9.3k documents / 301.2k snippets, point-of-care clinical decision support — the paper's first NLP evaluation of this source), Textbooks (18 USMLE-reference textbooks / 125.8k snippets), Wikipedia (6.5M documents / 29.9M snippets, general knowledge), and MedCorp, a simple union of all four (30.4M documents / 54.2M snippets). Retrievers (four, plus two fusions): BM25 (lexical), Contriever (general-domain dense), SPECTER (scientific-domain dense), and MedCPT (biomedical dense, trained on PubMed search-log clicks) — plus RRF-2 (Reciprocal Rank Fusion of BM25 + MedCPT) and RRF-4 (fusion of all four). LLMs (six): GPT-4-32k-0613 and GPT-3.5-turbo-16k-0613 (commercial), Mixtral-8×7B and Llama2-70B (open, general), and MEDITRON-70B and PMC-LLaMA-13B (open, biomedical fine-tunes). Every LLM answers with chain-of-thought prompting at temperature 0, with 32 retrieved snippets prepended to the question by default; CoT prompting with no retrieved context at all is the "no-RAG" baseline throughout.

Running the full space of corpus × retriever × LLM combinations plus the ablations reported in the paper adds up to 41 distinct MedRAG configurations, consuming over 1.8 trillion prompt tokens in total — the paper's basis for calling this the first systematic, multi-dimensional evaluation of medical RAG design choices rather than a single end-to-end system.

// what they found

The results

Holding the corpus fixed at MedCorp and the retriever fixed at RRF-4 (the paper's default MedRAG setting), Table 6 compares chain-of-thought (CoT) with MedRAG across all six backbone LLMs. GPT-4 leads under CoT alone (73.44% average) and improves further to 79.97% with MedRAG. The open/weaker models gain the most in relative terms: GPT-3.5 rises from 60.69% (CoT) to 71.57% (MedRAG), a 17.9% relative gain — the largest of any model — and Mixtral rises from 61.42% to 69.48% (+13.1% relative). That pushes both GPT-3.5 and Mixtral's MedRAG average close to (though still below) GPT-4's own CoT-only score of 73.44%, which is the basis for the paper's claim that MedRAG elevates GPT-3.5 and Mixtral "to GPT-4-level" performance. Llama2-70B gains the least from RAG (50.24% → 53.38%, +6.3%), while its medically fine-tuned derivative MEDITRON-70B scores higher under both CoT (57.04%, +13.5% over Llama2) and MedRAG (60.18%, +12.7% over Llama2 MedRAG) — the paper reads this gap as domain fine-tuning (SFT) currently mattering more than RAG for this base model. On the two literature tasks (PubMedQA*, BioASQ-Y/N), where PubMed genuinely contains the answer, MedRAG boosts Llama2 by 19.4% and 20.9% respectively, enough to match or beat MEDITRON's CoT score with no fine-tuning at all — but on the three examination datasets, which were "carefully designed to differentiate between medical students," MedRAG does not consistently beat SFT.

Varying the corpus and retriever (Table 7, GPT-3.5 as the backbone, since it benefits most from MedRAG) shows sharp task-dependence: MedRAG with Textbooks + RRF-2 hits the highest MMLU-Med score (76.68%), and StatPearls + Contriever is best on MedQA-US (67.48%) — but both corpora barely move PubMedQA* and BioASQ-Y/N, which draw almost entirely on PubMed. PubMed is the only single corpus that improves every MIRAGE task over the no-RAG baseline; MedCorp (the union of all four) is close behind and more robust across tasks. Among individual retrievers, MedCPT (the biomedical-domain retriever) and BM25 are the most consistently strong; SPECTER, trained to regularize pairwise article distance rather than query-to-article relevance, is the weakest on MedCorp (-7.8% to -6.8% versus other retrievers). RRF-4 on MedCorp gives GPT-3.5's best configuration overall, 71.57% average — matching the Table 6 headline number.

Two further discoveries generalize beyond MIRAGE itself. First, a log-linear scaling relationship: accuracy on MMLU-Med, MedQA-US, and MedMCQA rises roughly log-linearly with the number of retrieved snippets k for k ≤ 32, but too few snippets (k ≤ 8) can actively hurt by crowding out the model's own knowledge, and accuracy can also fall again once k grows large enough to dilute the signal-to-noise ratio. PubMedQA* is the exception — it peaks at k = 1, consistent with 79.6% of its ground-truth snippets being ranked first by the retriever. Second, a "lost-in-the-middle" position effect (echoing Liu et al., 2023): on the two tasks with labeled ground-truth snippets (PubMedQA*, BioASQ-Y/N), accuracy follows a U-shaped curve — highest when the correct snippet sits at the start or end of the retrieved context, lowest when it's buried in the middle.

// does it hold up?

The eval read

MIRAGE's real contribution is the ablation design, not a leaderboard — and read that way, it holds up well. But its two most quotable claims deserve scrutiny before they're repeated as facts. The "MedRAG elevates GPT-3.5 and Mixtral to GPT-4-level performance" framing compares GPT-3.5-with-retrieval (71.57%) and Mixtral-with-retrieval (69.48%) against GPT-4's own CoT-only, no-retrieval score (73.44%) — not against GPT-4 with the same retrieval pipeline, which reaches 79.97% and remains well ahead of both. The comparison is real but it is same-model-different-configuration dressed up as a cross-model result; GPT-4 + MedRAG is still the best system on the benchmark by a wide margin.

The benchmark's five component datasets (MMLU, MedQA/USMLE, MedMCQA, PubMedQA, BioASQ) are all public, years-old, and heavily mirrored online — exactly the kind of text that ends up inside GPT-4/GPT-3.5/Mixtral-era pretraining corpora. The paper never checks for contamination (no canary strings, no held-out-date split, no comparison against post-cutoff exam questions), so some fraction of the strong CoT-only baseline scores — especially GPT-4's 89.44% on MMLU-Med and 83.97% on MedQA-US — may already reflect memorized test content rather than in-context reasoning, which would also make the RAG "lift" numbers an underestimate of what retrieval adds to a genuinely unseen question.

Because every MIRAGE task is scored as multiple-choice or yes/no/maybe, the benchmark can only tell you whether the model's final letter is right, not whether the underlying chain-of-thought actually used the retrieved snippet correctly, faithfully, or at all — the authors themselves note in the Limitations section that "the rationales generated by MEDRAG remain to be evaluated." And the retrieval-quality analyses that most directly test whether RAG is doing real work — the top-1 hit-rate statistic and the lost-in-the-middle position study — are only possible on PubMedQA* and BioASQ-Y/N, the two tasks with labeled supporting snippets; the three harder examination tasks, which is where MedRAG's gains are least consistent, are exactly the tasks this analysis cannot reach.

baselines

CoT-only (no retrieval, same model) is the sole baseline — a fair ablation control, but not an external comparison against other published medical-RAG systems (Almanac, PaperQA) or against few-shot/self-consistency prompting, so "GPT-4-level" is really "beats this model's own no-RAG score," not "matches best-known medical QA performance."

contamination

All five source datasets are old, public multi-choice sets with no contamination check (no canary strings, no post-cutoff split) — plausible memorization inflates the CoT baseline and likely understates RAG's true marginal value.

what's missed

Multi-choice/yes-no scoring only — no evaluation of whether the CoT rationale actually reflects the retrieved snippet; retrieval-quality diagnostics (hit-rate, position effect) only run on 2 of 5 tasks, missing the 3 examination tasks where MedRAG helps least.

would raise confidence

A contamination probe (held-out or post-cutoff questions), human or attribution-based grading of whether the generated rationale is actually grounded in the retrieved snippet, and testing with post-2023 LLMs to see if the corpus/retriever conclusions still hold as backbone models change.

// bottom line

MIRAGE is a genuinely useful, carefully controlled stress test of the medical-RAG design space, and its log-linear scaling and lost-in-the-middle findings look like real, generalizable properties rather than benchmark artifacts. But its headline "GPT-4-level" framing is a same-model comparison, not a cross-model one, and because scoring stops at the multiple-choice answer, it can't distinguish genuine grounded reasoning from a model landing on the right letter for the wrong (or memorized) reason.

// takeaways

What to remember

  1. MIRAGE combines 7,663 zero-shot, question-only-retrieval questions from five medical QA datasets (MMLU-Med, MedQA-US, MedMCQA, PubMedQA*, BioASQ-Y/N) — the first benchmark to jointly require zero-shot learning, multi-choice evaluation, RAG, and question-only retrieval.
  2. MedRAG (5 corpora × 4 retrievers × 6 LLMs, 41 configurations, 1.8T+ prompt tokens) shows RAG lifts accuracy by up to 17.9% relative (GPT-3.5) over CoT, that PubMed and combined-corpus MedCorp are the only corpora that help on every task, and that MedCPT/BM25 are the strongest individual retrievers.
  3. Two general patterns emerge beyond the leaderboard: accuracy scales roughly log-linearly with retrieved-snippet count up to k≈32, and follows a "lost-in-the-middle" U-shape based on where the correct snippet sits in context — but because scoring is multiple-choice only, MIRAGE can't confirm the model's reasoning actually used that snippet correctly.
