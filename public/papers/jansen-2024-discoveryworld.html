DiscoveryWorld: A Virtual Environment for Developing and Evaluating Automated Scientific Discovery Agents

A 120-task virtual laboratory spanning eight scientific domains and three difficulty tiers, scored on three separate axes — task completion, procedural correctness, and discovered explanatory knowledge — built to show that zero-shot GPT-4o agents still trail the human scientists who attempt the same tasks by tens of points.

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

By 2024 a handful of narrow AI systems had produced genuine scientific results — AlphaFold and RoseTTAFold in protein structure, program-search systems finding new mathematical constructions, models proposing new materials. But as the authors point out, these systems deliberately bypass the messy parts of discovery: they search a pre-defined hypothesis space toward a pre-defined goal, rather than forming their own hypotheses, designing experiments to test them, and deciding what to do with the results. A second family of systems (CoScientist, ChemCrow, robot-scientist projects in genetics) does perform the fuller loop, but does so in expensive, slow, real-world lab settings that are hard to scale or reproduce as a benchmark.

Text- and vision-based simulated environments were the obvious place to look for a cheaper substitute, but the existing ones test the wrong skills. ALFRED, ALFWorld, and MiniGrid are pick-and-place navigation tasks with a single correct plan already implied by the instruction. NetHack and MineDojo reward exploration in worlds where "good scientific choices" aren't specifically incentivized — they're built for open-ended play, not hypothesis testing. The closest prior work, ScienceWorld (also co-authored by Jansen and Côté), asks an agent to execute elementary-school science procedures like "convert a liquid into a solid" — commonsense object manipulation, not ideation or systematic search over an unknown relationship. None of these require an agent to notice an anomaly, propose an explanation for it, design a measurement to distinguish between competing explanations, and then act correctly on whichever explanation survives — the actual shape of a discovery task. DiscoveryWorld's gap, then, is a benchmark cheap enough to run at scale, realistic enough that background scientific and commonsense knowledge actually transfers, but demanding enough that solving it requires the full ideation → experimentation → analysis → conclusion cycle.

// how it works

The method

DiscoveryWorld is a custom 2D simulation engine (~20K lines of Python, built on Pygame) presenting a 32×32-tile grid world. It exposes an OpenAI-Gym-style API: at each turn the agent receives an observation and chooses one of 14 possible actions (take, drop, move, open/close, activate/deactivate, use-one-object-on-another, and others), each taking zero, one, or two arguments. Observations can be text (a JSON description of nearby objects, inventory, dialog options, and the task description), a 768×512px visual screenshot centered on the agent, or both. Agents additionally get two "handicap" teleport actions — to a named task-relevant location, or directly beside any previously-observed object — since navigation itself isn't the skill being tested.

Content is organized into 8 discovery themes — Proteomics (spot an outlier species by clustering protein-concentration data), Chemistry (find a rust-removal mixture via hill-climbing over concentrations), Archaeology (validate radioisotope dating by correlation, then date an artifact), Reactor Lab (regress a crystal property against resonance frequency, linear or quadratic), Plant Nutrients (infer a logical rule — XOR/AND/OR/NOT — governing which nutrient combinations plants prefer), Space Sick (diagnose and fix the cause of a colonist illness), Rocket Science (measure unknown planetary properties and apply physics formulas), and Translation (ground the meaning of words in an unknown language from observation). Each theme has 3 difficulty levels (Easy, Normal, Challenge — e.g. for Proteomics, clustering over a 2D vs. 3D protein-feature space at Normal vs. Challenge, while Easy instead simplifies to just 3 animals in a small, static lab rather than 5 moving ones), and each of the resulting 24 theme×difficulty templates is parametrically instantiated over 5 random seeds, giving the paper's official benchmark of 8 × 3 × 5 = 120 task instances. Easy tasks are capped at 100 steps; Normal and Challenge run to 1000 steps. Separately, 10 unit-test themes (multi-turn dialog, measuring with an instrument, pick-and-place, pick-and-give, reading the in-world "DiscoveryFeed" social feed, moving through doors, using keys, navigating to a room, searching for an object, interacting with a moving agent) isolate whether poor performance comes from basic environment competency or from the discovery process itself.

Scoring runs on three separate automatic metrics, illustrated by the paper's own example scorecard for a Normal-difficulty Reactor Lab task: (1) Task Completion — a single binary point ("was the task completed successfully?"); (2) Procedure — a checklist of task-relevant sub-actions (that example: 6 items worth a total of 25 points, e.g. "each scientific instrument has been used with at least one crystal" worth 5, "the resonance frequency of the unknown reactors is correct" worth 2), normalized to 0–1; and (3) Explanatory Knowledge — a small set of binary yes/no questions against a gold explanation (that example: 2 questions worth 1 point each, e.g. "does it clearly state the relationship is linear, with crystal frequency = (96 × densitometer reading) + 102"), also normalized to 0–1. Completion and Procedure are scored automatically by the simulator itself; Knowledge is scored by comparing the agent's stated hypotheses/notes against the gold questions — the paper's own baselines use GPT-4o as that judge (the scorer also supports human grading, used for the human baseline below). The 10 unit-test themes report only Procedure and Completion — they carry no explanatory-knowledge target.

Three zero-shot GPT-4o-backed baseline agents are evaluated, with no training or fine-tuning on the benchmark: ReAct (thought+action at each step, trimmed to the last ~40 steps of trajectory to fit context; discovered knowledge is judged from the concatenation of all its "thoughts"); Plan+Execute (an LLM plan, executed one step at a time by a ReAct sub-agent, replanned iteratively as new evidence comes in, to shrink each sub-trajectory and reduce distraction); and Hypothesizer (architecturally close to CLIN, maintaining an explicit working-memory of hypotheses and measurements that conditions each next action, which also gives the cleanest signal for judging discovered knowledge).

To establish a human reference point, the authors recruited 11 practicing human scientists via Upwork, each screened for (1) an MSc or PhD in a natural science, (2) self-reported comfort with statistics and spreadsheet-style software, and (3) prior comfort with 2D top-down games (verified with a screening tutorial task). Recruits' actual backgrounds spanned astrophysics, bioinformatics, biology, biomedical engineering, biostatistics, chemistry, computer engineering, data science, electrical engineering, geology, machine learning, mathematics, and physics. Because humans retain what they learn between tasks, they were only run on Normal and Challenge difficulty (Easy was skipped, to avoid Easy-task knowledge leaking an advantage into the harder levels) — all on the same fixed Seed 0 instance of each task, with up to a 1-hour time limit, and no retries counted toward the reported scores. Not every participant attempted every task (each task instance drew 9–11 of the 11 recruits). Humans' Procedure and Completion scores were graded automatically exactly as for agents, but their Knowledge score was graded manually on a 3-point scale (1 = all gold knowledge found, 0.5 = some, 0 = none) rather than the agents' automatic per-question binary grading — a methodological difference worth keeping in mind when reading the human/agent knowledge comparison below. Compensation ran $20–$30/hr, with total spending under $4,000 across all participants.

// what they found

The results

Averaged across all 8 themes at each difficulty level (Table 4), the strongest agent by Completion, ReAct, completed 38% of Easy tasks and 18% of Challenge tasks; the strongest agent by Knowledge, Hypothesizer, correctly discovered 34% of gold explanatory knowledge on Easy tasks, falling to 8% on Challenge. Notably, all three agents' Procedure scores stay much higher across every difficulty (0.49–0.64 on average) than their Completion or Knowledge scores — agents are reliably taking many of the right kinds of actions (using instruments, collecting samples) without reliably finishing the task or landing on the correct explanation. At Challenge difficulty specifically, all three baselines score exactly 0.00 Completion on three of the eight tasks — Reactor Lab (quadratic regression), Space Sick (novel instruments), and Rocket Science (measuring 5 variables). Space Sick/Challenge is the hardest instance by both outcome metrics: all three agents also score exactly 0.00 Knowledge on it, whereas the other two still yield a nonzero Knowledge score for at least one agent (ReAct 0.20 on Reactor Lab, Hypothesizer 0.03 on Rocket Science).

On the 10 unit-test competency checks (Table 5) — dialog, pick-and-place, instrument use, navigation, and the like, not discovery — the baselines look far more capable: average Completion is 0.60 (ReAct), 0.44 (Plan+Execute), and 0.64 (Hypothesizer). Two of the three agents cross into a 60%+ average completion range on these basic-competency tasks; Plan+Execute, whose iterative-replanning approach trims each sub-trajectory, lags at 44%. The gap between these numbers and the near-zero Discovery-task completion above is the paper's central point: the agents can execute the environment's mechanics, but largely fail at the ideation-and-experimentation loop discovery specifically demands.

The human baseline (Table 6) — recall, Normal and Challenge difficulty only, Seed 0, evaluated zero-shot with no retries — averaged 66% task completion and 55% knowledge-discovery accuracy across the 16 task instances attempted (a 79% average Procedure score). At those same two difficulty levels, the best any agent reaches is a 23% average Completion (Hypothesizer, Normal) and a 19% average Knowledge score (Hypothesizer, Normal) — both roughly a third of the human average, and at Challenge both sides fall — humans to a 56% average, the best agent to 18% (ReAct) — so the absolute gap actually narrows, from about 53 points to about 38, while the ratio stays roughly constant. Humans still complete above 60% on four of the eight individual Challenge tasks (e.g. 100% completion on Proteomics Challenge, 89% on Chemistry Challenge). Performance was far from uniform across humans, too: completion on individual task instances ranged from several Challenge-difficulty tasks solved by only a single one of the 9–11 recruits (e.g. Space Sick Challenge and Rocket Science Challenge both average just 0.11 human Completion) to others solved by essentially everyone (Proteomics Challenge, Archaeology Normal: 1.00).

// does it hold up?

The eval read

The three-metric design (Completion / Procedure / Knowledge) is the paper's strongest methodological contribution — it's exactly what stops a single pass/fail number from hiding the fact that agents are executing plausible-looking procedures without reaching correct conclusions, and the Procedure-vs-Completion-vs-Knowledge gap the paper surfaces is a genuinely useful diagnostic that a single completion metric would have erased. The unit-test / discovery-task split is similarly well-designed: it directly rules out "the agent just can't navigate this UI" as the explanation for poor discovery scores.

That said, several things are worth weighing before treating the 43-point gap between human task completion (66%) and the best matched-difficulty agent (23%, Hypothesizer at Normal) as a clean measure of "discovery capability" specifically. All three baselines share the same underlying model (GPT-4o) — the paper varies only the agent scaffold (ReAct / Plan+Execute / Hypothesizer), so it isolates scaffold design well but says nothing about whether a different or larger model closes the gap; no open-weight model, no other frontier lab's model, and no fine-tuned or RL-trained agent is evaluated in the main results, despite the paper explicitly noting the benchmark supports single-task, multi-task, and curriculum-learning settings that were left to future work. The Knowledge metric — the one metric that most directly measures "did it actually discover the right explanation" — is graded by GPT-4o itself for the agent runs, with no reported inter-rater check against the manual grading used for humans on the same metric; the two populations' Knowledge scores are graded by genuinely different procedures (automatic per-question binary vs. manual 3-point holistic), which the paper doesn't flag as a caveat when it reports the 55%-vs-lower comparison. The human sample is also small per task (9–11 people, one fixed seed each), recruited for general MSc/PhD natural-science background rather than domain-matched expertise per theme (the listed disciplines include several — computer engineering, data science, electrical engineering, machine learning — that sit outside "natural science" as usually construed), and every human played only Seed 0, so human variance across the other 4 parametric seeds each agent was scored on is simply unmeasured.

Finally, because DiscoveryWorld's task templates, gold solutions, and code are public on GitHub as of the June 2024 preprint, there's a live contamination consideration for anyone benchmarking a model trained or updated after that date — the paper doesn't discuss this risk, which matters more here than for many benchmarks because the "discovery" being scored is grounded in fixed, enumerable gold facts (e.g. the exact linear/quadratic formula relating crystal density to resonance frequency) rather than an open-ended judgment call.

baselines

Fair relative to each other (same GPT-4o backbone, only the agent scaffold varies) but narrow overall: one model family, no fine-tuned/RL agent, no cross-lab model comparison in the main results.

contamination

Not discussed in the paper. Task templates and gold solutions are public on GitHub from the June 2024 preprint onward, which is a real exposure risk for any model trained/updated afterward, given how enumerable the gold facts are.

what's missed

A reported check of GPT-4o's automatic Knowledge grading against the human graders' judgments on the same metric — the two populations are scored on that axis by different procedures (automatic binary vs. manual 3-point), which isn't flagged when the paper compares them.

would raise confidence

Inter-rater agreement between the GPT-4o knowledge grader and human graders; results for a second model family/scale; and a per-seed (rather than single-seed) human run, or an explicit accounting of why Seed 0 alone is representative.

// bottom line

The headline finding — zero-shot LLM agents built on today's scaffolds complete a small fraction of what human scientists complete on the same tasks, and discover even less of the underlying explanation — is well-supported by the paper's own numbers and is exactly the kind of gap a well-designed benchmark should surface. The specific size of that gap should be read as one model family's zero-shot ceiling on one seed of human data, not as a settled measurement of the human/AI discovery gap in general.

// takeaways

What to remember

  1. DiscoveryWorld is 120 official task instances (8 themes × 3 difficulty levels × 5 parametric seeds), plus 10 separate unit-test themes that isolate basic environment competency from discovery-specific skill — scored on three independent, normalized (0–1) metrics: Task Completion (binary), Procedure (a weighted checklist of task-relevant actions), and Explanatory Knowledge (gold-referenced questions, GPT-4o-graded in this paper's baselines).
  2. Zero-shot GPT-4o agents (ReAct, Plan+Execute, Hypothesizer) complete 38%/18% of Easy/Challenge Discovery tasks at best (ReAct) and discover 34% of gold knowledge on Easy tasks at best (Hypothesizer), collapsing to 10% or less at Challenge for every agent — while scoring 44–64% completion on the unit-test competency checks, showing the failure is concentrated in the discovery loop itself, not basic environment interaction.
  3. 11 Upwork-recruited human scientists (MSc/PhD, natural-science background), tested only at Normal/Challenge difficulty on a single fixed seed with no retries, averaged 66% completion and 55% knowledge accuracy — several times the best matched-difficulty agent average, though the human and agent Knowledge scores are graded by different procedures (manual 3-point vs. automatic per-question), a caveat the paper doesn't flag when making that comparison.
