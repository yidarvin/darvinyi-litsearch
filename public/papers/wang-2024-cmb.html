CMB: A Comprehensive Medical Benchmark in Chinese

A two-part Chinese medical benchmark — 11,200 licensing-exam questions spanning physicians, nurses, technicians and pharmacists, plus 208 multi-turn diagnostic questions built from 74 real clinical cases — designed because translating a Western medical benchmark into Chinese loses both China's own licensing structure and Traditional Chinese Medicine.

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

By 2023 there were already several Chinese medical multiple-choice datasets — MLEC-QA (21,700 questions from the Chinese National Licensed Pharmacist Examination), CMExam (68,119 tagged questions from the same exam), and ExplainCPE (a free-text-explanation benchmark scoped to pharmacists) — but the authors argue each is narrow along one of two axes. First, occupational coverage: pharmacist-only or physician-only question banks say nothing about nurses or medical technicians, even though, as the paper puts it, "the potential assistance provided by LLMs in medical professions is not confined to just pharmacy." Second, and more fundamental, is cultural localization: "medical environments in different regions have their local characteristics, e.g., the ubiquity and significance of traditional Chinese medicine within China," so "merely translating English-based medical evaluation may result in contextual incongruities to a local region." The paper is concrete about why TCM resists translation: a disease in TCM has two aspects, "bìng" (disease entity) and "zhèng" (pattern) — the same common cold might present as a "wind-cold" pattern in one patient and "wind-heat" in another — and diagnostic methods such as tongue and pulse examination (redness on the tip of the tongue suggesting heat in the Heart, on the sides suggesting heat in the Liver) have no Western-medicine analogue to translate into. China also has roughly 13,000 TCM compounds and over 100,000 recorded TCM recipes that a benchmark built by translating USMLE-style English datasets (e.g. MedQA) would never surface.

The paper also distinguishes two evaluation philosophies that prior work rarely combines: objective multiple-choice knowledge tests (clear ground truth, cheap to score at scale) and subjective free-text evaluation of clinical dialogue (closer to real deployment, but expensive and hard to judge — existing subjective LLM evaluation, they note, citing Zheng et al. 2023's MT-Bench/Chatbot Arena line of work, typically uses no reference answer at all, which the authors argue is unsuited to a domain "much more professional than that of the general domain"). Few datasets combine both: the paper names PromptCBLUE, MultiMedQA (English) and CMExam as the closest prior attempts, but argues none pairs occupation-spanning multiple-choice knowledge testing with a reference-anchored, multi-turn clinical-case component the way CMB does.

// how it works

The method

CMB has two independently-constructed parts that test different things. CMB-Exam is a multiple-choice knowledge test built from real Chinese qualification-exam questions (mostly sourced from the tutoring site Medtiku, "from which we obtain explicit permission to share the data"), spanning six categories — Physician, Nurse, Technician, Pharmacist, Undergraduate Disciplines, and Graduate Entrance Exam — subdivided into 28 subcategories and 176 subjects (Table 1's own "# Subject" column, matched row-for-row by the Appendix B directory it points to — 81+53+42 subject rows summing to the exact question counts in Table 1). The body text separately states "a total of 174 categories" for what it calls the same subject-level division; the paper never explains the 174-vs-176 gap, and this page doesn't invent an explanation for it either. After de-duplication and manual OCR correction, this yields 280,839 raw questions; the paper carves out 400 questions per subcategory as a 11,200-question test set and 10 per subcategory (280 total, with expert-written chain-of-thought explanations) as a dev set for few-shot prompting, leaving 269,359 questions as a public CMB-Exam-Train split. Each question has four to six options, with one or more correct answers, scored by exact string match after regex-based answer extraction from model output.

CMB-Clin is a different instrument entirely: 74 expert-curated, complex real clinical cases (drawn from medical textbooks, screened to exclude cases needing image data such as CT/MRI, and to avoid near-duplicate diseases), turned into 208 multi-turn diagnostic questions. Each case has three parts — a Description D (history, physical exam, ancillary tests), a sequence of Questions Q, and expert Reference answers R — and the model is evaluated by simulating a multi-round dialogue: the input at round k concatenates the case description with all prior question/reference pairs and the current question, so the model must track the conversation as a clinician-style consult, not answer each question in isolation. Internal Medicine (15.57%) and Surgery (14.87%) are the largest represented departments, and by consultation stage, the questions concentrate in treatment-principles (30.09%) and diagnosis (18.44%).

On the evaluation side, CMB-Exam models are run in four conditions crossed 2×2: answer-only vs. chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting, and zero-shot vs. three-shot. Decoding is greedy for every model (to reduce variance), with min_new_tokens=1 and max_new_tokens=512. GPT-4 is evaluated as the gpt-4 API version and ChatGPT as gpt-3.5-turbo-16k-0613; all experiments ran in August 2023. Per Appendix C, the evaluation spans 30 systems in three groups: 12 Chinese medical LLMs (HuatuoGPT-II at three sizes, DISC-MedLLM, IvyGPT, Sunsimiao, MedicalGPT, ChatMed-Consult, ChatGLM-Med, Bentsao, BianQue-2, DoctorGLM), 5 proprietary/closed systems (GPT-4, ChatGPT, and three Chinese commercial medical models — JianPeiGPT, ShuKunGPT, AntGLM-Med), and 13 open general-domain LLMs (Qwen at 72B/7B/1.8B, Yi at 34B/6B, Deepseek-llm-67B, Baichuan2 at 13B/7B, ChatGLM3-6B, Internlm-Chat at 20B/7B, Mixtral-8x7B, and Mistral-7B-Instruct). DISC-MedLLM is additionally evaluated after being fine-tuned directly on CMB-Exam-Train.

CMB-Clin can't be scored by exact match, so the authors use GPT-4 as an automatic judge: given the case description, dialogue history, current question, model response and reference answer, GPT-4 scores the response on four dimensions — Fluency, Relevance, Completeness, and (medical) Proficiency — on a 1–5 scale, following the same rubric given to human raters. To validate this, three medical-background annotators independently scored a randomly sampled subset of 320 responses across 11 models using the identical four-dimension rubric, and the paper reports a Spearman rank correlation of 0.93 between the GPT-4-judged and expert-judged model rankings, and per-aspect Pearson correlations of 0.71 (Fluency), 0.81 (Relevance), 0.78 (Completeness) and 0.75 (Proficiency) between the two scoring systems. They additionally re-ran the GPT-4 judge three independent times on both the general- and medical-model sets (Jan 20–26, 2024) to report the mean ± standard deviation of the automatic scores, and separately checked how stable model rankings are across five decoding temperatures (0, 0.2, 0.6, 1.0, 1.5), finding pairwise Spearman correlations between temperature settings from 0.87 up to a perfect 1.00.

// what they found

The results

On CMB-Exam (answer-only accuracy, zero-shot), several open Chinese general-purpose models outscore GPT-4: Qwen-72B-Chat leads at 74.38%, followed by Yi-34B-Chat (69.17%) and Yi-6B-Chat (65.87%) — all above GPT-4's own answer-only average of 59.46% and above ChatGPT's 38.31%. The paper reads this as evidence that "domestic general models have completed catching up with OpenAI in medical knowledge in chinese," while separately flagging that Yi-6B's strong showing "with a smaller number of parameters is impressive, but it also reminds us of the possibility of data leakage" — a caveat the paper raises but does not test for on any model. Among medical-specialized systems, HuatuoGPT-II-34B tops the whole medical-model table at 76.82%, ahead of every open medical LLM and every asterisked commercial system (JianPeiGPT 72.84%, ShuKunGPT 64.44%, AntGLM-Med 55.00% — the paper marks these three as "only the best score," with "the generation strategy... unknown"). Chain-of-thought and three-shot prompting help small/weak-Chinese models (Qwen-1.8B-Chat's three-shot average jumps from 27.43% to 40.69%; Deepseek-llm-67B-Chat, Mistral-7B and Mixtral-8x7B — models with "limited support for Chinese" — gain from few-shot) but actively hurts already-strong models: ChatGPT's CoT accuracy collapses from 38.31% to 18.23%. Accuracy also tracks career stage unevenly: nurse exams show the expected monotonic decrease in difficulty from advanced to entry-level roles, but for physicians the Advanced Professional Physician and Resident Physician subcategories score the two lowest of the five stages, and for medical technologists the ordering is "completely opposite to expectations," which the paper attributes to those advanced subcategories weighting personnel-management and communication questions rather than harder clinical content.

The CMB-Clin picture (GPT-4-as-judge, four-aspect average) reorders the field. Yi-34B ties GPT-4 at 4.67/5 average, and Qwen-72B (4.55) still edges ChatGPT (4.53), but every other domestic general model tested falls below ChatGPT — "domestic General LLMs still lag behind OpenAI in CMB-Clin, which is closer to real scenarios," and the paper explicitly recommends "using both CMB-Exam and CMB-Clin for model capability awareness" because a model can rank well on one and poorly on the other. The clearest instance is DISC-MedLLM: fine-tuning it on CMB-Exam-Train raises its own CMB-Exam accuracy (39.76% → 40.85%) but drops its CMB-Clin average from 3.58 to 3.04 — training on multiple-choice format does not transfer to, and here actively costs, multi-turn clinical dialogue quality. Conversely BianQue-2 sits near the bottom of CMB-Exam (3.92% average — barely above chance-adjacent noise) yet scores respectably on CMB-Clin (3.75 automatic, and 3.67 in the smaller expert-scored table), which the paper reads as evidence it "lacks the ability to do multiple-choice questions and follow instructions" rather than lacking medical knowledge outright. On the held-out, expert-scored 320-response/11-model subset (Table 11), rankings mostly track the GPT-4-judge rankings, with one notable exception the paper calls out directly in its Figure 3 caption: "the rankings agree with each other except for a flip for GPT-4 and ChatGPT" — expert raters score ChatGPT (4.53 average) marginally above GPT-4 (4.52), reversed from GPT-4-judge's own scoring of itself.

// does it hold up?

The eval read

CMB is unusually careful about validating its own judge: an 11-model, 320-response expert-scored subset, a Spearman rank correlation (0.93) and four separate Pearson correlations (0.71–0.81) against that subset, a 3-independent-run robustness check reporting mean ± std for every model, and a five-temperature ranking-stability check. That is real methodological effort most LLM-as-judge papers skip. But look at exactly what got validated: the 11 models in the expert-scored subset (ChatGPT, GPT-4, Baichuan-13B-chat, ChatGLM2-6B, HuatuoGPT, BianQue-2, ChatMed-Consult, MedicalGPT, DoctorGLM, Bentsao, ChatGLM-Med) exclude Qwen-72B, Yi-34B, Internlm, Deepseek-llm and Mixtral — precisely the models that top CMB-Exam and, on CMB-Clin's automatic scoring, tie or nearly tie GPT-4 (Yi-34B at 4.67, Qwen-72B at 4.55). The 0.93/0.71–0.81 correlation numbers are real, but they describe agreement on a weaker-performing slice of the leaderboard, not on the specific models whose CMB-Clin standing against GPT-4 is the paper's most attention-grabbing finding. There's also a gap underneath that comparison: nowhere in the paper — not §5.1, not Appendix E.1, not the Table 11 or Figure 5 description — is any inter-annotator agreement (kappa, ICC, or otherwise) reported for the three expert raters themselves; each expert score is simply averaged across the three ("each expert score is averaged over the three expert annotators," per the Figure 5 caption). The entire 0.93 Spearman / 0.71–0.81 Pearson argument for the GPT-4 judge rests on that averaged human signal, and its own reliability is never measured.

The CMB-Exam side has a fairness problem the paper itself surfaces without resolving: the three commercial medical models (JianPeiGPT, ShuKunGPT, AntGLM-Med) are reported with "only the best score," under a "generation strategy... unknown" — i.e., unlike every other row in Table 7, these numbers are not a controlled, reproducible measurement under the paper's own fixed greedy-decoding/prompt protocol, and could reflect cherry-picked runs, different retrieval augmentation, or different prompting the paper has no visibility into. HuatuoGPT-II-34B's headline "surpasses all commercial models" claim is true against these three numbers, but the comparison itself is looser than every other cell in the same table. And the paper says outright where those numbers came from. Appendix C records that JianPeiGPT's “evaluation results were submitted and made public on December 15, 2023 after the opening of CMB,” and the identical note for ShukunGPT (October 23, 2023) and AntGLM-Med (December 23, 2023) — i.e. these are vendor-submitted scores, produced by the model owners after the benchmark was public, sitting in the same table as rows the authors measured themselves. That is a different kind of number, and the headline comparison spans both kinds.

On contamination: the paper raises the possibility explicitly for exactly one model ("Yi-6B achieves such good results with a smaller number of parameters is impressive, but it also reminds us of the possibility of data leakage") and then does not test for it — not for Yi-6B, and not for any of the other 29 systems. This matters more than usual here because CMB-Exam's source questions come from Medtiku, a public Chinese exam-question bank the authors got "explicit permission to share," meaning the same underlying questions were public web content before CMB existed and could plausibly already sit inside the pretraining corpora of Chinese-domestic LLMs (several of which — Qwen, Baichuan2, ChatGLM3, Yi — are exactly the models that lead the CMB-Exam table). No n-gram overlap check, decontamination filter, or held-out-question construction is reported anywhere in the paper or its five appendices.

Model coverage is real but skewed by construction: of the 13 open general-domain LLMs, 11 are Chinese-developed (Qwen×3, Yi×2, Baichuan2×2, ChatGLM3, Internlm×2, Deepseek-llm) and only 2 are Western open-weight models (Mistral-7B-Instruct, Mixtral-8x7B) — both of which land at or near the bottom of Table 6, which the paper attributes to their "limited support for Chinese." That's a fair, hedged explanation, but it also means the paper's central "domestic models have caught up with OpenAI" claim is a comparison against OpenAI's two systems (GPT-4, ChatGPT) and a field of Chinese-domestic competitors, not against the broader population of strong Western open-weight models circa 2023–24 — none of which (LLaMA-2-70B-Chat, for instance) appear in either table at all.

Finally, the accuracy metric itself has an unreported failure mode: answers are pulled from free-text model output via "an empirically designed regular expression," and a response is scored correct "if and only if" the extracted answer exactly matches — but the paper never reports how often extraction fails to find a parseable answer at all, for any model. A model that reasons correctly but formats its final answer atypically has no way to get credit, and there's no visibility into how much of the accuracy gap between, say, DoctorGLM (3.17% average) and the field is a knowledge gap versus a formatting/instruction-following gap the regex simply can't see through.

Two things the paper establishes about itself deserve more weight than it gives them. The first is that its localization case is measured, not merely asserted: Appendix B.1's Table 10 runs 2-gram segmentation over CMB's traditional-Chinese-medicine subcatalog and over MedQA-USMLE and prints the top five medical keywords of each. CMB-TCM returns 气血 (Qi and blood), 舌苔 (tongue coating), 虚证 (deficiency syndrome), 病程 (course of disease) and 舌淡 (pale tongue); MedQA-USMLE returns blood pressure, respiratory rate, physical examination, heart rate and abdominal pain. The overlap is zero. That is a far stronger argument for building a Chinese medical benchmark than any amount of prose about cultural context, and it sits in an appendix.

The second is that CMB's premise closes its own loop, uncomfortably. The paper reports that “subcategories involving traditional Chinese medicine always have relative low accuracy, indicating that additional data on traditional Chinese medicine still needs to be supplemented” — so the exact material that justifies the benchmark's existence is the material models handle worst. Read one way that is the benchmark working as designed; read another, it means the headline averages are dragged by a component whose difficulty is partly a data-availability artifact rather than a reasoning one, and the paper does not separate those. A related crack in the same direction: the paper notes that HuatuoGPT-II-34B, which beats GPT-4 on the exam component, “is even lower than its base model Yi-34B in CMB-Clin” — a model can be tuned into the top of the multiple-choice table while going backwards on the clinical one.

baselines

Mostly fair and controlled (fixed greedy decoding, documented prompts, matched conditions) — except the three commercial medical models (JianPeiGPT, ShuKunGPT, AntGLM-Med), reported as "only the best score" under an "unknown" generation strategy, which the paper's own headline "surpasses all commercial models" claim leans on.

contamination

Flagged in prose for exactly one model (Yi-6B) and never tested for any model, despite CMB-Exam's source questions being pre-existing public exam-bank content that Chinese-domestic pretraining corpora could plausibly already contain.

what's missed

The 320-response expert validation of the GPT-4 judge covers 11 models that exclude Qwen-72B, Yi-34B and every other model that actually ties or nearly ties GPT-4 on CMB-Clin; and the regex answer-extraction failure rate is never reported for any model.

would raise confidence

An n-gram or embedding-based overlap check between CMB-Exam(-Train) and each evaluated model's known pretraining corpus; expert validation extended to the CMB-Exam leaders (Qwen-72B, Yi-34B); a reported answer-extraction failure/abstention rate per model; and a same-protocol re-run of the three asterisked commercial models under the paper's own fixed decoding settings.

// bottom line

CMB is honest about scope — "although not framed as a competitive leaderboard, it serves as a crucial tool for tracking LLM progress in medical domains" — and its judge-validation work (expert correlation, multi-run robustness, temperature-stability) is more thorough than most contemporary LLM-as-judge benchmarks. But the validation covers a different slice of models than the ones its headline results are about, contamination is named as a risk and then left unmeasured, and the exam-side accuracy metric has no reported failure rate for its own answer-extraction step.

// takeaways

What to remember

  1. CMB pairs two genuinely different instruments — CMB-Exam (280,839 raw multiple-choice questions across 6 categories/4 professions/28 subcategories, an 11,200-question test set) for knowledge recall, and CMB-Clin (74 real clinical cases, 208 multi-turn diagnostic questions) for applied reasoning — and the paper's own results show a model's rank on one does not predict its rank on the other.
  2. On CMB-Exam, Qwen-72B-Chat (74.38%), Yi-34B-Chat (69.17%) and even Yi-6B-Chat (65.87%) beat GPT-4's own answer-only average (59.46%, just under the paper's stated 60% "licensing" bar; GPT-4 clears it only under three-shot, at 60.22%) — but the paper flags Yi-6B's result as a possible sign of data leakage without testing for it, on this or any other model.
  3. Fine-tuning DISC-MedLLM directly on CMB-Exam-Train raises its own CMB-Exam accuracy (39.76%→40.85%) but drops its CMB-Clin score (3.58→3.04 out of 5) — training toward the multiple-choice format measurably cost multi-turn clinical dialogue quality, the paper's clearest built-in caution against optimizing for one half of the benchmark alone.
  4. The GPT-4-as-judge protocol behind CMB-Clin is validated more carefully than most (320 expert-scored responses, 0.93 Spearman rank correlation, per-aspect Pearson 0.71–0.81, a 3-run robustness check) — but only on an 11-model subset that excludes the very models (Qwen-72B, Yi-34B) whose near-tie with GPT-4 is the paper's most notable CMB-Clin finding.
  5. CMB explicitly disclaims leaderboard framing: "although not framed as a competitive leaderboard, it serves as a crucial tool for tracking LLM progress in medical domains."
