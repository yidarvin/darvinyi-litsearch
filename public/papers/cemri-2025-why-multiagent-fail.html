Why Do Multi-Agent LLM Systems Fail?

A taxonomy of 14 concrete multi-agent LLM failure modes across 3 categories — system design issues, inter-agent misalignment, task verification — built via grounded theory on real execution traces and scaled to a 1,642-trace public dataset with a calibrated LLM judge.

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

// version note

This page follows the NeurIPS 2025 camera-ready (arXiv v3, posted 26 Oct 2025) — the version source ↗ currently resolves to. The widely-circulated March 2025 preprint (v1, which this node's timeline placement is dated to) called the taxonomy MASFT rather than MAST, studied 5 frameworks instead of 7, and did not include MAST-Data — the 1,642-trace annotated dataset that is v3's headline artifact — at all. The 14 failure-mode IDs and names are stable across both versions; the study around them grew substantially.

Multi-agent LLM systems (MAS) — several LLM-driven agents coordinating through role specifications, message passing, and shared state — have become a default architecture for coding assistants, research agents, and general-purpose task automation. But the paper opens with a blunt empirical finding: across six popular open-source frameworks it evaluates head-to-head (MetaGPT, ChatDev, HyperAgent, AppWorld, AG2, and Magentic-One, each measured on its own natural benchmark), failure rates run from 41.0% (AG2 on OlympiadBench) up to 86.7% (AppWorld on Test-C). Performance gains over single-agent baselines and even simple best-of-N sampling are, in the authors' words, "often minimal."

What's missing, the paper argues, is not another framework or another benchmark score, but a shared vocabulary for what goes wrong. Existing evaluation practice reports a single pass/fail number per run and leaves diagnosis to a human reading raw traces — traces that can run over 15,000 lines of text apiece. There has been no standardized way to say that two different systems failed for the same underlying reason, or that a fix which helps one failure mode leaves five others untouched. The authors motivate this with a pointed inversion of Tolstoy: "Successful systems all work alike; each failing system has its own problems" — and then spend the rest of the paper arguing that isn't quite true either, because failing MAS keep failing in a small, name-able set of recurring ways.

// how it works

The method

MAST — the Multi-Agent System Failure Taxonomy — was built in three stages (Figure 2). First, six expert human annotators ran a grounded-theory analysis (open coding, constant comparative analysis, memoing) over 150 execution traces drawn from five of the seven frameworks eventually studied, spanning programming and math-problem-solving tasks. This took over 20 hours of annotation per expert and continued until the annotators hit theoretical saturation — the point where further traces stopped surfacing new failure patterns.

Second, the resulting draft taxonomy was hardened through three rounds of inter-annotator agreement (IAA): three annotators independently labeled random 5-trace batches with the current taxonomy version, then discussed disagreements and revised failure-mode definitions — merging, splitting, or renaming modes — until consensus stabilized. This took roughly 10 hours of discussion alone (not counting annotation time) and converged on a final Cohen's κ = 0.88 across 15 traces (five per round, three rounds) drawn from the same 150-trace pool the taxonomy was being built from. The output is MAST's 14 failure modes, grouped into 3 categories — System Design Issues, Inter-Agent Misalignment, and Task Verification — each mapped to the execution stage (pre-execution, execution, or post-execution) where its root cause typically originates. Figure 3 shows one labeled trace segment: in an AppWorld task, a Phone Agent never tells the Supervisor Agent that its login API expects a phone number in the username field, and the Supervisor never asks for clarification — labeled FM-2.4, Information Withholding.

Manually annotating thousands of traces this way doesn't scale, so the third stage builds an LLM-as-a-Judge pipeline (the "LLM annotator"): OpenAI's o1 model, prompted with a trace, the MAST definitions, and a handful of human-labeled few-shot examples. Calibrated this way, the annotator reaches 94% accuracy and κ = 0.77 against held-out human labels — up from 89% accuracy and κ = 0.58 zero-shot. Checked again on two frameworks (OpenManus, Magentic-One) and two benchmarks (MMLU, GAIA) that played no role in building the taxonomy, a fresh human IAA round against the finalized MAST still holds at κ = 0.79, supporting the claim that the definitions transfer rather than overfitting to the five original frameworks. That calibrated annotator is what scales the dataset from 150 hand-built traces to MAST-Data: 1,642 annotated traces spanning seven frameworks (the original five, plus Magentic-One and OpenManus) and six models across four families (GPT-4 series — GPT-4, GPT-4o, GPT-4o-mini — plus Claude-3.7-Sonnet, Qwen2.5-Coder-32B-Instruct, and CodeLlama-7b-Instruct-hf).

// what they found

The results

Applied across all 1,642 traces, MAST's own diagram (Figure 1) gives the headline breakdown: System Design Issues account for 44.2% of labeled failures, Inter-Agent Misalignment for 32.3%, and Task Verification for 23.5%. Within those, the single most common individual mode is Step Repetition (FM-1.3, 15.7% of traces — an agent looping on an already-completed sub-task), followed by Reasoning-Action Mismatch (FM-2.6, 13.2%) and Unaware of Termination Conditions (FM-1.5, 12.4%). The rarest labeled mode is Information Withholding (FM-2.4), at well under 1% of traces — the figure prints 0.80% while the paper's own body text gives 0.85% for the same mode, a small internal inconsistency we can't resolve from outside the paper.

Break the same taxonomy down by framework (Figure 4, using a smaller 210-trace human/LLM double-annotated core rather than the full 1,642) and the profiles diverge sharply. AppWorld's failures skew toward premature termination — its star topology has no predefined workflow, so agents lack an obvious signal for "done." OpenManus leans on step repetition, and HyperAgent's dominant modes are step repetition and incorrect verification. The paper is explicit that this framework-level chart is "not intended to serve as a performance comparison across MAS," since each system runs its own benchmark.

MAST also isolates how much of a failure profile traces back to the underlying LLM versus the MAS architecture. Holding MetaGPT fixed and swapping Claude-3.7-Sonnet for GPT-4o cuts System Design Issues from 63 to 38 failures — a 39% reduction — while Task Verification barely moves (83 vs. 77), suggesting verification weaknesses are more architectural than model-dependent (see the critique section for this comparison, Figure 8). Symmetrically, holding GPT-4o fixed and swapping MetaGPT for ChatDev on the same ProgramDev-v2 benchmark cuts System Design and Inter-Agent-Misalignment failures by 60-68%, but roughly triples Task Verification failures (77 for MetaGPT vs. 30 for ChatDev) — MetaGPT enforces roles mostly through Standard-Operating-Procedure prompts, while ChatDev builds explicit review and testing phases into its workflow.

The taxonomy is also demonstrably actionable, not just descriptive. In a ChatDev case study, tightening role-specification prompts alone raised task success on a 32-task custom coding benchmark (ProgramDev-v0) from 25.0% to 34.4% (+9.4 points), and restructuring the agent topology to add a CTO-confirmed, higher-level verification step raised it further to 40.6% (+15.6 points over baseline) — the paper's own headline "max improvement" figure. On AG2's MathChat setup (200 GSM-Plus problems, six repetitions per configuration), a clearer prompt plus an explicit verification step improved accuracy with GPT-4o from 84.25% to as high as 89.00% (Wilcoxon p = 0.03); with GPT-4, the improved prompt alone was likewise significant (84.75% → 89.75%), but the separately-tested three-role topology — a parallel configuration, not an addition to the improved prompt — was not (84.75% → 85.50%, Wilcoxon p = 0.4).

// does it hold up?

The eval read

The central methodological question for a grounded-theory taxonomy is whether its categories are actually distinct causes rather than co-occurring symptoms of the same underlying problem, and here the paper's own numbers are in tension with its own framing. At the category level, MAST reports low pairwise correlations (0.17-0.32) and argues this "suggests the categories capture distinct aspects of MAS failures with limited overlap." But the finer-grained, mode-level correlation matrix (14×14) that the paper reports in its own appendix reaches a maximum of 0.63 — between FM-1.4 (Loss of Conversation History) and FM-2.1 (Conversation Reset) — which the authors themselves flag as a case where "modes with similar symptoms might lead automated evaluators to conflate distinct root causes." That's a fair and honest admission, but it also means the clean 44.2/32.3/23.5 category split in Figure 1 is doing more rhetorical work than the correlation evidence alone supports, and while the paper's own appendix worked examples label single traces with several modes from different categories at once (e.g. Appendix N.1, N.9, N.11), it never reports a labels-per-trace distribution or co-occurrence rate — the detail that would settle whether "distinct categories" means causally distinct or just definitionally distinct.

Two of the paper's own reconciliation claims don't check out against its own figures, and given that failure-counting is the entire contribution, that's worth flagging precisely rather than gesturing at. Comparing MetaGPT and ChatDev on the same GPT-4o/ProgramDev-v2 setting (Figure 9), the text states MetaGPT "has 1.56x more FC3 failure than ChatDev" — but Figure 9's own printed bars give MetaGPT 77 Task Verification failures against ChatDev's 30, a ratio of 2.57x, not 1.56x. The qualitative direction (MetaGPT verifies less rigorously) is right; the multiplier that's supposed to quantify it is not. Separately, the taxonomy's rarest mode (FM-2.4, Information Withholding) is given as 0.80% in Figure 1's own diagram and 0.85% in the body text describing that same figure two pages earlier — a small gap, but one the paper never resolves either way.

On sample size and generalizability: the human-annotated core that underwrites most of the paper's qualitative claims about per-framework failure profiles is 30 traces per framework (210 total) — each framework paired with one benchmark, one model family, once. The paper's own framing treats this as descriptive rather than comparative for exactly this reason, but readers citing "AppWorld fails on premature termination" or "HyperAgent struggles with verification" should keep in mind that's a 30-trace, single-configuration read, not an averaged property of the framework. And the seven frameworks studied (five chosen via "theoretical sampling" for the taxonomy-building phase, two added later for generalization testing) are all open-source and mostly research-oriented (ChatDev, MetaGPT, AG2, Magentic-One, OpenManus) rather than production agent stacks — it's an open question how much of the 44.2/32.3/23.5 split would shift for, say, a commercial customer-support or coding-agent deployment with heavier guardrails.

baselines

Figure 5's six framework/benchmark pairs are each measured on a different task distribution (MetaGPT/ProgramDev, AppWorld/Test-C, AG2/OlympiadBench, etc.) — the paper is careful to caption this as non-comparable, but the 41%-86.7% failure-rate range is still liable to be read as a framework leaderboard rather than what it is: six single-configuration snapshots.

contamination

Not discussed for any of the benchmarks reused off the shelf (GSM-Plus, OlympiadBench, SWE-Bench Lite, GAIA, MMLU). The one benchmark the authors built themselves, ProgramDev, was deliberately designed to be "relatively straightforward... rather than exceptionally difficult" to isolate failure dynamics — a reasonable design choice for taxonomy-building, but it also means the paper's coding-task failure profile may not transfer to harder, more contamination-resistant coding benchmarks.

what's missed

Whether the LLM annotator's errors are systematically biased toward or against particular modes (rather than just an aggregate 94%/κ=0.77) — if the judge under-detects, say, subtle information-withholding relative to loud step-repetition, the 1,642-trace MAST-Data distribution inherits that skew even though the taxonomy definitions themselves generalize (κ = 0.79 on held-out frameworks).

would raise confidence

A per-mode breakdown of the LLM annotator's precision/recall (not just an aggregate F1 of 0.80), a labels-per-trace distribution or co-occurrence rate (the appendix already shows individual traces carrying several modes at once, e.g. N.1, N.9, N.11, but never quantifies how often), and a corrected next revision reconciling the 1.56x/2.57x Figure-9 discrepancy and the 0.80%/0.85% FM-2.4 discrepancy — small fixes, but exactly the kind of internal-consistency check a failure-counting paper should pass cleanly.

// bottom line

MAST is a genuinely useful contribution: 14 modes concrete enough to name a bug and specific enough to guide a fix, built through a defensible grounded-theory process rather than an armchair top-down taxonomy, and validated by its own intervention studies (+9.4 to +15.6 points from MAST-guided fixes). But the categories are less independent than the headline correlation numbers suggest once you look at mode-level pairs, and at least two of the paper's own summary statistics don't match the figures they're describing — worth a careful re-read of the exact numbers before citing MAST's percentages as settled ground truth.

// takeaways

What to remember

  1. MAST names 14 concrete multi-agent failure modes across 3 categories — System Design Issues (44.2%), Inter-Agent Misalignment (32.3%), Task Verification (23.5%) of 1,642 annotated traces — and those 14 mode IDs are stable across the paper's preprint and camera-ready versions, even though the taxonomy's name (MASFT → MAST), the study's scope (5 → 7 frameworks), and its headline dataset (MAST-Data didn't exist in the preprint at all) all changed.
  2. The taxonomy scales via a calibrated LLM-as-a-Judge annotator (94% accuracy, κ = 0.77 against human experts) — this is what turns a 150-trace, expert-hours-intensive grounded-theory study into a 1,642-trace public dataset, MAST-Data. A separate human IAA round on two frameworks/benchmarks the taxonomy was never built on holds at κ = 0.79, showing the definitions themselves transfer — that number is about MAST's generalization, not the LLM judge's own agreement.
  3. The taxonomy is demonstrably actionable: MAST-guided interventions raised ChatDev's task success by up to 15.6 percentage points on a custom coding benchmark. But the gains aren't universal (AG2's topology change wasn't statistically significant with GPT-4, p = 0.4, though its prompt change still was) and are explicitly framed by the authors as a first step, not a fix for MAS reliability.
