Where LLM Agents Fail and How They can Learn From Failures

A five-module taxonomy of agent failure modes, a 200-trajectory human-annotated benchmark of failed rollouts across ALFWorld, GAIA and WebShop, and AgentDebug — a framework that isolates one root-cause step in a failed trajectory and feeds the agent corrective advice to try again.

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

LLM agents that chain planning, memory, reflection and tool-use modules fail often, and when they fail on a long trajectory, the visible symptom — a malformed API call at step 24, say — is usually not the cause. An early misread of the environment at step 4 can silently distort every plan that follows, and by the time the agent runs out of steps the failure looks like it belongs to whichever module happened to be active last. The paper's term for this is error propagation: "a single root-cause error propagates through subsequent decisions, leading to task failure."

The authors argue prior failure-analysis work — including taxonomies of multi-agent breakdowns and case studies of planning brittleness — stops at enumerating error types or giving qualitative examples, without a systematic mechanism for tracing a failure back to the step that actually caused it, and without turning that diagnosis into a way for the agent to recover. Naming a failure type at every step tells you where things went wrong locally; it doesn't tell you which of those local mistakes, corrected, would have flipped the trajectory to success. That's the gap the paper's three contributions target: a taxonomy that organizes failures by the module they originate in, a benchmark of trajectories annotated down to that root cause, and a debugging framework that acts on the diagnosis rather than just reporting it.

// how it works

The method

The paper is three linked artifacts, built in sequence.

1. AgentErrorTaxonomy

Starting from a large-scale study of "hundreds" of failed trajectories, the authors decompose every agent rollout into four operational modules — Memory (recall/retrieval), Reflection (progress and outcome judgment), Planning (strategy formation) and Action (execution) — plus a fifth, non-cognitive System category for tool crashes, API mismatches and step-limit exhaustion. Each module gets 3–4 leaf error types (17 in total: e.g. Memory's "Hallucination (False Memory)" and "Retrieval Failure"; Planning's "Constraint Ignorance" and "Impossible Action"; System's "Tool Execution Error" and "LLM Limit"), documented with one-line definitions in the paper's Table 2. Because the module boundaries are fixed in advance — they mirror the agent's own plan→act→observe architecture — the taxonomy doubles as an annotation schema: any error a human reviewer flags has a module and an error type it plugs into by construction (see the critique below for what this trades away).

2. AgentErrorBench

From an initial pool of over 500 failed trajectories collected on ALFWorld, GAIA and WebShop, the authors curated 200 for annotation — 100 ALFWorld, 50 WebShop, 50 GAIA. Ten expert annotators (graduate students with NLP/agent-research background) labeled every decision step against the taxonomy, and were separately asked to identify the minimal set of root-cause failures — not every surface mistake, but the earliest step whose correction would explain the downstream cascade — under guidelines refined over three rounds of pilot annotation. Consistency was checked with a training phase plus independent double-annotation on a shared subset, with disagreements adjudicated collectively; the paper reports the resulting inter-annotator agreement at Cohen's κ = 0.55 across modules, which it calls "substantial agreement" (see critique). The paper does not state which model(s) produced the original 500-trajectory failure pool before curation.

3. AgentDebug

AgentDebug is a three-stage pipeline that runs on a completed (failed) trajectory:

  • Stage 1 — Fine-grained analysis. Every step is mapped, module by module, to a taxonomy error type, producing a full per-step, per-module error profile.
  • Stage 2 — Critical-error detection. The system identifies the earliest step whose correction would flip the trajectory to success. Here the paper describes the mechanism two different ways: Algorithm 1's own inline comment labels this stage "Critical Error Detection via LLM (no rollout/counterfactuals)," driven by a single call to a `DetectCriticalErrors` LLM routine — but the prose in §3.2 (two pages later) is titled "Critical Error Detection via Counterfactuals" and describes literally re-executing the trajectory: "we perform counterfactual testing step by step: at each point, we substitute a corrected action and test whether the rollout would succeed." Whichever mechanism actually runs, the identified step, module and error type become the diagnosis.
  • Stage 3 — Iterative debugging. The agent re-rolls out from the critical step under targeted, taxonomy-grounded feedback (up to 5 attempts); if it still fails, the feedback is refined and the attempt repeats.

// what they found

The results

Two separate experiments. The first scores critical-error detection against AgentErrorBench's 200 human-annotated ground truths, using GPT-4.1 as AgentDebug's detector, temperature 0. AgentDebug is compared against three baselines the authors built for this task specifically: Direct Prompting (ask the LLM directly, no search), Brute Force (substitute a corrected action at each step in order, stop at the first success), and Binary Search (divide-and-conquer over the trajectory). On average across the three environments, AgentDebug reaches 45.0% Step accuracy, 31.3% Step+Module, and 24.3% All-Correct, against the strongest baseline's 28.0% / 10.0% / 0.3% (Direct Prompting on Step and Step+Module; Direct Prompting ties Binary Search at 0.3% on All-Correct) — the paper's headline "24% higher all-correct, 17% higher step accuracy."

The second experiment tests whether that detection translates into recovered task success. On ALFWorld, with token budgets matched across methods, AgentDebug raises accumulative success from a First-Attempt baseline of 21→55 on GPT-4o-mini, 48→74 on Qwen3-8B, and 60→84 on Qwen3-Next-80B, against baselines Self-Refine, Best-of-N(5), Vanilla-Debug and Tree-of-Thought. AgentDebug is the strict maximum on GPT-4o-mini and Qwen3-Next-80B; on Qwen3-8B it ties Self-Refine at 74 (see critique).

Extending to GAIA and WebShop (against the two strongest baselines, Self-Refine and Best-of-N; the paper does not state which backbone model this comparison uses), AgentDebug's relative improvement is 26% on ALFWorld (tied with Self-Refine), 14% on GAIA (tied with Best-of-N), and a clear 16% on WebShop against Best-of-N's 10% and Self-Refine's 4% — averaging 18.7% against 14.7% (Best-of-N) and 14.0% (Self-Refine).

Ablations: swapping AgentDebug's own detector model shows GPT-4.1 as the clear best base model (42.0 Step / 44.0 Error / 32.0 Step+M / 32.0 All), against Llama-3.3-70B (16.0/16.0/6.0/2.0), GPT-4o-mini (14.0/10.0/4.0/2.0) and Qwen3-Next-80B (4.0/14.0/2.0/2.0) — the max on every one of the four metrics, checked against the full column. On ALFWorld zero-shot rollout-strategy comparisons, the paper's proposed "Modular" rollout scores 0.38 success, ahead of Memory+ReAct (0.34), Reflection (0.32), ReAct (0.26) and Act Only (0.10) — again the maximum of its column.

// does it hold up?

The eval read

Root cause is a causal claim, and the paper answers it two different ways. For AgentErrorBench's ground truth, "root cause" is established by human annotation judgment: ten graduate annotators, trained and calibrated over three pilot rounds, asked to name the minimal step whose correction explains the cascade — not a formal counterfactual replay against the live environment. For AgentDebug's own inference-time detection, the paper gives two incompatible answers to the same question. Algorithm 1's pseudocode explicitly labels Stage 2 "Critical Error Detection via LLM (no rollout/counterfactuals)", with the mechanism being a single LLM call. Section 3.2's prose, two pages later, titles the identical stage "Critical Error Detection via Counterfactuals" and describes literally substituting a corrected action at each step and testing whether the rollout would succeed — i.e. exactly the expensive procedure the paper elsewhere calls "Brute Force" and reports at 0.0% All-Correct accuracy. A reader cannot tell from the paper which of these AgentDebug actually runs, and the two mechanisms have very different cost and correctness profiles.

The inter-annotator agreement is real but oversold. Unlike the closest sibling paper in this space — Who&When (Zhang et al., 2025), whose ground-truth labels carry no formal agreement statistic at all — this paper does report one: Cohen's κ = 0.55 across modules. That is a genuine strength. But the paper calls κ = 0.55 "substantial agreement," and under the standard Landis & Koch bands the paper is implicitly invoking, 0.55 falls in the "moderate" band (0.41–0.60), one tier below "substantial" (0.61–0.80). It's a specific, checkable mislabel, not a fatal flaw — 0.55 is still a real, positive signal — but "substantial" overstates it by one band.

The "first dataset" claim is scoped, but not consistently. The paper does cite Zhang et al. (2025) — Who&When — in its related-work section, filed among multi-agent studies of "coordination collapse and dialogue drift." It never engages with Who&When as a competing prior dataset of annotated failure trajectories, even though that's exactly what Who&When is: 184 expert-annotated failure-log instances across 127 multi-agent systems, five months earlier. The abstract's actual claim — "AgentErrorBench, the first dataset of systematically annotated failure trajectories from ALFWorld, GAIA, and WebShop" — survives on that environment scoping, since Who&When's instances come from different multi-agent systems rather than these three single-agent benchmarks. But the paper repeats an unscoped version of the claim twice — "offering the first standardized testbed for error detection and debugging" (§1 and again §2.2, word-for-word) — with no environment qualifier, and Who&When already is a standardized testbed for automated failure/error detection (its own contribution is scoring LLM-based attribution methods against annotated ground truth). The "and debugging" half of that claim is a fair distinction — Who&When has no recovery/correction component — but "for error detection" alone is not clearly first.

Two of the paper's three headline superlatives are ties, not wins, on the paper's own figures. "AgentDebug consistently outperforms all baselines" (§4.2, discussing Figure 5): true on GPT-4o-mini (55 vs. next-best 41) and Qwen3-Next-80B (84 vs. 78), but on Qwen3-8B, Self-Refine and AgentDebug are both printed at 74 — a tie, not an AgentDebug win. "AgentDebug consistently delivers the largest gains across all three benchmarks" (§4.2, discussing Figure 6): true on WebShop (16 vs. 10 vs. 4) but AgentDebug ties Self-Refine on ALFWorld (26=26) and ties Best-of-N on GAIA (14=14) — two of the three benchmarks are ties, and only the WebShop margin carries the average (18.7 vs. 14.7 vs. 14.0). Neither finding erases the paper's real advantage — the average and the detection numbers are genuinely ahead — but "consistently outperforms" and "consistently delivers the largest gains" are stronger than what the paper's own bar charts show.

Everything happens in simulated or sandboxed environments. ALFWorld is embodied text-simulation, WebShop a scripted e-commerce sandbox, GAIA a benchmark suite of tool-use/QA tasks — none involve a live, adversarial, or physically embodied deployment. The taxonomy's five modules were derived from a large-scale read of failures in exactly these three environments, and they mirror the agent's own memory→reflection→planning→action architecture rather than being built bottom-up without that scaffold; that's a reasonable and interpretable design choice, but it also means an error that doesn't cleanly decompose into one of these five buckets (e.g. a failure that's genuinely about miscommunication between modules, rather than a mistake localized inside one of them) has nowhere obvious to land.

baselines

Split by experiment. The recovery baselines (Self-Refine, Best-of-N, Tree-of-Thought, Vanilla-Debug) are real published techniques, token-budget-matched to AgentDebug — a fair comparison, even where it produces ties. The detection baselines (Direct Prompting, Brute Force, Binary Search) are constructed by the authors specifically for this new task; no existing published attribution method (e.g. Who&When's own methods) is run head-to-head on AgentErrorBench.

contamination

Not a pretraining-contamination question. The closer risk is circularity between taxonomy, ground truth and method — mitigated on the label side (all 200 AgentErrorBench annotations are human, with double-annotation and adjudication), but the paper never states which model(s) generated the original 500-trajectory pool that was curated down to those 200.

what's missed

Whether AgentDebug's own root-cause detector actually re-executes candidates (as §3.2's prose says) or is a single LLM guess (as Algorithm 1's own comment says); a per-module or per-pair breakdown of the κ=0.55 agreement figure rather than one aggregate number; and a direct comparison against Who&When's attribution method on AgentErrorBench, which would test whether the benchmark travels beyond the paper's own three home-built baselines.

would raise confidence

Resolving the Stage-2 discrepancy with an ablation that isolates LLM-only vs. rollout-verified critical-error detection; stating the backbone model(s) used for Figure 6 and for the original trajectory collection; and reporting the ALFWorld/GAIA ties in Figure 5/6's own prose rather than the current "consistently outperforms" framing.

// bottom line

The taxonomy is a clean, well-documented organizing scheme, the benchmark is a genuine (and, per the released GitHub repository, actually shipped) contribution with a reported agreement statistic that Who&When conspicuously lacked, and AgentDebug's detection numbers are a real, large jump over three home-built baselines. But the paper's own algorithm box contradicts its own method prose on how AgentDebug identifies a root cause, its two strongest downstream-recovery claims are ties on its own bar charts on two of three benchmarks tested, and its broadest priority claim ("first standardized testbed for error detection") is not clearly true against a paper it cites twice — once in the introduction, once in related work — without ever engaging the overlap. It also reports AgentDebug's own headline detection scores twice with different numbers: 45.0% step accuracy and 24.3% all-correct in §4.1, then 50.0% and 42.5% in the Findings summary two paragraphs later. None of that erases the contribution; all of it is checkable from the paper's own figures and prose, and none of it was.

// takeaways

What to remember

  1. Three linked contributions: AgentErrorTaxonomy (5 modules, 17 error types), AgentErrorBench (200 human-annotated failure trajectories from ALFWorld/GAIA/WebShop, κ=0.55 agreement), and AgentDebug (isolates one root-cause step, gives corrective feedback, re-rolls out).
  2. Detection: AgentDebug averages 45.0% Step / 31.3% Step+Module / 24.3% All-Correct accuracy against home-built baselines topping out at 28.0% / 10.0% / 0.3%. Recovery: up to 26% relative task-success improvement on ALFWorld and WebShop, though it ties the strongest baseline on Qwen3-8B and on the ALFWorld/GAIA improvement figures.
  3. The paper's own algorithm box and its own method prose describe AgentDebug's root-cause detection two contradictory ways — "no rollout/counterfactuals" (LLM-only) vs. "via Counterfactuals" (step-by-step re-execution) — a discrepancy worth resolving before trusting either the cost or the correctness story.
