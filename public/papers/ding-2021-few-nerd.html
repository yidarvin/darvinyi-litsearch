Few-NERD: A Few-shot Named Entity Recognition Dataset

Few-NERD swaps the 4–18-type schemas of CoNLL'03, WNUT'17 and OntoNotes for an 8-coarse/66-fine hierarchy over 188,238 human-annotated Wikipedia sentences — the first NER dataset purpose-built for few-shot learning rather than repurposed. A fully-supervised BERT-Tagger still drops from 91.34 F1 (CoNLL'03) to 67.13 on FEW-NERD (SUP), and the best few-shot baseline tops out at 58.79 F1 in its easiest setting.

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

Few-shot NER research before this paper reused existing supervised NER corpora — CoNLL'03 (4 entity types), WNUT'17 (6 types), OntoNotes 5.0 (18 types, 7 of which are value/numeric types like Cardinal or Percent rather than named entities) — and artificially re-split their small label sets into episodic few-shot tasks. That strategy has two structural problems the authors point to. First, with only 4–18 coarse types on offer, there isn't enough variety to build a meaningful number of distinct N-way meta-tasks, and the type-to-type correlations a few-shot learner needs to exploit barely exist at that granularity. Second, because no shared few-shot NER benchmark existed, separate papers (Hofer et al., 2018; Fritzler et al., 2019; Yang and Katiyar, 2020; Li et al., 2020a; Huang et al., 2020) each built their own incompatible few-shot splits from these datasets, so reported numbers across papers weren't really comparable.

Underlying both problems is a mismatch with practice: the authors argue that most genuinely unseen entity types encountered in the wild are fine-grained — a new kind of scholar, disease, or vehicle — rather than coarse — a wholly new notion of "location." A benchmark built only from coarse relabelings of old corpora tests the wrong generalization skill. FEW-NERD is the response: a dataset built from scratch with a two-level, 8-coarse/66-fine entity schema, specifically so that few-shot NER can be evaluated on the axis that actually matters.

// how it works

The method

FEW-NERD is built from scratch rather than re-split from an existing corpus. Starting from FIGER's 112-tag fine-grained entity schema (Ling and Weld, 2012), the authors first collapse it into a two-level hierarchy of 8 coarse types — Person, Location, Organization, Art, Building, Product, Event, Miscellaneous — deliberately omitting value/numerical/date types (Cardinal, Day, Percent, etc.) that FIGER-style schemas usually carry, since those aren't named entities. They then keep only the 80 highest-frequency fine subtypes from FIGER's automatic annotations, and finally run several rounds of pre-annotation and manual denoising to fix problems the frequency filter alone couldn't catch — merging Country/Province/State/City/Restrict into one GPE type because they're hard to tell apart from context (especially GPEs at different points in history), and adding a dedicated Person-Scholar type once pre-annotation surfaced how many person mentions were actually mathematicians, physicists, chemists, biologists, or paleontologists that the FIGER schema had no slot for. The result is the final 66 fine types shown below.

Text comes from the full English Wikipedia dump, extracted with WikiExtractor and tokenized with NLTK. Candidate paragraphs are located through a Lucene-indexed search over dictionaries of entity mentions harvested from FIGER's automatic (distantly-supervised) annotations: 10 candidate paragraphs are retrieved per entity, then 1,000 paragraphs are randomly sampled per fine type for human annotation — 66,000 paragraphs in total, averaging 61.3 tokens each. Annotation itself is entirely human: 70 trained annotators plus 10 experienced experts double-annotate every paragraph (each annotator spends an average of 32 hours on the task), an expert then adjudicates each pair, and quality is enforced per batch — a random 10% of each 1,000–3,000-sentence batch is spot-checked, and if sentence-level accuracy falls below 95% the entire batch is re-annotated. Overall inter-annotator agreement, measured with Cohen's Kappa, is 76.44%. The final corpus totals 188,238 sentences, 4,601,160 tokens, and 491.7k manually annotated entities across the 66 fine types — well past OntoNotes 5.0 (103.8k sentences / 2,067k tokens / 161.8k entities / 18 types) and I2B2 (107.9k sentences / 805.1k tokens / 28.9k entities / 23 types), the next-largest comparators the paper reports.

On top of this one corpus, the paper defines three benchmark tasks that probe different kinds of generalization. FEW-NERD (SUP) is an ordinary 70/10/20 supervised split with all 66 types present in every split, testing instance-level generalization. The two few-shot tasks instead partition the 66 fine-grained types into disjoint train/dev/test type sets and blank out (replace with the "O" tag) any entity whose type falls outside the active split — but they disagree on how that partition is drawn. FEW-NERD (INTRA) splits by coarse type: every fine type under Person, Miscellaneous, Art, and Product is assigned to train; Event and Building to dev; Organization and Location to test — so train, dev, and test share almost no coarse-level structure. That design choice is motivated by a separate correlation study (Figure 2): a BERT-Tagger trained on 70% of FEW-NERD (SUP) is used to embed entity mentions from the held-out 20%, and the mutual similarity between all 66 fine types shows entity types under the same coarse parent clustering more tightly than types across coarse boundaries — implying an INTRA-style split should make transfer harder. FEW-NERD (INTER) instead splits within each of the 8 coarse types, sending roughly 60% of each coarse type's fine subtypes to train, 20% to dev, and 20% to test, so all 8 coarse categories appear in every split — testing whether coarse-level shared knowledge helps a model generalize to genuinely new fine types.

Because NER is sequence labeling rather than single-label classification, one sentence typically contains several entities from different classes at once — which breaks the greedy N-way K-shot samplers used by prior few-shot NER work, since they get stuck near the end of an episode needing a sentence with exactly one specific remaining type. FEW-NERD instead uses a N-way K∼2K-shot greedy sampling algorithm (Algorithm 1): sentences keep being added to the support set as long as no class count exceeds a 2K cap, relaxing the exact-K requirement so sentence-level sampling stays tractable on densely-annotated text. Evaluation reports micro precision/recall/F1 over an IO tagging scheme (I-type / O, no B- prefix) across sampled test episodes.

// what they found

The results

Every model in the paper shares the same BERTbase backbone (768 hidden size, 12 layers/heads, ~120M parameters), so differences below come from the task, not the encoder. BERT-Tagger — a linear classifier on top of BERT, trained under full supervision — reaches 91.34 F1 on CoNLL'03 and 89.11 F1 on OntoNotes 5.0, the two most commonly reported supervised NER numbers, but the identical architecture drops to 67.13 F1 on FEW-NERD (SUP) — roughly a 22–24 point fall relative to both, from having to distinguish 66 fine types instead of 4 or 18.

Per coarse type, performance is uneven (Figure 3): averaged F1 ranges from 85% on Person, the best-performing coarse type, down to 60% on Product, the worst.

The few-shot settings are harder still. Across ProtoBERT, NNShot, and StructShot at four sampling regimes (5-way 1∼2-shot, 5-way 5∼10-shot, 10-way 1∼2-shot, 10-way 5∼10-shot), every model scores lower on FEW-NERD (INTRA) than on the matching FEW-NERD (INTER) setting — for example StructShot's F1 falls from 51.88 (INTER, 5-way 1∼2-shot) to 30.21 on the equivalent INTRA setting — consistent with INTRA's design intent of withholding shared coarse-level structure. Within each task, the hardest setting is always 10-way 1∼2-shot and the easiest is always 5-way 5∼10-shot, for all three models on both tasks. The three models also trade places depending on shot count: ProtoBERT has the strongest F1 of the three in all four 5∼10-shot columns (58.79 F1 on INTER 5-way 5∼10-shot, vs. 55.64 for NNShot and 57.32 for StructShot) but is the weakest of the three in all four 1∼2-shot columns (32.45 vs. 38.87/43.34 on INTER 10-way 1∼2-shot) — separately, the paper attributes StructShot's large precision gain on FEW-NERD (INTRA) specifically to its Viterbi decoder removing false-positive predictions, which it frames as most useful exactly where INTRA's coarse-disjoint split makes knowledge transfer hardest. Even in its single best-case setting (INTER, 5-way 5∼10-shot), the best-placed model — ProtoBERT — tops out at 58.79 F1, still roughly 30 points behind the supervised BERT-Tagger numbers above (91.34 on CoNLL'03, 89.11 on OntoNotes 5.0).

An error-decomposition study (5-way 5∼10-shot on FEW-NERD (INTER), averaged over 5 runs) splits mistakes into span errors — an "O" token wrongly tagged as an entity (false positive) or an entity token wrongly tagged "O" (false negative) — and type errors, where the span is found correctly but the wrong type is assigned, either "within" the same coarse type or to an "outer," entirely different, coarse type. Span-level FP/FN rates are modest for all three models (2.17–4.45%), but "outer" type errors are the single largest error category for every model (5.35–6.90%) — larger than "within"-coarse-type confusions (3.86–3.94%). The paper's own reading of this breakdown does not follow the percentages directly: it first notes that token-level accuracy is "not that low since most O tokens could be detected," but because the evaluation counts an entire entity mention wrong if even a single token is mistagged, that strict entity-level criterion is what the paper calls "the main reason for the challenge of FEW-NERD" — and it states that "if an entity span could be accurately detected, the models could yield relatively good performance on entity typing." So the paper reads the numbers as showing typing is comparatively manageable once a span is right, and points to the strictness of mention-level scoring — not the type-error percentages themselves — as the harder-won half of the problem.

// does it hold up?

The eval read

FEW-NERD's baselines are a genuinely fair comparison on paper: BERT-Tagger, ProtoBERT, NNShot, and StructShot all sit on an identical BERTbase encoder, so the reported numbers isolate task difficulty rather than encoder quality, and NNShot/StructShot were the contemporaneous state of the art for few-shot NER (Yang and Katiyar, 2020). But several things temper how much weight to put on the specific numbers, and one of them is a flag about the paper itself rather than about the dataset design.

The version history is a real reproducibility hazard, and a narrower one than a first diff suggests. arXiv:2105.07464 has gone through six revisions between May and September 2021. Diffing v1 against the currently-live v6 shows every FEW-NERD-specific result was silently re-run: Table 4's FEW-NERD (SUP) row (65.56/68.78/67.13 here, versus 67.39/70.45/68.88 in v1), all of Tables 5, 6 and 7, and the per-coarse-type F1 breakdown (Figure 3 above), which does not exist in v1 at all. The two borrowed reference rows in that same Table 4 — CoNLL'03 (90.62/92.07/91.34) and OntoNotes 5.0 (90.00/88.24/89.11) — are byte-identical between v1 and v6, so those baselines were left untouched while everything specific to the authors' own dataset was redone. The dataset-construction facts (188,238 sentences, the 8-coarse/66-fine schema) also stayed fixed throughout. The arXiv comment field for v6 reads only "Accepted by ACL-IJCNLP 2021 (long paper), update," with no erratum, corrigendum, or changelog anywhere in the paper explaining what changed or why. That matters in practice: FEW-NERD was cited hundreds of times through 2021–2023, plausibly against the old v1 FEW-NERD (SUP) and few-shot numbers, and anyone checking a remembered baseline figure against the arXiv page today (v6, the version this page uses throughout) will find it silently disagrees. One smaller slip sits in the same neighbourhood: the paper cites CoNLL'03 as “(Tjong Kim Sang, 2002)” throughout, but that is the CoNLL-2002 shared task, which covers Spanish and Dutch. CoNLL-2003 is Tjong Kim Sang & De Meulder — a name that appears nowhere in the bibliography — and the paper's own description of the dataset (“curated from Reuters News and includes 4 coarse-grained entity types”) is the 2003 English set, not the 2002 one. The description is right; the citation points one year and two languages away.

There is no human-performance ceiling anywhere in the paper. The only label-quality signal offered is a single aggregate Cohen's Kappa of 76.44% between the two human annotators — not broken out by coarse or fine type — and no separate human baseline is run through the same P/R/F1 evaluation harness as the models. Given that fine-grained types are exactly where genuine ambiguity concentrates (the paper's own example: whether "London" is tagged Location-GPE or Art-Music depends entirely on whether the sentence is about the city or the album), it's hard to know how much of the 30–70-point F1 gap between the few-shot models and CoNLL’03/OntoNotes-level supervision (against Few-NERD’s own 67.13 SUP score the gap is 8.3–52.1) reflects model weakness rather than irreducible annotation disagreement.

The corpus is not sampled independently of the label schema. Candidate paragraphs are found through a distant-dictionary search — a Lucene index built over automatically (FIGER-)labeled entity mentions — so every candidate sentence already contained a term the search engine believed matched one of the 66 target types before a human annotator ever saw it. That's a reasonable way to build a class-balanced corpus (the stated goal, since raw Wikipedia entity distributions are highly skewed), but it also means FEW-NERD's sentences are systematically pre-filtered toward canonical, keyword-triggered entity mentions rather than a random cross-section of how entities actually appear in text — and the paper reports no random-sample control to bound how much that skews measured difficulty.

FEW-NERD (INTRA), the paper's hardest and most-cited setting, rests on one fixed partition. The coarse-type-to-split assignment (Person/Miscellaneous/Art/Product → train, Event/Building → dev, Organization/Location → test) is a single choice made by the authors, not resampled or cross-validated across alternate assignments. Because the type-similarity heat map that motivates the design (Figure 2) shows uneven correlation structure across coarse types, a different assignment could plausibly move the headline numbers by more than the gap between models — and only the one split is reported.

baselines

Fair like-for-like: BERT-Tagger, ProtoBERT, NNShot, and StructShot all share the same BERTbase encoder, and NNShot/StructShot were the contemporaneous few-shot NER SOTA (Yang and Katiyar, 2020).

contamination

Not classic train/test leakage, but a selection-bias analogue: candidate paragraphs are found via a distant-dictionary/Lucene search over FIGER's automatically-labeled mentions, so every sentence was pre-filtered toward containing a plausible entity keyword before a human ever saw it — no random-sample control is reported to bound the effect.

what's missed

No human-performance baseline is run through the same evaluation harness; the sole label-quality signal is one aggregate Cohen's Kappa (76.44%), not broken out by type, so headline F1 gaps can't be split into model weakness vs. irreducible annotation noise.

would raise confidence

A published changelog explaining the silent v1→v6 re-run of FEW-NERD's own results; a human-performance number on at least one setting; and a robustness check on FEW-NERD (INTRA)'s single fixed coarse-type-to-split assignment.

// bottom line

FEW-NERD is a careful, genuine advance over recycled coarse-type NER splits, and its central empirical claim — that fine-grained few-shot NER is hard, with even a fully-supervised BERT-Tagger dropping over 20 F1 points relative to CoNLL'03/OntoNotes and the strongest few-shot method topping out at 58.79 F1 in its easiest setting — holds up under its own numbers. The open questions are less about internal consistency and more about how much weight any single number can bear: which arXiv version of the results you're looking at, how much of the model-to-supervised gap is annotation noise rather than model failure, and how much of INTRA's reported difficulty rides on one particular choice of which coarse types to hold out.

// takeaways

What to remember

  1. FEW-NERD replaces the 4–18-type schemas of CoNLL'03, WNUT'17, and OntoNotes with an 8-coarse/66-fine hierarchy over 188,238 human-annotated Wikipedia sentences — the first NER dataset purpose-built (not repurposed) for few-shot learning.
  2. Fine granularity alone crushes performance: an identical BERT-Tagger goes from 91.34 F1 (CoNLL'03) and 89.11 (OntoNotes 5.0) to 67.13 on FEW-NERD (SUP), and the best few-shot model tops out at 58.79 F1 in its easiest setting (INTER, 5-way 5∼10-shot).
  3. Splitting by coarse type (INTRA) is harder than splitting within it (INTER), for every model at every shot count — direct evidence that fine-grained types sharing a coarse parent transfer knowledge to each other, and that a benchmark's split design determines difficulty as much as its label count does.
  4. Every FEW-NERD-specific result — the SUP row, all three few-shot tables, and the per-coarse-type F1 breakdown — was silently re-run between arXiv v1 (May 2021) and v6 (September 2021) with no erratum, while the borrowed CoNLL'03/OntoNotes reference rows stayed byte-identical — a caution for anyone still citing a Few-NERD baseline number from an older paper without checking which version it came from.
