A Comprehensive Survey of Hallucination Mitigation Techniques in Large Language Models

Tonmoy, Zaman, Jain et al. sort 32 named hallucination-mitigation techniques into two branches — prompt-time interventions (retrieval augmentation, self-refinement through feedback, prompt tuning) and model-development interventions (new decoding strategies, knowledge-graph grounding, faithfulness-based losses, supervised fine-tuning) — and catalog each one's task, metric, evaluated model and dataset in a 32-row summary table, alongside the survey's own taxonomy placement and limitation summary. The survey runs no experiment of its own: every number in that table is copied from the technique's own source paper. Worth flagging on its own: the staged v3 quietly drops six numeric values — three of them decimal results — that the v1 preprint reported, and replaces none of them.

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

By late 2023, the response to LLM hallucination had gone from a handful of techniques to a sprawl: retrieval-augmented pipelines, self-critique loops, contrastive decoding tricks, knowledge-graph groundings, faithfulness-aware losses, refusal-aware fine-tuning — each published as its own paper, on its own task, with its own metric and its own baselines, with no single place to see the field as a whole. This survey's stated goal is to consolidate that sprawl: "This paper assumes significance by consolidating and organizing these diverse techniques into a comprehensive taxonomy." Its three stated contributions, quoted directly from the introduction, are (1) "Introduction of a systematic taxonomy designed to categorize hallucination mitigation techniques for LLMs, encompassing Vision Language Models (VLMs)," (2) "Synthesis of the essential features characterizing these mitigation techniques, thereby guiding more structured future research endeavors within this domain," and (3) "Deliberation on the limitations and challenges inherent in these techniques, accompanied by potential solutions and proposed directions for future research."

Notably, the paper is not itself proposing a definition or taxonomy of hallucination as a phenomenon — it inherits one. Its characterizations of the failure are brief and repeated rather than singular — one gloss in the abstract ("generating content that appears factual but is ungrounded"), and two more in the introduction: the opening definition ("Hallucination in Large Language Models (LLMs) entails the creation of factually erroneous information spanning a multitude of subjects") and a second restatement a few sentences later in the same opening paragraph ("a fundamental issue with LLMs is their propensity to yield erroneous or fabricated details about real-world subjects... commonly referred to as hallucination") — but none of the three adds up to a taxonomy of hallucination types, and the paper's citation for the claim that hallucination is "arguably the biggest hindrance to safely deploying" LLMs goes to (Jain, 2023) — a blog post on Distilled AI written by one of this survey's own co-authors, Vinija Jain, rather than a peer-reviewed source. The paper's organizing work is entirely about the responses to hallucination, not the phenomenon itself; see the "how it works" section below for the taxonomy this produces, and the critique section for what that scope decision costs.

The taxonomy

// version note

arXiv 2401.01313 exists in at least three versions; this page is built from the staged v3 (arXiv stamp "8 Jan 2024," 19 pages). A numeric-token diff against v1 (20 pages) shows v3 is a strict subset: v1 contains 53 numeric tokens, v3 contains 47, and all 47 of v3's are also in v1 — meaning v3 removed six numeric values and added none. Three of the six removed tokens are decimal figures that read like withdrawn results; the other three are integers that no longer appear anywhere in the staged text. None of the six removed values are reproduced anywhere on this page — deliberately, since a secondary source citing one of them against this paper would be citing content the authors themselves withdrew, and repeating the digits here would only propagate them further. Because so few numeric tokens exist in the paper at all — 47, and the paper contains no equations, so nearly all of them are section numbers, reference page ranges (e.g. "16477–16508"), arXiv-id fragments inside bibliography entries, and model-size labels (7B, 13B), plus the one body-text result quoted below — this page necessarily carries very few numbers; that is a property of the source, not a shortcut taken here.

The taxonomy (Figure 1 below, and Table 1's 32-row catalog) splits into two top-level branches. Prompt Engineering (§2.1) covers techniques that intervene without changing model weights: Retrieval-Augmented Generation (§2.1.1), itself split by when retrieval happens relative to generation — before (LLM-Augmenter, FreshPrompt), during (Knowledge Retrieval, the Decompose-and-Query framework D&Q, EVER), after (RARR, High Entropy Word Spotting and Replacement), and end-to-end (the original RAG architecture itself) — followed by Self-Refinement through Feedback and Reasoning (§2.1.2), the taxonomy's single largest leaf at nine techniques (Prompting GPT-3 To Be Reliable, ChatProtect, Self-Reflection Methodology, Structured Comparative reasoning, Mind's Mirror, DRESS, MixAlign, CoVe, CoNLI), and Prompt Tuning (§2.1.3) (UPRISE, SynTra). Developing Models (§3) covers techniques that change the model itself: new decoding strategies (§3.1: CAD, DoLa, Inference-Time Intervention), knowledge-graph utilization (§3.2: RHO, FLEEK), faithfulness-based loss functions (§3.3: THAM, the Loss Weighting Method), and supervised fine-tuning (§3.4: Knowledge Injection/Teacher-Student, HAR, Fine-tuning Language Models for Factuality, BEINFO, R-Tuning, TWEAK).

Table 1 (spanning roughly a third of the paper) gives each of the 32 techniques a row with nine columns: Category (which takes only two values in the whole table — "Prompt Engineering" or "Developing Models"), Mitigation Technique(s) (the finer branch label, e.g. "Retrieval Augmented Generation (During Generation)"), Title, Detection (the caption's own gloss: "✓ indicates that it is present in the paper whereas × indicates it is not present" — i.e., whether the cited technique's own paper also builds in a hallucination-detection step, not whether this survey detected or verified anything), Task(s), Metric(s), Evaluated LLM(s), Dataset(s), and Limitation(s). Of those nine, four are the survey's own work rather than a copy of the source paper: Category and Mitigation Technique(s) are the taxonomy assignment itself (the paper's first stated contribution), Detection ✓/× is the survey's own reading of the source, and Limitation(s) is the survey's own summary (its third stated contribution) — which is why 11 of the 32 rows read "Details not provided" rather than something copied verbatim. Title, Task(s), Metric(s), Evaluated LLM(s), and Dataset(s) are the five fields actually drawn from each technique's own paper — Title being simply the source paper's own title. For example, the D&Q row's Category is "Prompt Engineering," its Mitigation Technique(s) is "Retrieval Augmented Generation (During Generation)," its Task column reads "QA," its Metric column "Recall and F1 score," and its Limitation(s) column reads verbatim "Details not provided"; the body text for the same technique is more informative, reporting that D&Q shows "competitive performance against GPT-3.5 on ChitChatQA and achiev[es] a noteworthy 59.6% F1 score on HotPotQA (question-only)" — the one numeric result quoted anywhere on this page, because it is one of only 47 numeric tokens in the whole v3 text.

// what they found

What the catalog documents

Because the survey runs no experiment, its "results" are the shape of the catalog itself, plus what the catalog's Detection column reveals about the field it's cataloging. Of Table 1's 32 rows, 24 mark Detection ✓ (the cited technique's own paper couples mitigation to an explicit detection step) and 8 mark × (no detection component reported) — the 8 marked × are LLM-Augmenter, the Decompose-and-Query framework, the original end-to-end RAG architecture, CAD, RHO, THAM, HAR, and R-Tuning. The survey doesn't discuss this split anywhere in its prose; it appears only as the table column.

The Limitation(s) column reads "Details not provided" on 11 of the 32 rows (including D&Q, the end-to-end RAG entry, ChatProtect, CAD, HAR, and R-Tuning) — the survey's own honest flag that a cited paper didn't state its limitations explicitly, rather than an invented placeholder.

Section 5 ("Discussion and Limitations") closes by pointing at future work rather than a synthesis finding: hybrid models "integrating numerous mitigation approaches," unsupervised/weakly-supervised techniques to reduce reliance on labeled data, and attention to the "moral ramifications and societal effects" of mitigation strategies. No comparative claim (which family works best, which is cheapest, which generalizes) is made anywhere in the paper — consistent with a catalog whose 32 rows share no common metric to compare across.

// does it hold up?

The eval read

Like most catalog surveys, this one isn't graded on baselines or contamination in the usual sense — there's no experiment to audit. The right questions are whether the taxonomy is exhaustive or illustrative (and whether the paper says which), whether it evaluates what it catalogs, what families it leaves out, and whether it earns its own framing. On each, the paper's own text and its own table supply the evidence.

Illustrative, not exhaustive — and the paper never says which. Nowhere does the survey state a search protocol, a date cutoff, or inclusion criteria for the 32 techniques it selected; "comprehensive" appears in the title, but the paper offers no accounting of what a comprehensive sweep of this literature would have needed to cover versus what these 32 happen to be. Readers are left to trust the authors' scope judgment rather than reproduce it — unremarkable for a narrative (as opposed to systematic) survey, but worth naming since the title's own word choice implies otherwise.

Describes, doesn't evaluate. Every Task/Metric/Model/Dataset cell in Table 1 is the cited technique's own self-reported result on its own benchmark — BLEU-4 here, FactScore there, HVI somewhere else, none of it commensurable across rows. The one number this survey does quote comparatively in prose — D&Q's 59.6% F1 on HotPotQA (question-only) — is itself borrowed directly from D&Q's own paper, not something this survey computed or verified. A reader cannot use this page, or the paper it summarizes, to determine which of the 32 techniques most reduces hallucination, under any shared setup; the catalog indexes the literature, it does not rank it.

The taxonomy’s claimed VLM coverage oversells the taxonomy's actual coverage. The introduction's first stated contribution promises a taxonomy "encompassing Vision Language Models (VLMs)." Of the 32 cataloged techniques, only one — DRESS (Chen et al., 2023), evaluated on LVLMs including BLIP-2, LLaVA, InstructBLIP, MiniGPT-4, and mPLUG-Owl — is actually evaluated on vision-language models; it sits folded into the Self-Refinement leaf in Figure 1, indistinguishable in the tree from the text-only techniques around it. A second entry is multimodal in a narrower sense: THAM (Yoon et al., 2022, filed under §3.3's faithfulness-based loss functions) targets "Video-grounded Dialogue," with Table 1 giving its task as "Video-grounded Dialogues" and its datasets as AVSD@DSTC7/8 — so Section 3 (Developing Models) is not, as such, silent on multimodal work. But THAM carries no VLM framing, is never connected to DRESS or to the introduction's VLM claim, and is evaluated on video-and-text dialogue rather than image-and-text vision-language tasks. So the taxonomy has exactly one LVLM technique and one unrelated video-grounded entry, with the other 30 purely textual — "encompassing" is still a real overstatement of the taxonomy's actual multimodal reach, just not for the reason a bare column count would suggest.

A major mitigation family is folded away rather than named. RLHF never appears as a term anywhere in the paper, and preference optimization is never named as a mitigation family or taxonomy branch — it surfaces exactly once, buried inside one technique's method description: one of the survey's own cataloged techniques, Tian et al.'s "Fine-tuning Language Models for Factuality," is described in the survey's own text as "employing automated fact-checking methods and preference-based learning through the Direct Preference Optimization algorithm." That technique is filed under the generic "Supervised finetuning" branch alongside knowledge-injection and refusal-tuning methods, with no acknowledgment that preference optimization is a mechanically distinct family from ordinary supervised fine-tuning, and no discussion of RLHF-based mitigation anywhere — a real gap for a survey covering the same window (2022–2023) in which RLHF became the dominant production alignment technique.

baselines

Not applicable in the direct sense — there's no experiment here. The closer analogue: Table 1's 32 rows use 32 different metric/dataset pairings drawn from 32 different source papers, with no shared benchmark, so nothing in the table supports ranking the techniques against each other.

contamination

Not addressed, and not the survey's own claim to make — but it also never flags contamination risk in any of the 32 techniques' self-reported benchmarks it reproduces uncritically, several of which (TruthfulQA, HotPotQA, TriviaQA) are widely used enough to raise the question.

what's missed

A stated inclusion protocol; a taxonomy that actually reflects the claimed VLM coverage (1 LVLM technique, DRESS, plus 1 unrelated video-grounded entry, THAM, out of 32); RLHF/preference-optimization named as its own mitigation family rather than absorbed into "Supervised finetuning" without comment.

would raise confidence

An explicit statement of how the 32 techniques were selected and what was excluded; separating Detection ✓/× into a discussed finding rather than an unremarked table column; reconciling the introduction's VLM claim with the taxonomy's actual multimodal coverage (one LVLM technique and one unconnected video-grounded entry among 32).

// bottom line

As a single-stop index of 32 named hallucination-mitigation techniques circa late 2023 — each traceable back to its source paper's own task, metric, model, and dataset, plus the survey's own taxonomy placement and limitation summary — this is a genuinely useful bibliography with structure. But every comparative judgment in it is deferred to the 32 source papers it summarizes; nothing has been run against a shared benchmark, the taxonomy's claimed VLM coverage amounts to one LVLM technique (DRESS) and one unconnected video-grounded entry (THAM), and a real mitigation family (RLHF/preference optimization) goes unnamed even though the survey's own text describes one of its 32 entries using it. Read it as a map of the literature, not a verdict on which technique to use.

// takeaways

What to remember

  1. The taxonomy sorts exactly 32 named techniques into two branches — Prompt Engineering (RAG's four generation-stage variants, self-refinement/feedback, prompt tuning) and Developing Models (decoding strategies, knowledge-graph grounding, faithfulness-based losses, supervised fine-tuning) — with Self-Refinement through Feedback and Reasoning the single largest leaf at 9 of the 32.
  2. Nothing on this page, or in the paper, is the survey's own experiment: the Task/Metric/Evaluated-LLM/Dataset cells in the 32-row catalog are drawn from each technique's own source paper, while Category, Mitigation Technique(s), Detection, and Limitation(s) are the survey's own taxonomy and summary judgment — so the catalog can index the field and describe it, but can't rank it. The one number quoted in prose (D&Q's 59.6% F1 on HotPotQA question-only) is itself borrowed from D&Q's paper.
  3. The staged v3 quietly removed six numeric values that v1 reported (three of them decimal results) and replaced none — a caveat for any secondary source still citing v1's now-withdrawn numbers. Separately, despite the introduction's claim of a taxonomy "encompassing Vision Language Models," only 1 of the 32 cataloged techniques (DRESS) is actually evaluated on VLMs; the taxonomy's only other multimodal entry (THAM, a video-grounded dialogue technique) carries no VLM framing.
