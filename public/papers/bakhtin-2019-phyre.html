PHYRE: A New Benchmark for Physical Reasoning

PHYRE turns intuitive physics into 5,000 single-action puzzles across two action tiers, scored by AUCCESS -- a log-weighted area under the success-vs-attempts curve. The best 2019 baseline, an online-updating deep Q-network, reaches only 56.2% AUCCESS on puzzle templates it never trained on (39.6% once a second ball must be placed), versus 77.6%/67.8% on templates it has already seen.

What problem is this solving?

Reasoning about physics -- gravity, momentum, friction, what will happen if you nudge this object -- is a hallmark of intelligence that shows up in how human children and even non-human animals (capuchin monkeys, chimpanzees, crows) solve novel physical puzzles. By 2019, machine-learning work on "intuitive physics" mostly tested something narrower than that: models were trained to predict whether a stack of blocks would topple, or to judge whether a video of a scene was physically plausible -- passive perception tasks, not intervention. A separate line of visual-reasoning benchmarks (VQA-style datasets, CLEVR) required multi-object reasoning but assumed a static world with no dynamics at all. And the RL/robotics environments that did require physical understanding (autonomous-driving simulators, MuJoCo-based humanoid control, embodied navigation) ran in realistic, non-deterministic, partially observed 3D worlds -- rich, but too uncontrolled to systematically probe reasoning ability or generalization.

PHYRE's authors wanted a benchmark that isolates two things at once: (1) genuine physical intervention -- an agent must act on the world (place a new object) to cause a goal state, not just predict or classify -- and (2) sample-efficient generalization to puzzles the agent has never seen, evaluated inside a fully deterministic, symbolically specified 2D environment simple enough to analyze systematically. No existing benchmark combined the goal-driven intervention setup, the deterministic simulator, and an explicit scoring rule that rewards solving new puzzles in few attempts rather than many.

// how it works

The method

PHYRE is a 2D physics simulator with constant downward gravity and a small amount of friction. Every scene is built from a small vocabulary of rigid bodies -- balls, bars, standing sticks, and jars -- that are either static (fixed, unaffected by gravity/collision) or dynamic (subject to gravity and collision), distinguished by color. A task pairs an initial world state with a goal expressed as a (subject, relation, object) triplet; the current release uses a single relation, "touching for at least 3 seconds." The agent's move is a single action: place one or more new dynamic bodies into the scene (an action that overlaps existing bodies or leaves the world boundary is rejected as invalid). The simulator then runs to completion or a time limit, and the agent receives a binary reward for whether the goal was met, plus the intermediate world states as raster observations (a 256×256 grid where each cell takes one of seven values encoding goal roles, confounding bodies, agent-placed bodies, or background). If the attempt fails, the task resets and the agent tries again -- so the setting is a contextual bandit with an offline training phase (unlimited simulator access, no direct access to solutions) followed by an online testing phase where the agent gets up to 100 attempts per task, informed by what it observed on earlier attempts.

The released benchmark defines two action tiers of increasing difficulty: PHYRE-B, where the action is the placement of a single ball (a continuous 3D action: x, y, radius), and PHYRE-2B, where the action is the placement of a pair of balls (continuous 6D). Each tier has 25 task templates, and every template generates 100 tasks by varying parameters such as body positions -- 2,500 tasks per tier, 5,000 tasks total. Tasks in the same template share a goal but differ in their initial world state (Figure 2). Templates support two ways of testing generalization: in the within-template setting, an agent trains on a subset of a template's 100 tasks and is tested on the rest of that same template; in the cross-template setting, test tasks come exclusively from templates the agent never trained on. All splits are deterministic and pre-defined across 10 folds, so different agents are always compared on identical train/validation/test partitions.

Performance is judged not just by whether an agent eventually solves a task, but by how few attempts it needs. The paper records the success-percentage curve -- cumulative percentage of test tasks solved as a function of attempts taken -- and reduces it to a single number, AUCCESS, a weighted average that emphasizes solving with fewer attempts:

AUCCESS = Σk wk · skΣk wk ,   wk = log(k+1) − log(k),   k ∈ {1,…,100} // s_k = the success percentage at k attempts; AUCCESS is equivalent to the area under the success curve when attempts are plotted on a log axis

Because the weights are logarithmic in the attempt count, the first 10 of the 100 allowed attempts already carry roughly half the total weight -- so an agent that needs more than 10 attempts per task cannot score above 50% AUCCESS, which is the point: PHYRE is explicitly designed to reward sample efficiency, not eventual brute-force success.

The paper benchmarks five agents that rank candidate actions given the initial observation: a random agent (RAND); a non-parametric memorization agent (MEM), which samples a fixed pool of actions during training, scores each by the fraction of training tasks it solves, and replays that ranking at test time (a single shared ranking in the cross-template setting, since unseen templates give it nothing task-specific to condition on; a separate per-template ranking, over the same action pool, in the within-template setting); an online variant (MEM-O) that keeps updating those per-action success-rate estimates using the rewards it receives on test tasks; a deep Q-network (DQN), which fuses a ResNet-18 image encoder with an MLP action encoder (FiLM-style channel-wise bias/gain modulation applied just before the ResNet's last residual block) and is trained with a class-balanced cross-entropy loss to predict success probability for an (observation, action) pair; and an online DQN variant (DQN-O) that keeps fine-tuning on test-task feedback. At test time every non-random agent ranks a shared pool of 10,000 candidate actions per task (MEM/MEM-O were trained on that same 10,000-action pool; DQN/DQN-O were trained on 100,000 actions per task) and is capped at 100 solution attempts per task. The paper also explains why three families of alternatives aren't scored baselines: linear and Gaussian-process contextual bandits, which the authors argue are unsuited to the setting in principle (no useful linear representation of raw observation images; no obvious kernel over that image space); a deep-network contextual bandit adapted from prior published code, which they report being unable to train once its shallow MLP was swapped for a CNN image encoder; and two policy-gradient learners, PPO and A2C, which did train but which the authors could not get to outperform the random baseline, citing poor training stability.

// what they found

The results

Before scoring any learned agent, the paper characterizes how hard the puzzles are for a random agent: it designed both tiers so that, on average, a random agent needs no more than 10,000 attempts to solve a PHYRE-B task and no more than 100,000 attempts for a PHYRE-2B task. That average hides a wide per-template spread (Figure 3b): the plotted per-template mean probability that a single random attempt solves a task ranges from roughly 4×10-4 up to roughly 7×10-2 across PHYRE-B's 25 templates, and from roughly 10-5 up to roughly 6×10-3 across PHYRE-2B's -- so a handful of templates are nearly trivial by chance while, in the paper's words, "other tasks require thousands of attempts to be solved." Per Figure 3a, at roughly 1,000 attempts a random agent has already solved about 80% of PHYRE-B tasks but only about 40% of PHYRE-2B tasks -- the same budget buys roughly twice the coverage on the easier tier.

The headline comparison is the mean AUCCESS (over the 10 folds) of the five agents, on both tiers, in both generalization settings. In the cross-template setting -- the harder, more meaningful test of generalization to genuinely new puzzles -- the best agent is DQN-O, the online-updating deep Q-network, which reaches 56.2%±10.5 AUCCESS on PHYRE-B but only 39.6%±11.1 on PHYRE-2B; both are the statistically best score in their column (one-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test across folds, p=0.01). In the within-template setting -- test tasks drawn from templates already seen in training -- the (offline) DQN reaches 77.6%±1.1 on PHYRE-B and 67.8%±1.5 on PHYRE-2B, again the best in its column. (MEM-O and DQN-O are not reported separately for within-template because cross-validation selects zero online updates for both agents in that setting -- for DQN-O, online updates actually impede performance there, see below -- which makes both agents identical to MEM and DQN in that column.) The paper reports two overall patterns (Section 4.3): within-template generalization is much easier than cross-template generalization for all non-random agents -- MEM is a documented exception on PHYRE-B, where its within-template score (2.4%) is lower than its cross-template score (18.5%), discussed further below -- and every agent's cross-template score falls moving from PHYRE-B to PHYRE-2B (for DQN-O, 56.2% drops to 39.6%); within-template scores fall too for RAND and DQN, but MEM's within-template score instead rises slightly (2.4%→3.2%).

The random agent (RAND) scores 13.0%±5.0 (B, cross) and 2.6%±1.5 (2B, cross) AUCCESS -- essentially the same whether or not the test templates were seen in training, as expected for an agent that ignores the observation entirely. The memorization agent (MEM) does better than random in the cross-template setting on PHYRE-B (18.5%±5.1) but drops sharply in the within-template setting there (2.4%±0.3) -- lower than in the harder-sounding cross-template setting, an inversion the paper's table shows without further comment. On PHYRE-2B the same two settings are close enough to be within noise of one another (3.7%±2.3 cross vs. 3.2%±0.2 within), so the inversion is clearly established only on PHYRE-B. The paper additionally reports success at a fixed 10-attempt budget (a different metric from AUCCESS, since it is a single point on the curve rather than a weighted average over the whole 1-100 range): DQN-O again leads cross-template, at 58.2%±10.9 (B) and 41.6%±11.7 (2B) of tasks solved within 10 attempts. A separate analysis (varying how many candidate actions each agent ranks at test time) shows that an oracle "OPTIMAL" ranker -- given perfect knowledge of which actions solve which tasks -- can solve all of PHYRE-B and 95% of PHYRE-2B by ranking fewer than 100,000 attempts, which the authors read as evidence that ranking actions is a viable strategy at all, not as a justification for any particular budget: the real agents' test-time budget is a fixed 10,000-action pool for every non-random agent (see "how it works" above), and 100,000 is instead how many actions the DQN(-O) agents saw during training.

// does it hold up?

The eval read

PHYRE's engineering is genuinely careful: a deterministic simulator, fixed 10-fold splits released for reproducibility, a scalar metric (AUCCESS) with a clean axiomatic motivation (log-weighting over a bounded attempt budget), and an explicit statistical test (one-sided Wilcoxon, p=0.01) rather than eyeballing gaps between means. The paper is also unusually candid about the limits of its own baselines: it argues linear and Gaussian-process contextual bandits aren't suited to its image-based action space, reports that its deep contextual-bandit implementation failed to train at all, and reports that PPO and A2C trained but never beat the random baseline -- and it explicitly flags and discourages the obvious ways an agent could game the setup (hard-coding PHYRE-specific rules, or calling the real simulator internally to brute-force a solution before submitting an attempt). That said, a few things are worth flagging for anyone using the headline AUCCESS numbers as a yardstick for "physical reasoning."

baselines

The five scored agents span only two real model families: non-parametric memorization (MEM/MEM-O) and one CNN-based value network (DQN/DQN-O). No object-centric or physics-simulation-aware model is benchmarked here, even though the paper's own related-work section discusses several such models (Janner et al.'s object-oriented prediction-and-planning network, and simulation-conjoined predictors like Galileo and visual de-animation) as the kind of method PHYRE is meant to push toward. The comparison set the launch paper actually reports is therefore narrower than the design space it surveys -- it establishes that generic value-based RL clearly beats memorization and random guessing, not that any 2019-era method is close to solving cross-template generalization.

contamination

Not the pretraining-leakage sense (this is a procedurally generated, deterministic physics simulator, not text/image corpora) -- but there is a real information-leakage subtlety in how the success-vs-attempts curve is computed for the online agents. The paper states plainly that all agents get up to 100 attempts per task, and that "when computing the success percentage at k<100 attempts, online agents will have learned from up to 100 (not k) attempts per task" -- a deliberate simplification the authors call "pragmatic." But the leak this creates runs across test tasks, not within one: MEM-O's ranking update and DQN-O's gradient update are both applied "after finishing a test task" and used "when the next [test] task is attempted." So by the time an online agent is scored on, say, the 5th test task in a fold, its ranking or weights have already absorbed the complete 100-attempt outcome of every earlier test task in that fold -- not a leak from later attempts on the very task being scored, but still more information than a genuinely task-by-task sequential deployment would have accumulated by that point. The reported early-curve (small-k) sample-efficiency of the online agents is therefore optimistic relative to that stricter sequential setting.

what's missed

AUCCESS is reported only as a single aggregate over each tier's 25 templates x 100 tasks; the paper never publishes a per-template AUCCESS breakdown for the learned agents (only the random baseline gets a per-template breakdown, Figure 3b, which itself shows a several-orders-of-magnitude spread in template difficulty). A 56.2% aggregate score is consistent with either broadly-competent-but-imperfect performance or with solving a handful of easy templates well and failing hard templates outright -- the released numbers can't distinguish these, and the paper doesn't say which it is.

would raise confidence

Three things: (1) a human or animal AUCCESS baseline on the same fold splits -- the paper spends its whole introduction comparing physical reasoning across humans, capuchins, chimps, and crows, but never measures a human on PHYRE's own tasks, so there's still no human ceiling to compare 56.2% against (the paper's OPTIMAL oracle is an upper bound on ranking-based solving, not a substitute for one); (2) the per-template score breakdown described above; (3) an actual forward-model or object-centric planning baseline, which is the exact extension the paper's own discussion section calls for as future work rather than testing itself.

// bottom line

PHYRE is a well-built, reproducible benchmark whose scoring rule (AUCCESS) is a defensible way to reward sample-efficient generalization -- but the launch paper's own baselines only clear the low bar of "better than memorization and random guessing." The 56.2%/39.6% cross-template numbers are a floor to beat, not evidence that any tested method understands physics; readers should treat the generalization gap (56.2% cross vs. 77.6% within, same DQN architecture family) as the paper's real finding, not the absolute AUCCESS values themselves.

// takeaways

What to remember

  1. PHYRE reduces physical reasoning to a single-action, contextual-bandit puzzle game: place one ball (PHYRE-B) or two (PHYRE-2B) to satisfy a touching goal, across 5,000 tasks (2 tiers × 25 templates × 100 tasks), scored by AUCCESS -- a log-weighted area under the success-vs-attempts curve that puts roughly half its weight on the first 10 of 100 allowed attempts.
  2. The cross-template generalization gap is the paper's central finding: the best agent (DQN-O, updating online on test feedback) reaches only 56.2%±10.5 AUCCESS on PHYRE-B and 39.6%±11.1 on PHYRE-2B when tested on template families never seen in training -- versus 77.6%±1.1 and 67.8%±1.5 for the same DQN architecture when the test tasks come from templates it trained on.
  3. Adding a second ball to place (PHYRE-2B vs. PHYRE-B) cuts the best cross-template AUCCESS by roughly 30% (56.2% → 39.6%), showing the benchmark's built-in difficulty knob works even though the physics and object vocabulary barely change between tiers.
