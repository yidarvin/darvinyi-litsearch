CRUD-RAG: A Comprehensive Chinese Benchmark for Retrieval-Augmented Generation of Large Language Models

Borrows the CRUD acronym from database interfaces — Create, Read, Update, Delete — to argue that "read" (question answering) is only one of the ways an LLM actually touches a knowledge base, then builds a 36,166-instance Chinese benchmark across all four and ablates the whole RAG pipeline (chunk size, chunk overlap, embedding model, retriever, top-k, LLM) rather than just the generator. The headline result is a negative one, repeated at every ablation: no chunk size, retriever, embedding model or LLM wins across all four scenarios — Hybrid+Rerank retrieval takes 14 of the 15 scored cells across the three question-answering tasks (16 of 30 across the full 6-task grid; BM25 edges it on the one QA cell it misses), but BM25 sweeps every overall-similarity metric on hallucination correction instead.

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

By the time CRUD-RAG was written, the standard way to evaluate a retrieval-augmented generation system was already narrow in two compounding ways. First, nearly every reference-required benchmark — RGB, Natural Questions — is a question-answering benchmark: it hands the system a query, checks the answer against a reference, and calls that "RAG evaluation." But question answering is only one thing people build RAG systems to do. Feeding an LLM external context to draft new text, to correct a factual error in a draft, or to condense many sources into one summary are all just as common, and an optimization that helps QA (say, a large top-k for recall) has no guarantee of helping any of the others. Second, even within QA-style evaluation, the field had split into two evaluation styles that each throw away half the pipeline: reference-free frameworks like RAGAS and ARES check only whether the generated text is internally consistent with whatever the retriever happened to return — a criterion that says nothing when the retrieved content itself is wrong or irrelevant — while reference-required frameworks that do check against ground truth typically evaluate only the LLM's generation quality, or only the retriever's accuracy on a knowledge-intensive task, never both together with the surrounding pipeline (chunk size, chunk overlap, embedding model) that determines what the LLM actually sees.

CRUD-RAG's response is to import a framing from database systems — the four canonical operations on a data store, Create, Read, Update, Delete — and treat them as four qualitatively different ways an LLM can interact with an external knowledge base: generating original content from retrieved context ("Create"), answering knowledge-intensive questions ("Read"), correcting inaccuracies in existing text using retrieved evidence ("Update"), and condensing multiple retrieved documents into one summary ("Delete"). Each gets its own dataset and its own evaluation, and every experiment sweeps the retriever, the embedding model, the chunk size and overlap, and the LLM together, rather than fixing three of the four and varying only the generator.

// how it works

The method

The four CRUD categories map to five concrete tasks, each with its own dataset built from a corpus of Chinese news published after July 2023 (to minimize the chance any evaluated LLM had already seen it during pretraining): roughly 300,000 articles were crawled and filtered down to over 80,000, which — after removing the 10,000 seed articles used to build the summarization/continuation datasets and adding back the articles the construction pipeline retrieved from Baidu — becomes the 86,834-document shared retrieval database. Create is text continuation (10,728 instances): given the opening of a real article, generate a plausible continuation. Read is question answering, split by how many documents the answer requires — single-document (3,199), two-document (3,192), and three-document (3,189) — the multi-document sets specifically testing reasoning across sources rather than single-passage extraction. Update is hallucination modification (5,130 instances), built by reusing UHGEval's factual errors — a model generates the flawed continuations and GPT-4 annotates and marks which segments are hallucinated — planted in real news continuations, and asking the RAG system to retrieve the true story and correct the error. Delete is open-domain multi-document summarization (10,728 instances).

Two of the five datasets — text continuation and summarization — are built "in reverse," a technique adapted from prior multi-document summarization work: rather than writing a summary from existing related articles, the pipeline starts from one seed article, has GPT-4 extract its key events and a summary, then searches Baidu for roughly ten more articles connected to those events (filtering out near-duplicates of the seed) to serve as the multi-document context the system must retrieve from and reconstruct the summary or continuation out of. Multi-document QA questions are built through a five-step chain-of-thought pipeline: retrieve two or three connected news articles, have GPT-4 identify their shared "contact point," identify how the articles differ, generate a question per focus point, then reconstruct those into one logically-linked question rather than two questions stapled together. Three native-Chinese annotators with at least a bachelor's degree then reviewed the queries in one-fifth of the dataset — the paper's stated manual-annotation budget, not the full set — refining any that weren't "reasonable"; 5.8% of that reviewed fifth needed refinement.

To score a RAG system's output, CRUD-RAG needs a metric that goes beyond word-overlap (bleu, rouge-L) and embedding similarity (bertScore), which don't isolate whether the specific factual content of the reference made it into the generation. The paper adapts QuestEval — originally a reference-free summarization metric that checks consistency between generated text and its source document — into RAGQuestEval, which instead checks consistency between the generated text and the ground-truth reference (since in a RAG setting, the retrieved source may itself be wrong, so matching it isn't a valid target). A question generator extracts entities and noun phrases from the ground-truth reference to produce a set of questions; a question answerer then answers each question using both the reference and the model's generated text.

// what they found

The results

Across every ablation, the pattern is the same: whichever component is being swept, the "best" setting flips depending on which of the four CRUD scenarios is being scored. Chunk size (64→512, with top-k adjusted to hold the token budget near-constant) helps text continuation and multi-document QA — larger chunks preserve article structure, which both creative generation and cross-document reasoning depend on — but hurts single-document QA, where a smaller, more targeted chunk makes it easier to extract one specific fact. For summarization, larger chunks raise RAGQuestEval recall (more key information retrieved) while lowering its precision (harder to select only the relevant part of a larger block) — a real precision/recall trade-off, not a uniform improvement.

Retriever choice (Table 5) shows the clearest scenario split. Sweeping BM25, Dense, Hybrid and Hybrid+Rerank across the 6 tasks and 5 scored metrics (bleu, rouge-L, bertScore, RAGQuestEval-precision, RAGQuestEval-recall — 30 cells total), Hybrid+Rerank takes 16 of 30: 14 of the 15 cells across the three question-answering tasks — the exception is 2-document QA's bleu, where BM25 edges it 24.61 to 24.53, and the paper's own bold-face marks BM25 as the winner there — plus rouge-L and recall on summarization. It does not dominate the other two scenarios — BM25 takes 7 of 30 in total (2-document QA bleu, plus bleu/rouge-L/bertScore on hallucination modification and bleu/precision on summarization, plus precision on text continuation), and Dense/Hybrid split the remaining 7. A separate MRR-based retrieval-only evaluation (Figure 7) reaches the same ranking as the end-to-end RAGQuestEval scores, which the paper reads as cross-validating its own evaluation method.

The embedding model sweep (m3e-base, bge-base, stella-base, gte-base) shows the same lack of a uniform winner across tasks — m3e-base on hallucination modification, where it tops all five scored columns, is the only task block any embedding model sweeps outright. Choosing among them, the paper notes, can't be read directly off a general-purpose leaderboard like MTEB, since "the actual performance of the RAG system may differ from the leaderboard."

The LLM comparison (Table 8, 8 models: ChatGLM2-6B, Qwen-7B, Baichuan2-13B, Qwen-14B, GPT-3.5-turbo, GPT-4-0613, plus Qwen2-7B and GPT-4o added in the paper's July-2024 revision) is the flagship table, and it is also not a clean sweep for GPT-4: GPT-4-0613 and GPT-4o between them top most of the 30 scored cells, but GPT-3.5-turbo alone posts the single highest bleu, rouge-L and bertScore on single-document QA, and Baichuan2-13B posts the highest recall on that same task (76.92). The paper's own closing recommendation (Table 9) leans on this: because GPT-4 is expensive to call at scale, it suggests Qwen-14B as a lower-cost stand-in for text continuation ("cost-effective high-quality text") and summarization ("high-quality summaries") — where it takes 3 of the 6 overall-similarity cells once Qwen2-7B is counted (it loses bertScore on both, 82.97 vs 83.82 and 85.62 vs 88.60) against the smaller open models (Qwen-7B still edges it on text-continuation bleu, 7.10 vs. 5.70, and Baichuan2-13B on summarization bertScore, 85.64 vs. 85.62) — and Baichuan2-13B for QA, reserving GPT-4 itself for update/error-correction tasks that need the highest precision.

// does it hold up?

The eval read

The CRUD framing is a genuine methodological difference, not just a relabeling — Create/Delete are built by a "reverse," search-engine-driven construction pipeline, Read by a chain-of-thought multi-document question pipeline, and Update by reusing an entirely separate hallucination-injection dataset (UHGEval) — so the four categories really do stress different capabilities, not one QA task wearing four names. But two of the four (Create and Delete) share the same construction method, the same retrieval database, and the same three overall-similarity metrics plus RAGQuestEval; the real methodological split is closer to "generation from retrieved context" (Create + Delete) vs. "question answering" (Read, at three difficulty levels) vs. "targeted correction" (Update) than a clean four-way partition.

RAGQuestEval's own validity is worth separating from the dataset's. It's adapted from QuestEval, and the paper's claim that this family of metric "improves the correlation with human judgments over previous metrics" is QuestEval's own original validation, not a new human-correlation check run on RAGQuestEval in this paper's Chinese, retrieval-augmented setting. What the paper does validate is metric-internal consistency: an independent MRR-based retrieval ranking (Figure 7) agrees with the end-to-end RAGQuestEval-based ranking (Table 5) — a real cross-check, but a check between two automated metrics, not between an automated metric and a human rater. The one human-annotation effort described (three native-Chinese annotators reviewing a one-fifth sample of generated queries) validates that those questions read naturally, not that RAGQuestEval's scores track human judgments of answer quality. Neither the paper's main text nor an appendix (there isn't one — this is a single, self-contained 31-page paper with no separate limitations section or appendix material) revisits this gap. The metric's own definition has a smaller internal wrinkle, too: the printed precision equation (Eq. 2) divides by the count of all generated questions, while the surrounding prose ("the average answer similarity of all questions, excluding the unanswerable ones") and Figure 6's own worked example (2 answerable of 3, averaging over 2) both divide by the answerable subset instead — this page follows the prose and figure, which is almost certainly what was intended, but the printed formula doesn't match either.

Every dataset is built from Chinese news text, and four of the five — summarization, both multi-document QA sets plus single-document QA, and hallucination modification — have every ground truth, question, or correction GPT-4-generated or GPT-4-guided. The exception is text continuation: its reference is simply the second half of a real, human-written article (§3.3), with no GPT-4 involved in producing it at all. For the other four, that's a deliberate, reasonable contamination defense on its own terms (news published after July 2023 minimizes pretraining overlap), but it also means every system evaluated on those four tasks — including GPT-4-0613 and GPT-4o themselves — is scored against a reference whose phrasing, question style, and framing trace back to GPT-4. RAGQuestEval's actual scoring is a token-level F1 comparison, not an LLM judge, so there's no direct "GPT-4 grades GPT-4" loop in the metric itself — but a subtler stylistic-alignment risk remains, and the paper doesn't discuss or test for it. Its own numbers, though, let you check the hypothesis directly, and they cut against it: single-document question answering (§3.4) is the one block where GPT-4 authored both the question and the reference answer, so self-alignment should be most visible there if it's real. Instead it's the single block of six where GPT-4 variants take only 1 of 5 scored columns — GPT-3.5-turbo posts the highest bleu, rouge-L and bertScore (39.76 / 57.24 / 83.81), Baichuan2-13B the highest recall (76.92), and GPT-4-0613's own bleu (33.87) trails GPT-3.5-turbo's by 5.9 points. Across all six task blocks GPT-4 variants take 22 of 30 cells overall, so single-document QA is the outlier — and it's the wrong direction for the self-preference worry, not confirmation of it.

Two of the six ablation tables' own bold-face "best" markers don't always match the printed numbers — a font-layer check (bold vs. regular weight) against the recomputed column maximum turned up 3 of 30 scored cells wrong in both Table 6 (embedding models) and Table 8 (LLMs): in Table 8's two-document QA row, GPT-4o's 22.84 bleu and Qwen2-7B's 41.25 rouge-L both beat the bolded GPT-3.5-turbo values (22.75, 37.25) but aren't marked; in Table 6's summarization row, bge-base's 23.69 bleu is the true max but stella-base's lower 23.50 is bolded instead. This reads like an incomplete table refresh after Qwen2-7B and GPT-4o were added in the July-2024 revision, and §4.1's own prose corroborates it: its list of base LLMs still names only six — GPT-3.5, GPT-4, ChatGLM2-6B, Baichuan2-13B, Qwen-7B, Qwen-14B — against the eight Table 8 actually prints — Tables 3, 4, 5 and 7 (chunk size, chunk overlap, retriever, top-k) all carry the same bold-face convention and every one of them is clean (0 of 30 mismatches each) — but it means a reader should check the printed numbers directly for any specific comparison in Tables 6 or 8 rather than trust the paper's own emphasis.

Finally, every ablation table reports a single number per cell with no repeated runs, variance, or confidence interval anywhere in the paper (confirmed by searching the full text for "std", "variance", "confidence interval" and "seed" — nothing beyond one unrelated match), and there's no separate appendix where such a study could be hiding. That already matters for margins as small as 2-document QA's bleu (BM25's 24.61 vs. Hybrid+Rerank's 24.53) — but it compounds with a limit the paper states once and the results section doesn't repeat: "considering that we used gpt-3.5 as the baseline model for the experiments, to reduce the cost, we only conducted experiments on 1/5 of our dataset" (§4.1). Every number in Tables 3–8 — the five parameter ablations (chunk size, chunk overlap, retriever, embedding model, top-k), each run with GPT-3.5-turbo as the fixed baseline LLM, and the Table 8 LLM comparison itself — comes from a 20% subsample of the 36,166-instance benchmark, with no repeated draws reported. For a benchmark whose whole argument is "the right pipeline setting depends on the task," a single run over a fifth of the data is a thin base for point-and-a-half margins.

baselines

8 LLMs spanning open (ChatGLM2-6B, Qwen-7B/14B/2-7B, Baichuan2-13B) and closed (GPT-3.5-turbo, GPT-4-0613, GPT-4o) models is a fair, reasonably current-for-2024 spread, but the original six are 2023 vintage and the July-2024 update stops at GPT-4o/Qwen2-7B — no Claude, no Gemini, no Llama-3+, no reasoning model, no DeepSeek.

contamination

Handled deliberately and well for the retrieval corpus (news after July 2023); four of five task references are GPT-4-authored or GPT-4-guided (text continuation is the exception), raising a stylistic-alignment risk the paper never tests for — though single-document QA, where the risk should bite hardest, shows the opposite pattern.

what's missed

No repeated-run variance anywhere; RAGQuestEval's human-correlation claim is inherited from QuestEval's original paper, not re-validated here; 2 of 6 ablation tables have stale bold-face "best" markers on 3 of 30 cells each.

would raise confidence

A small human-rating study correlating RAGQuestEval scores with human judgments of the actual RAG outputs (not just query naturalness); repeated runs with reported variance on at least the closest ablation comparisons; a refreshed LLM/embedding roster for a 2026 reader.

// bottom line

The pipeline-wide ablation methodology — sweep chunk size, chunk overlap, embedding, retriever, top-k and LLM together, across four genuinely different application scenarios — is the paper's durable contribution, and it's executed with real construction-pipeline variety rather than one QA task renamed four times. The specific numbers and rankings, though, are a snapshot of one model/embedding generation from early-to-mid 2024, RAGQuestEval's validity rests on a metric validated in a different paper for a different setting, and even the tables' own visual "best" markers need a second check before you quote them.

// takeaways

What to remember

  1. CRUD-RAG's real contribution is evaluating the whole RAG pipeline — chunk size, chunk overlap, embedding model, retriever, top-k, and LLM — across four distinct application scenarios (36,166 instances, an 86,834-document corpus), not just scoring the generator on question answering the way most prior reference-required benchmarks did.
  2. No single setting wins everywhere: Hybrid+Rerank retrieval takes 14 of the 15 scored cells across the three QA tasks (16 of 30 across the full grid, including 2 more in summarization) — BM25 edges it on 2-document QA's bleu — but BM25 instead sweeps every overall-similarity metric on hallucination modification — the paper's own scenario-specific recommendations (Table 9) follow directly from this, not from one "best" configuration.
  3. Treat the specific numbers as time-boxed rather than a current leaderboard: RAGQuestEval borrows its human-correlation validation from QuestEval's original (different) evaluation, 3 of 30 cells in two of the six tables have bold-face "best" markers that don't match the printed digits, and the newest model in any version of the LLM ablation is GPT-4o from mid-2024.
