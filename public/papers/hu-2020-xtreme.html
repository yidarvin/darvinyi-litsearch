2020 · ICML · Benchmarks & Evals

XTREME: A Massively Multilingual Multi-task Benchmark for Evaluating Cross-lingual Generalization

Nine established cross-lingual tasks — XNLI, PAWS-X, POS, NER, XQuAD, MLQA, TyDiQA-GoldP, BUCC, Tatoeba — bolted onto one protocol: fine-tune on English only, then evaluate zero-shot across 40 typologically diverse languages spanning 12 language families. XLM-R Large tops every task under that protocol (68.2 average vs. mBERT's 59.8), but a machine-translation baseline beats it on 3 of the 5 tasks where translation is possible — though two of those three wins are translate-test QA scores the paper's own table caption calls not directly comparable; on the comparable translate-train baseline alone, XLM-R Large actually wins 4 of the 5 on F1.

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

By 2020, English-only NLP had settled on a standard playbook: evaluate general-purpose representations across a broad, multi-task suite (GLUE, then SuperGLUE) rather than any single dataset, because single-task evaluation is gameable — MLDoc can be solved by keyword matching, and MultiNLI (the corpus XNLI is derived from) is known to contain superficial cues models can exploit rather than genuinely reasoning. Cross-lingual representation learning had no equivalent. Prior cross-lingual evaluation clustered around two narrow pillars: translation quality (bilingual lexicon induction, MT) restricted mostly to typologically related, high-resource language pairs, or single downstream tasks — dependency parsing, NER, sentiment, NLI, document classification, QA — each evaluated in isolation, on its own language subset, with no shared protocol tying them together. That made it impossible to compare "general-purpose" multilingual encoders (mBERT, XLM) on equal footing, since each new representation paper simply picked whichever tasks and languages flattered it.

XTREME's fix is to do for cross-lingual transfer what GLUE and SuperGLUE did for English: fix a suite of tasks, fix an evaluation protocol, and force every model through the same paces. The specific protocol it locks in is zero-shot cross-lingual transfer — fine-tune once on English-labelled data, then evaluate without any further training on the target languages — chosen "both for efficiency purposes (as it only requires testing, not training, on each language) and practical considerations (as annotated training data is not available for many languages)," even though the paper is explicit that English is not generally the best transfer source for every target language. The suite spans nine tasks XTREME organizes into four categories — sentence classification (XNLI, PAWS-X), structured prediction (POS, NER), question answering (XQuAD, MLQA, TyDiQA-GoldP), and sentence retrieval (BUCC, Tatoeba) — chosen for task difficulty (there should be real headroom below human performance), task diversity (word-, phrase-, and sentence-level transfer), single-GPU training efficiency, and permissive licensing.

// how it works

The method

XTREME covers 40 languages spanning 12 language families and two isolates (the paper's Appendix A names them explicitly: Basque and Korean). Language selection starts from the top 100 Wikipedias by article count as of December 2019 (a data-availability proxy, and a practical one — it also guarantees coverage by mBERT and XLM's own pretraining corpora). From that pool, every language appearing in at least three of the constituent datasets is kept first, yielding 19 languages — mostly Indo-European or otherwise "major world languages." A further 21 languages are then added specifically to pull in under-represented families, aiming for at least two languages per family "wherever possible" — footnoted exceptions are Austro-Asiatic, Kartvelian, and Kra-Dai, plus the isolates, which contribute only one language apiece. So "12 families" is true by the paper's own count, but several of those families are represented by a single language standing in for the whole family's typology.

Each of the 9 tasks reuses an existing dataset rather than collecting new data (Table 1 lists exactly one corpus per task): XNLI (Conneau et al., 2018) and PAWS-X (Yang et al., 2019) are professionally-translated test sets built on MultiNLI and PAWS training data respectively; POS uses Universal Dependencies v2.5 treebanks (17 shared tags); NER uses WikiAnn with the Rahimi et al. (2019) balanced splits; XQuAD (Artetxe et al., 2020) and MLQA (Lewis et al., 2019) are both SQuAD v1.1-trained span-extraction QA evaluated on translated or parallel-mined non-English test data; TyDiQA-GoldP is the gold-passage variant of a QA set written without seeing the answer first (3× and 2× less lexical overlap with the question than XQuAD/MLQA respectively, by the authors' own estimate); BUCC and Tatoeba are unsupervised sentence-retrieval tasks scored by cosine similarity with no fine-tuning at all. Tatoeba's metric is stated inconsistently, and the inconsistency inverts its direction: §3.2 says the nearest neighbour is found by cosine similarity and the paper then “calculate[s] error rate” — lower is better — while Table 1's metric column, Table 2's metric row and Table 13's caption all say Accuracy, where higher is better. The reported numbers rise with model quality, so the tables are the ones to trust.

Because most tasks only have gold test data in a handful of languages, XTREME adds pseudo test sets for two tasks whose English test data is itself a human translation source (XNLI and XQuAD): the English test set is machine-translated (an in-house system) into all 40 languages, giving 40-way-parallel diagnostic data. The authors first sanity-check this trick against the real translated test sets: on XQuAD, the machine-translated pseudo test set underestimates mBERT's true performance by 3.0 F1 / 0.2 EM; on XNLI, it overestimates accuracy by 2.4 points. Translation quality against the professional references averages 34.2 BLEU / 58.9 chrF across the 14 XNLI languages checked, correlating only moderately with mBERT's actual score (Pearson ρ = 0.57 BLEU, 0.28 chrF) — the MT system is good enough to be a useful diagnostic, not good enough to be mistaken for gold data.

Baselines fall into five families: mBERT, XLM, and XLM-R Large (self-supervised multilingual masked-LM pretraining, at increasing scale of data/vocabulary), MMTE (the encoder of a massively multilingual NMT model, fine-tuned for transfer), plus non-representation-learning baselines that use an in-house MT system directly: translate-train (translate English training data into the target language, fine-tune mBERT there), translate-train multi-task (one multi-task mBERT over all translated languages jointly), and translate-test (fine-tune English BERT-large on English, translate the target test data back to English at inference time). Where target-language training data exists (POS, NER, TyDiQA-GoldP), the paper also reports in-language mBERT variants (1,000 examples / full data / multi-task) as an upper-reference, plus human performance estimates carried over from each task's source English dataset (MNLI/PAWS-X/SQuAD/TyDiQA/97%-canonical-POS) — with no human estimate attempted for NER (automatically-generated labels) or the retrieval tasks (impractical to elicit).

The paper's central diagnostic is the cross-lingual transfer gap: English test-set performance minus the average performance over the other languages, with 0 meaning perfect transfer. It is a plain difference, not a ratio or a normalized score, computed per task. Table 3's own caption states plainly that "the average gaps are computed over the sentence classification and QA tasks" — POS and NER are reported in the same table but left out of that average, with no reason given there. A separate footnote attached to the §4.3 results discussion (not to Table 3 itself) cautions that scores aren't directly comparable across languages "for the tasks where test sets differ, i.e. POS, NER, MLQA, and TyDiQA-GoldP" — but that list also names two of the five tasks Table 3 does average over (MLQA, TyDiQA-GoldP), so it can't be read as the paper's reason for excluding POS/NER specifically.

// what they found

The results

XLM-R Large is the strongest zero-shot transfer model, beating mBERT on every one of the 9 tasks — average 68.2 vs. 59.8 — with the gap smallest on the two structured-prediction tasks (POS +2.3, NER +3.2) and largest on sentence retrieval (Tatoeba +18.6, BUCC +9.3) and the two harder QA sets (XQuAD F1 +12.1, MLQA F1 +10.2). MMTE is competitive with mBERT but beats it on exactly three of the nine tasks — XNLI (67.4 vs. 65.4), POS (73.5 vs. 71.5), and BUCC (59.8 vs. 56.7) — and trails on the rest, landing at a near-identical overall average (59.5 vs. 59.8).

Even XLM-R Large's best zero-shot numbers sit well below the human-performance reference wherever one exists: 79.2 vs. 92.8 on XNLI, 86.4 vs. 97.5 on PAWS-X, 73.8 vs. 97.0 on POS, 60.8 vs. 82.3 EM on XQuAD. And zero-shot transfer is not the strongest way to use these models — with a caveat the paper itself prints: where a machine-translation baseline is available (XNLI, PAWS-X, and the three QA tasks — the paper withholds translation baselines for POS/NER since annotation projection is unreliable, and for BUCC/Tatoeba since "translation-based baselines are not meaningful for sentence retrieval"), the best translate-based score beats XLM-R Large's zero-shot score on 3 of those 5 tasks: translate-train multi-task mBERT reaches 88.9 on PAWS-X (vs. XLM-R's 86.4); translate-test BERT-large reaches 72.9 F1 on MLQA (vs. 71.6) and 72.1 F1 on TyDiQA-GoldP (vs. 65.1). But Table 2's own caption flags exactly those last two wins as suspect: "for the QA tasks, translate-test performance is not directly comparable to the other scores as a small number of test questions were discarded and alignment is measured on the English data." Restricted to translate-train — the caption-approved MT baseline — XLM-R Large's zero-shot score actually wins 4 of the 5 on F1, losing only PAWS-X. The QA tasks are reported as paired F1 / EM with neither designated primary, and one pair splits: on TyDiQA-GoldP, XLM-R takes F1 (65.1 vs. 64.2) but loses EM by 4.3 points (45.0 vs. 49.3). On EM the count is 3 of 5. Zero-shot XLM-R also wins outright on XNLI (79.2 vs. best-translate 76.8) and on XQuAD's F1 (76.6 vs. 76.3, though translate-test's EM of 62.1 does edge XLM-R's 60.8 — another translate-test QA number the same caption caveat applies to).

The cross-lingual transfer gap (Table 3, lower is better) tells the same story from the other direction. Averaged over the five classification/QA tasks, mBERT's gap is 21.1 points and XLM-R's is 14.3 — smaller everywhere (XLM-R's gap is lower than mBERT's on all 7 of the 7 shared columns, including POS and NER, which sit outside the cross-task average) but only proportionally: POS's gap barely moves (25.5 → 24.3, about 5% smaller) while XNLI's shrinks by 38% (16.5 → 10.2), which is exactly what the paper means by "[XLM-R does] not have the same impact on the syntactic structured prediction tasks." Using machine translation shrinks the gap further still: translate-train's average gap is 16.1 and translate-test's is 12.9 — both below mBERT's zero-shot gap, and translate-test's is even below XLM-R Large's.

Two further diagnostics probe why transfer fails on syntax. First, mBERT's accuracy on POS tag n-grams that never appeared in the English training data collapses relative to n-grams that did: on unseen trigrams, English scores 63.0 but the average of every other language is only 12.1 (a 50.9-point gap); on unseen 4-grams the gap is 49.2 (67.5 vs. 18.3) — far larger than the corresponding "seen" gaps (39.7 for trigrams, 43.7 for 4-grams), which the authors read as evidence models struggle specifically with word-order combinations that differ from English, not just with unfamiliar vocabulary. Second, an error-consistency analysis on the XNLI dev set finds mBERT reproduces its English-correct predictions in another language only 71.8% of the time, and is "about random" on examples it got wrong in English — i.e., cross-lingual transfer is not simply "whatever mBERT knows in English, ported."

// does it hold up?

The eval read

XTREME's central design choice — fine-tune on English only, then test zero-shot everywhere — measures a specific and useful thing: whether a multilingual representation's English-side supervision generalizes without any target-language signal at all. It does not measure, and the paper is upfront about this, whether English is even the best source language (it explicitly cites Lin et al. (2019) to the contrary), nor how models fare with a little in-language signal — which the paper's own in-language-few-shot ablation shows matters differently by task: 1,000 target-language examples get mBERT to 87.6 POS / 77.9 NER, only about 2 points off full in-language training on POS (89.8) but a full 10.4 points short of it on NER (88.3). The paper's own "similar to being trained on the full labelled dataset" claim for 1,000-example transfer holds for POS but not for NER, and zero-shot transfer itself sits well below both on either task. A 2026 reader should treat the zero-shot number as a floor for "no target data available," not a ceiling on what any given deployment could achieve, and should note the whole comparison predates the current practice of prompting large instruction-tuned multilingual models directly rather than fine-tuning a frozen encoder per task.

The "40 typologically diverse languages" framing survives scrutiny only partway. The selection procedure is transparent and reasonable — top-100 Wikipedia by article count, then a deliberate second pass to pull in under-represented families — but the paper's own footnote admits Austro-Asiatic, Kartvelian, Kra-Dai, and both isolates get exactly one language each. "12 language families" is a true count of families touched, not a claim that each family's internal diversity is represented; a single Vietnamese data point standing in for all of Austro-Asiatic is a coarser proxy than the "12 families" framing implies on first read. Figure 3 (below) makes the resource skew visible directly: mBERT's transfer score rises with each family/script group's Wikipedia resourcing, from Niger-Congo and Kra-Dai at the low end to the Indo-European branches (Germanic, Romance, Slavic) at the high end. Figure 2's correlation analysis reinforces the same point from the pretraining-data side: mBERT's score correlates strongly with a language's share of Wikipedia on 6 of 9 tasks (ρ 0.79-0.95) and much more weakly on POS/NER (ρ≈0.35) and Tatoeba (ρ=0.68) — meaning a large chunk of the "cross-lingual" gap the paper reports is confounded with pretraining-data volume, not just linguistic distance from English.

The pseudo test sets and translate-train/translate-test baselines all lean on the same in-house, unreleased MT system, whose quality the paper itself only spot-checks on 14 XNLI languages (34.2 BLEU / 58.9 chrF, correlating just ρ=0.28-0.57 with the metric it is meant to proxy). That is good enough to justify using pseudo data as a rough diagnostic tool, which is all the paper claims for it — but it also means every translate-train/translate-test number in Table 2 is a joint measurement of "representation quality" and "this specific proprietary translation system's quality for this language pair," and a lower-resource language with worse MT will look like it has a bigger transfer problem than it does. By the raw table, a machine-translation baseline beats XLM-R Large zero-shot on 3 of 5 comparable tasks — but two of those three wins (MLQA, TyDiQA-GoldP) are translate-test QA scores that Table 2's own caption calls not directly comparable, and restricting to the comparable translate-train baseline flips the picture to XLM-R Large winning 4 of 5, losing only PAWS-X. Either way this isn't a minor caveat — MT quality (or its absence, and which MT-dependent variant is even admissible for a given task) is doing real, unquantified work in the paper's own headline comparison.

Read in 2026, the specific numbers are a historical snapshot rather than a live scoreboard: XLM-R Large's 68.2 average and mBERT's 59.8 have both been overtaken by later massively multilingual and instruction-tuned models operating in entirely different regimes (in-context, no fine-tuning). What remains useful is the harness: nine tasks spanning four transfer levels, a documented and reproducible language-selection procedure, an explicit transfer-gap metric, and (per the paper's own stated contributions) a public leaderboard and platform — a structure later multilingual suites (XTREME-R, XGLUE, and domain-specific descendants like LEXTREME) still borrow from directly.

baselines

Four representation-learning models (mBERT, XLM, XLM-R Large, MMTE) plus three MT-dependent baselines (translate-train, translate-train multi-task, translate-test) built on an in-house, unreleased translation system — reasonable coverage for 2020, but the MT baselines aren't independently reproducible by later papers using a different MT system.

contamination

Not discussed. mBERT and XLM are pretrained on Wikipedia, and several of XTREME's tasks (NER, XQuAD, MLQA, TyDiQA-GoldP) are themselves built from Wikipedia text — the paper never checks for train/pretrain overlap, and Figure 2's ρ≈0.8 correlation between score and Wikipedia size is consistent with (though not proof of) some of that being memorization rather than pure transfer.

what's missed

No variance/seed reporting anywhere in Table 2 or 3 (single run per model per task); no analysis of how much of the "transfer gap" is MT-system quality versus genuine representational limits; family coverage of 1 language for 3 of 12 families plus 2 isolates means "12 families" overstates within-family diversity.

would raise confidence

Multiple seeds/runs with reported variance; an ablation swapping the in-house MT system for a public one to isolate translation-quality confounds; a released pseudo-test-set validation across all 40 languages (only 14 XNLI languages get the BLEU/chrF check) rather than a partial spot-check.

// bottom line

XTREME is a well-built aggregation of nine already-established cross-lingual tasks into one shared zero-shot protocol — its real contribution is the harness (task suite, language-selection methodology, transfer-gap metric, leaderboard), not a novel evaluation paradigm. The 2020 leaderboard numbers are dated, but the structure is still the one later multilingual benchmarks (including this graph's own LEXTREME) build on.

// takeaways

What to remember

  1. XTREME packages 9 existing tasks (XNLI, PAWS-X, POS, NER, XQuAD, MLQA, TyDiQA-GoldP, BUCC, Tatoeba) across 40 languages and 12 language families + 2 isolates into a single zero-shot cross-lingual transfer protocol: fine-tune on English only, evaluate everywhere else, with no target-language training data at all.
  2. XLM-R Large beats mBERT on all 9 tasks zero-shot (68.2 vs. 59.8 average) but still trails human performance by double digits everywhere it can be compared. A machine-translation baseline beats its zero-shot score on 3 of 5 comparable tasks by the raw table, but two of those wins are translate-test QA scores the paper's own caption calls not directly comparable — on the comparable translate-train baseline alone, XLM-R actually wins 4 of 5 — so zero-shot transfer is a useful floor, not the strongest available approach.
  3. The typological-diversity claim is real but uneven: 3 of 12 language families plus both isolates are represented by exactly one language each, and mBERT's score correlates strongly with a language's share of Wikipedia on 6 of 9 tasks (ρ 0.79-0.95) — meaning some of the reported "cross-lingual gap" is confounded with pretraining-data volume rather than pure linguistic distance.
