NegotiationToM: A Benchmark for Stress-testing Machine Theory of Mind on Negotiation Surrounding

A theory-of-mind benchmark built on 395 real human-to-human negotiation dialogues (from the CaSiNo corpus) rather than synthetic templates, scoring desire, belief, and intention separately at every conversation round. Across six 2023-era LLMs and three prompting regimes, the best model still trails human annotators by 27.85 points on desire, 32.96 on belief, and 43.82-48.98 on intention, even with chain-of-thought.

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

By early 2024, theory-of-mind (ToM) evaluation for LLMs had converged on two dominant recipes, and the authors argue both are compromised. One lineage adapts the Sally-Anne false-belief test into synthetic, template-generated stories (ToMi and its relatives); the other, exemplified by FANToM — whose own "stress-testing machine theory of mind" framing this paper explicitly borrows — probes belief tracking through conversations, but the conversations themselves are still machine-generated. Both approaches, the paper argues, are "prone to shortcuts and spurious correlations": a model can often get the right answer by pattern-matching template structure rather than genuinely modeling another agent's mind, and templated data can't capture the "complicated and diverse aspects of a realistic negotiation, such as rapport building, discussing preferences, exchanging offers, emotional expression, and persuasion." A second, narrower gap: most prior ToM benchmarks — including the closely related BigToM (Gandhi et al., 2023), which the paper calls "the most related work to ours" — test belief in isolation and rarely cover the full desire-belief-intention (BDI) triad in one setting.

NegotiationToM targets both gaps at once by grounding its questions in a real human-to-human negotiation corpus rather than a synthetic one, and by scoring three mental-state dimensions — desire, belief, and intention — jointly, at every round of the same conversation, so a model's coherence in tracking a shifting mental state can be measured, not just its accuracy on isolated snapshots.

// how it works

The method

The benchmark is built on top of CaSiNo (Chawla et al., 2021, NAACL), a human-to-human negotiation corpus in which two participants role-play campsite neighbors bargaining over food, water, and firewood packages, using real linguistic strategies such as small talk, preference elicitation, and fairness appeals. NegotiationToM takes the strategy-annotated subset of CaSiNo and layers three question types onto it, explicitly grounded in Bratman's (1987) Belief-Desire-Intention (BDI) agent model:

// the three mental-state axes

Desire: at each round, what is each agent's high/medium/low preference ranking over food, water, and firewood, given only the dialogue so far? Belief: what does each agent think the other agent's preference ranking is, given the same dialogue history? Intention: what negotiation strategy — one of nine categories (Build-Rapport, Show-Empathy, Promote-Coordination, Callout-Fairness, Undermine-Requirements, Discover-Preference, Describe-Need, No-Need, No-Intention) — is expressed in a given utterance? Intention labels are inherited directly from CaSiNo's existing expert strategy annotations, mapped onto this nine-way scheme; desire and belief are newly annotated.

Five graduate-student annotators (recruited from English-speaking universities) labeled the desire and belief states for both participants at every truncated round of every dialogue, after a calibration pass on the first 100 rounds. Inter-annotator agreement is reported at Fleiss's κ = 79.03% overall, breaking down to 83.02% / 72.23% / 79.32% for desire's high/medium/low sub-questions and 85.25% / 74.03% / 78.81% for belief's. The resulting benchmark spans 395 dialogues, 2,380 rounds of truncated conversation, and 4,618 utterances, with each utterance carrying seven questions (three desire sub-questions, three belief sub-questions, one multi-label intention question) for a total of 13.8 thousand questions — roughly 7.0 questions per context and an average turn length of 42.2 tokens. That turn length is by far the longest of the three benchmarks compared in the paper's own statistics table (ToMi's synthetic turns average 4.7 tokens, FANToM's machine-generated turns 21.9), but the questions-per-context figure isn't: NegotiationToM's 7.0 sits between ToMi's 6.0 and FANToM's 12.9, the higher of the two.

Because CaSiNo has been public since 2021 and predates every tested model's training cutoff, the authors run an explicit contamination check (Appendix A.1, following Golchin and Surdeanu, 2024): they sample 100 CaSiNo instances, prompt ChatGPT and GPT-4 to generate the dialogue's likely next turn, and have a human verify whether any generated continuation matches the real one. None did — but the authors are careful to caveat that this only rules out verbatim memorization, not exposure during pretraining, noting "no systematic approach can effectively address the contamination issue unless all training datasets utilized for LLMs are made publicly available."

Six instruction-tuned LLMs are evaluated — GPT-4 (0613), ChatGPT (0613), Claude-v1.3, Claude-v2.1, LLaMA-2-Chat 13B, and LLaMA-2-Chat 70B — under three prompting regimes: zero-shot (formulated as multiple-choice QA, following Robinson and Wingate, 2023), zero-shot chain-of-thought ("let's think step by step," following Kojima et al., 2022), and few-shot (four hand-written exemplars for desire/belief, seven for intention, following Brown et al., 2020). Desire and belief are scored by exact match (all three of high/medium/low must be correct); intention, a multi-label task, by micro and macro F1. Two composite metrics are also reported: an "All" score requiring desire, belief, and intention to all be answered correctly for the same conversational turn, and a consistency score requiring correct answers across an entire dialogue. A human baseline comes from three CS graduate students independently answering the same multiple-choice questions, majority-voted.

// what they found

The results

The headline finding: every tested model, in every prompting regime, scores well below the human baseline on every dimension. The human majority vote reaches 91.14% exact match on both desire and belief, 83.75% micro F1 / 84.65% macro F1 on intention, 43.78% on the "All" score, and 75.44% consistency on both desire and belief. Comparing each dimension to its own best-performing model configuration, the gaps are 27.85 points on desire, 32.96 on belief, 43.82 on intention micro F1, and 48.98 on intention macro F1 — four separate gaps on four separate metrics, not a single blended number. GPT-4 0613 (CoT) is the strongest model on desire (63.29% EM) and belief (58.18% EM); Claude-v2.1 (CoT) is strongest on intention (39.93% micro F1, 35.67% macro F1) and on the composite "All" score, where it reaches only 3.68% against the human 43.78%.

Chain-of-thought prompting helps most models on most dimensions but the picture is mixed: LLaMA-2-Chat 70B's desire EM rises from 24.40% (zero-shot) to 30.34% (CoT), but its intention micro F1 actually falls slightly, from 33.23% to 30.57%. On the few-shot setting, GPT-4 0613's intention scores (35.10% micro F1, 33.21% macro F1) edge out its own CoT result (34.90% / 31.26%) — the one dimension where few-shot beats CoT for the strongest model. Consistency scores are far lower than the exact-match numbers suggest they should be: GPT-4 (CoT) leads other models at 17.72% (desire) and 14.18% (belief), and every model scores exactly 0% consistency on intention, since intention is multi-label and the metric requires an exact whole-conversation match.

A prompt-format ablation on ChatGPT and GPT-4 finds the benchmark's main-table format ("combined," which bundles all three preference questions together) substantially outperforms alternatives: for GPT-4, desire EM is 62.77% combined versus 40.01% individual versus 20.10% ranking-format — a roughly 3x swing from format choice alone. A downstream check ties the annotations to a practical use: injecting the annotated desire and belief states into the prompt for CaSiNo's original strategy-prediction task improves every one of the six models, e.g. Claude-v2.1 gains +7.44 points micro F1 (27.12% → 34.56%) and +5.54 points macro F1 (24.48% → 30.02%) when given desire/belief context alongside the raw dialogue.

The paper's error analysis (based on a 1,000-response sample) finds a consistent pattern within each dimension: for desire and belief, the dominant error is "Incorrect Items Selection" — models guess a specific item ranking rather than answering "Not Given" when the dialogue hasn't actually disclosed enough information, an error CoT reduces for most (not all) models. For intention, the dominant errors are "Include Incorrect Intentions" and "Did Both" (including wrong intentions while also missing correct ones) — models tend to over-select from the nine intention options rather than under-select. LLaMA-2 models specifically produce a disproportionate share of "Irrelevant Response" errors, such as repeating the question instead of answering it.

// does it hold up?

The eval read

The core design choice — real human negotiation dialogue instead of synthetic templates, with three mental-state axes scored jointly at every round — is a genuine improvement over the shortcut-prone benchmarks this paper critiques, and the authors do run a real (if partial) contamination check rather than ignoring the issue. But several parts of the evaluation deserve more scrutiny than the headline "significantly worse than human" framing suggests.

The six baselines — GPT-4-0613, ChatGPT-0613, Claude-v1.3, Claude-v2.1, LLaMA-2-Chat 13B/70B — are all mid-2023 model snapshots, already a year old relative to this paper's own EMNLP 2024 publication and lacking any 2024-era model (no GPT-4-Turbo, no Claude 3, no Llama 3, no open reasoning model). The paper's own text calls these "state-of-the-art LLMs," but the reported 27.85-48.98-point human gaps characterize one generation of models frozen at a point roughly a year before publication, not current LLM capability generally — a caveat the paper doesn't flag explicitly.

The contamination check is real but narrow: it tests only whether ChatGPT/GPT-4 can regenerate a held-out dialogue continuation verbatim, which rules out literal memorization but says nothing about whether models absorbed CaSiNo's label distributions, negotiation-strategy vocabulary, or item-preference structure during pretraining — a live risk given CaSiNo has been a public, fairly well-used NAACL dataset since 2021, well before any of the six models' training cutoffs. The authors themselves acknowledge this limit rather than papering over it, which is to their credit, but it means the contamination finding supports a narrower claim than "not contaminated."

The format-sensitivity ablation (5.4) is the sharpest internal warning sign: on GPT-4, switching only the question format — not the model, not the data — swings desire EM from 20.10% to 62.77%, a roughly 3x range. That ablation runs on just two models (ChatGPT, GPT-4); nothing in the paper establishes whether Claude or LLaMA's main-table numbers would show similarly large swings under a different format, which limits how much weight the specific per-model rankings in Table 4 should carry. Separately, the exact-match metric for desire/belief requires all three of high/medium/low to be correct simultaneously, with no partial credit — a design that, combined with the paper's own finding that models specifically struggle with the "medium" tier when information is incomplete (Section 5.4's case study), likely understates genuine partial competence. And the consistency metric is uninformative for intention by construction (every model scores exactly 0%, since it demands an exact multi-label match across an entire conversation) — a real limitation the paper reports honestly but doesn't flag as a metric-design issue.

The human baseline itself is thin on reported detail: three CS graduate students, majority-voted, using the same multiple-choice format the models see — but the paper never states how many of the 13.8K questions the human study actually covered, so it's unclear whether 91.14%/83.75%/84.65% reflects the full test set or a smaller sample.

baselines

Six models, all mid-2023 snapshots (GPT-4-0613, ChatGPT-0613, Claude-v1.3/v2.1, LLaMA-2-Chat 13B/70B) — a full model generation behind the paper's own EMNLP 2024 publication date, with no 2024-era or reasoning-focused model included.

contamination

A genuine check exists (100-instance next-turn regeneration test, human-verified), but it only rules out verbatim memorization, not distributional exposure to a dataset that's been public since 2021 — the authors flag this limitation themselves.

what's missed

No partial credit on the strict all-three-correct desire/belief metric; the format ablation (up to 3x swings from prompt format alone) runs on only 2 of 6 models; the human-eval sample size (how many of 13.8K questions) is unstated; the intention consistency metric is 0% for every model by construction.

would raise confidence

Re-running the format ablation across all six models; testing at least one contemporaneous (2024) model alongside the 2023 baselines; reporting the exact sample size for the human baseline and an inter-rater agreement statistic for its three-person panel (Appendix C.2 reports only a majority vote there, unlike the five-annotator labeling study, whose per-sub-question Fleiss's κ the paper does report in Table 3); a partial-credit variant of the desire/belief metric to separate "wrong on everything" from "wrong on the medium tier only."

// bottom line

The human-vs-model gap is real and the underlying design — real negotiation dialogue, three jointly-scored mental states, an honest (if partial) contamination check — is a solid contribution to the ToM-benchmark space FANToM helped define. But the specific per-model rankings in the main results table are resting on a prompt format shown, on the two models actually tested, to move scores by up to 3x, and the baselines are already a model generation stale relative to the paper's own publication date — both of which matter more to the durability of the paper's specific numbers than to its core "LLMs still lack negotiation ToM" claim.

// takeaways

What to remember

  1. Real dialogue, not synthetic templates. NegotiationToM repurposes CaSiNo, a human-to-human negotiation corpus, rather than generating templated or LLM-authored scenarios — 395 dialogues, 2,380 rounds, 4,618 utterances, 13.8K questions across desire, belief, and intention, explicitly framed by Bratman's BDI agent model.
  2. Every model trails humans, on every dimension, by a wide but dimension-specific margin. The best model config lags the human baseline by 27.85 points on desire exact match, 32.96 on belief exact match, and 43.82-48.98 on intention F1 — four distinct gaps on four distinct metrics, none of them interchangeable.
  3. Prompt format alone can swing scores 3x. On GPT-4, desire exact match ranges from 20.10% (ranking format) to 62.77% (combined format) with no change to the model or data — evidence the main-table rankings are more format-dependent than the paper's framing suggests, though the ablation itself only covers 2 of the 6 tested models.
  4. A core ToM benchmark, tagged accordingly. The benchmark itself — not a model, method, or agent framework — is the paper's entire contribution, squarely inside this atlas's benchmarks survey.
