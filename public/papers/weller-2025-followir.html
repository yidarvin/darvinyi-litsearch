FollowIR: Evaluating and Teaching Information Retrieval Models to Follow Instructions

FollowIR repurposes the detailed narratives TREC gives its professional relevance assessors into a benchmark that tests whether retrieval models actually change their rankings when that narrative is altered, via a new pairwise metric (p-MRR) — and trains FollowIR-7B, a Mistral-7B-Instruct fine-tune, to follow such instructions better.

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

Nearly every modern retrieval and text-embedding model is now built on a language-model backbone that is, in principle, capable of following long, complex instructions — yet the paper's own survey of prior instruction-aware retrieval work (INSTRUCTOR, TART, and GritLM among the models it evaluates) finds that models are given instructions that are short — "fewer than 10 words" — and repetitive: typically one instruction per whole dataset, naming a domain or a document format rather than a query-specific relevance definition. None of that prior work has an evaluation set that explicitly measures instruction-following itself, as opposed to ordinary ad-hoc retrieval quality: a model can simply ignore the instruction and still post a strong nDCG or MAP.

Meanwhile a much richer source of instructions has existed for decades and gone almost entirely unused by retrieval systems: every well-constructed TREC track ships a detailed "narrative," written for the professional human assessors who judge document relevance, that lays out fine-grained inclusion criteria, background context the annotator should ignore, and explicit exclusion criteria (including negation). IR models are only ever given the bare query, never these narratives. FollowIR's gap is exactly this: no benchmark tests whether a retrieval model's output changes when a TREC-grade instruction is deliberately altered, and no training data exists to teach a model to do so.

// how it works

The method

FollowIR repurposes three heavily-judged TREC collections — TREC Robust 2004 (news wire; Voorhees, 2005), TREC Common Core 2017 (New York Times Annotated Corpus; Allan et al., 2017), and TREC News 2021 (Washington Post v4; Soboroff et al., 2020) — precisely because each ships the kind of narrative described above. Figure 1 shows what these narratives actually contain: on 90.4% of TREC instructions the narrative specifies "and"/"or" inclusion conditions, on 52.9% it lists specific document types to exclude, and on 34.6% it adds background context the annotator is told is not itself grounds for relevance.

To turn a narrative into a pairwise instruction-following test, two expert annotators altered each TREC narrative slightly — adding constraints that narrow the definition of relevance — and then manually re-annotated only the documents the original TREC assessors had marked relevant. Figure 2 shows the mechanism: a query keeps its original TREC narrative and receives one altered version, and because alterations only ever remove relevant documents (never add new ones outside the originally-judged pool), only the previously-relevant documents — dozens per query, not the thousands in the full pool — need re-judging. The authors sampled 50 TREC Robust 2004 queries chosen specifically to not overlap with TREC Common Core 2017 (Common Core had itself already reused a different 50 Robust04 topics on the New York Times collection), plus 30 TREC News 2021 queries, and instructed annotators to change each narrative so that the number of relevant documents was cut roughly in half. After annotation, Table 1 puts the final benchmark at 52 Robust04, 20 Core17, and 32 News21 queries — what the paper's own appendix rounds to "our 102 query set" (roughly 20k relevance annotations in total).

Because different retrievers surface different relevant documents out of a multi-million-document collection, FollowIR evaluates as a reranking task over a fixed candidate pool per query: every annotated-relevant document plus the top non-relevant passages pulled by a pool of five retrieval models (BM25, BGE-base, E5-base-v2, TART-Contriever, and INSTRUCTOR-xl). Documents are chunked into 400-word passages with 200-word overlap and scored with MaxP, both to bound passage length and to let the benchmark be redistributed under fair use (the TREC document collections themselves can't be freely redistributed).

Every model is then scored two ways. First, with standard IR metrics computed on the query with its (unaltered) instruction attached — MAP for Robust04 and Core17, nDCG@5 for News21 (its official TREC track metric) — but, as the authors note, a model can score well here purely by treating the instruction as extra keyword soup. The paper's actual contribution is p-MRR, a new pairwise metric that isolates instruction-following by comparing where a document ranks under the original narrative to where it ranks under the altered one, computed only over the documents whose gold relevance actually changed:

p-MRR = RRogRRnew − 1  if Rog > Rnew,  else  1 − RRnewRRog // RR = reciprocal rank; R_og / R_new = a document's rank under the original / altered instruction. Since every FollowIR alteration only ever demotes a document's gold relevance, a correct model pushes that document to a worse rank under the altered instruction — which this formula scores positively. Scores are macro-averaged per query, then across queries; the text defines the range as −1 (worst) to +1 (best) with 0 for no change, and Table 2's caption states the printed scale as −100 to 100 (higher is better). This page follows the NAACL 2025 camera-ready, which notates this equation with RR/"reciprocal rank"; the arXiv preprint linked above writes the same formula with MRR/"mean reciprocal rank" — same math, different label.
// what they found

The results

Table 2 is the paper's central table: for each model, a standard IR score computed with the original instruction, and p-MRR computed on the alteration, per collection and averaged. The no-instruction-trained models (E5, Contriever, MonoBERT, BM25, MonoT5-base, E5-large-v2) range widely on standard metrics but score negative on average p-MRR (down to −3.9 for E5-base-v2); the lone exception is MonoT5-3B, the only model in that group over 3B parameters, at an average +2.5 p-MRR. Models trained with (short, dataset-level) instructions fare similarly — mostly negative average p-MRR, with GritLM-7B averaging essentially zero (−0.0) and TART-FLAN-T5-xl positive on two of its three collections (average +1.4). Among the three commercial embedding APIs, OpenAI v3 Large and Cohere v3 English are both negative on average (−2.7, −0.2) despite strong standard-metric scores (25.3 and 23.7), while Google Gecko is positive (+2.3) despite an identical 25.3 standard-metric score to OpenAI's — the paper attributes Gecko's edge to its own reported instruction-tuned training data.

The clearest pattern is in the last group: every general instruction-tuned LLM used as a reranker (FLAN-T5-base/large, Llama-2-7B-chat, GritLM-Reranker, Mistral-7B-instruct, and the paper's own FollowIR-7B) scores positive average p-MRR. Of these, the paper states that only FLAN-T5, Llama-2-7B-chat, and Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2 were not trained on any retrieval-specific data at all; GritLM-Reranker's underlying GritLM model is itself trained for embedding/retrieval, and FollowIR-7B — as below — is fine-tuned directly on the paper's own retrieval training data. Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2 is the strongest pre-existing model at +10.1 average p-MRR; fine-tuning it into FollowIR-7B — on roughly 1,800 GPT-3.5-Turbo-generated relevant/non-relevant training instances, filtered by using Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2 itself to check each generated label — raises that to +12.2 average p-MRR (a +20.8% relative gain) while also lifting the standard-metric average from 23.4 to 24.8 (+6.0% relative). FollowIR-7B's +12.2 average p-MRR is the highest of any row in Table 2, though its 24.8 standard-metric average is not the highest overall — OpenAI v3 Large and Google Gecko both post 25.3.

A separate ablation (Figure 3) asks why so many models fail: comparing query-only retrieval to adding the instruction as keywords, a short instruction, or the full instruction, most non-instruction-following models' scores decrease as instruction length grows, while models that do follow instructions gain from the added detail. The paper also validates the finding outside FOLLOWIR entirely: on the separate, concurrent InstructIR benchmark's "Robustness@10" metric (the minimum nDCG@10 across 10 instructions per query), FollowIR-7B scores 71.5 — roughly double its own base model Mistral-7B-instruct's 35.3, and the highest of the six models reported there (BM25 26.9, TART-Contriever 47.5, RepLLaMa 52.6, E5-Mistral 55.4).

// does it hold up?

The eval read

p-MRR is a genuinely new evaluation protocol, not a relabeled existing benchmark, and its construction is careful: it isolates instruction-following from keyword-search ability by measuring rank change only on the specific documents whose gold relevance the alteration flips. But that same construction has a structural asymmetry worth naming — every alteration in FOLLOWIR narrows the definition of relevance — annotators were instructed so that "the number of relevant documents was cut roughly in half," in the paper's own words; none broadens it, and none leaves the affected-document count at zero as a negative control. A model could in principle earn a strongly positive p-MRR by learning a coarse heuristic — "when the instruction gets longer or more specific, rank fewer documents as relevant" — without parsing what changed in the instruction's actual content. The paper's own Figure 3 partly cuts against a version of this worry (models that improve with instruction length do so gradually, keywords < short < full), but it doesn't test the reverse direction directly, so the concern isn't ruled out.

The evaluation is also, by the authors' own admission, a reranking-over-a-fixed-pool setup rather than full-collection retrieval: "we cannot use the full collection for retrieval (as each retriever finds different relevant documents by design)." Full-corpus numbers appear only for a subset of models in an appendix, described as showing "similar trends." That's a reasonable engineering trade-off for a benchmark this carefully hand-annotated, but it means the headline Table 2 numbers describe reranking behavior on a five-model-selected candidate pool, not end-to-end search.

FollowIR-7B's training data is also worth flagging on its own terms: the relevant/non-relevant documents are GPT-3.5-Turbo-1106-generated (synthetic, not real corpus text), and the label-filtering step that removes noisy synthetic examples uses Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2 — the very same base model FollowIR-7B is fine-tuned from, and a model whose own p-MRR (+10.1) is well short of perfect. That makes the training signal's ceiling partly bounded by the base model's own imperfect instruction-following, a mild self-distillation loop, even though the resulting gains are then verified on real, human-annotated TREC test data rather than on more synthetic text. Appendix B's replication of the recipe on a Llama-3-8B backbone shows the same fine-tuning approach transfers to a different base model — 0.03 → 5.1 p-MRR — and in fact by a larger absolute margin (+5.07 against FollowIR-7B's +2.1, since Mistral-Instruct already started at 10.1). What is lower is where it lands: 5.1 final p-MRR against 12.2, and 15.4 nDCG against 24.8. So the recipe transfers, but the level a backbone reaches is bounded more by where it starts than by how much the fine-tuning moves it.

baselines

Broad and reasonably current for 2024: four model categories spanning 100M–7B+ parameters (no-instruction IR, dataset-level-instruction IR, three commercial embedding APIs, and general-purpose instruction-tuned LLMs used as rerankers). No GPT-4-class reranker is evaluated, and BM25 is the only lexical baseline.

contamination

Not directly tested. TREC Robust04/Core17/News21 are public, decades-old collections that pretrained retrievers and LLM backbones could plausibly have seen — but the altered narratives and their resulting relevance flips are novel and hand-written for this paper, so memorization can't explain a model's p-MRR score even where it might inflate raw MAP/nDCG on the unaltered instruction.

what's missed

p-MRR (Eq. 1) is defined only over documents whose gold relevance changed; a model's behavior on the rest of the ranking isn't scored by p-MRR itself. And because every FollowIR alteration narrows relevance in the same direction, the metric can't yet distinguish genuine instruction comprehension from a one-directional "be stricter" heuristic.

would raise confidence

A held-out slice of alterations that broaden rather than narrow relevance; full-collection (not reranking-pool) results for the complete model roster rather than an appendix subset; and an ablation on FollowIR-7B's training pipeline that replaces the Mistral-filtered synthetic labels with human-verified ones.

// bottom line

FollowIR's central claim — that most current retrieval and embedding models, instruction-trained or not, largely fail to act on the instructions attached to their queries — is well supported by a genuinely new pairwise protocol built on real, professionally-written TREC narratives rather than synthetic ones. The caveat is that every constructed test case pushes relevance in the same direction (narrower), so a high p-MRR is consistent with, but doesn't fully distinguish from, a coarser "longer instruction ⇒ retrieve less" heuristic; and the reranking-only setup (acknowledged by the authors) means the numbers describe how well a model reorders a fixed candidate pool, not full end-to-end retrieval.

// takeaways

What to remember

  1. Most retrieval and embedding models — including many built on LLM backbones and some explicitly trained with instructions — score at or below zero average p-MRR on FOLLOWIR: they don't reliably change their rankings when a TREC-grade narrative is altered, even while still posting solid MAP/nDCG using the (unaltered) instruction as extra keyword context.
  2. Positive average p-MRR shows up almost exclusively in two places in Table 2: retrieval models over 3B parameters (MonoT5-3B: +2.5) or general instruction-tuned LLMs repurposed as rerankers that were never trained on retrieval data at all (Mistral-7B-instruct: +10.1, FLAN-T5-large: +4.7) — suggesting the skill traces to general instruction-following pretraining rather than IR-specific instruction training.
  3. That gap is closeable but not free: fine-tuning Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2 on ~1,800 synthetic query/instruction/document triples (FollowIR-7B) lifts average p-MRR from +10.1 to +12.2 on FOLLOWIR (+20.8% relative) — the highest average p-MRR of any row in Table 2 — and Robustness@10 from 35.3 to 71.5 on the separate InstructIR benchmark, but the training signal is synthetic and self-filtered, the evaluation itself is reranking-only, and Appendix B shows the level reached is backbone-dependent (a Llama-3-8B version of the same recipe climbs further in absolute terms, 0.03 to +5.1, but still lands well under Mistral's +12.2).
