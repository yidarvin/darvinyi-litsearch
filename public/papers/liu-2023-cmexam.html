Benchmarking Large Language Models on CMExam - A Comprehensive Chinese Medical Exam Dataset

CMExam turns 68,119 questions from the real Chinese National Medical Licensing Examination into a benchmark that grades models two ways at once — a scoreable multiple-choice answer and a free-text explanation — and layers five expert-annotated dimensions (disease group, clinical department, discipline, competency, difficulty) on top for stratified analysis. Zero-shot GPT-4 leads every model at 61.6% prediction accuracy against a 71.6% human baseline, yet on every reasoning metric it scores below all nine CMExam-fine-tuned models that report reasoning scores (two of the eleven, BERT-CMExam and RoBERTa-CMExam, are prediction-only).

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

By 2023, Chinese medical question-answering datasets fell into two camps, and the paper argues both fall short as LLM benchmarks. One camp is scraped from consumer sources — webMedQA, cMedQA-v1/v2, ChiMed, Huatuo-26M — open-ended, doctor-answered forum questions that need human review to trust and rarely come with a single objectively correct answer. The other camp is built from research literature, textbooks, or exams, which the authors treat as inherently more reliable; in Chinese, the only prior entry here is MLEC-QA, drawn from the same Chinese National Medical Licensing Examination (CNMLE) as CMExam itself. But MLEC-QA offers only the multiple-choice questions — no solution explanations to probe reasoning, and no annotation scheme beyond the bare question.

English-language exam-based sets (MedQA, MedMCQA, MMLU, Singhal et al.'s MultiMedQA) don't close this gap either: the paper's own stated reason non-English medical QA lags is resource inequality, not translatability — “these challenges are particularly amplified in non-English languages, such as Chinese, due to the pervasive inequality in language resources that exists in the NLP field.” The paper draws its sharpest explicit contrast not against the English sets but against MLEC-QA, the one other Chinese dataset built from the same CNMLE exam: both exceed 50K questions (Table 1 marks both with its >50K-question dagger), so scale is not the differentiator the paper claims. Instead, in its own words, CMExam “has been meticulously crafted as a foundational benchmark dataset. It introduces question explanations for reasoning ability inspection, incorporates expansive annotation facets with authoritative references, and includes question-wise medical competencies and difficulty ratings calculated from human performance” — none of which MLEC-QA offers. So the concrete gap CMExam targets is a Chinese, exam-sourced (hence reliable) dataset that is gradable in an open-ended way and stratifiable along authoritative external taxonomies, not merely a larger multiple-choice pool.

// how it works

The method

The authors collected 96,161 CNMLE questions from the internet, then dropped anything that leaned on non-textual information — questions referencing an image or table, or containing the keywords "graph"/"table" — and removed duplicates, leaving 68,119 questions. Each question carries an ID, up to five candidate answers (65,950 are single-choice, 2,169 allow multiple correct answers), and a correct answer; 85.24% also carry a brief solution explanation. Because the CNMLE itself publishes no official explanations, the authors cross-referenced questions against three online exam-prep platforms to recover them. The corpus splits 8:1:1 into train/dev/test (54,497 / 6,811 / 6,811 questions), and only the test split receives the paper's five additional annotation layers.

Those five dimensions are each tied to an external, citable taxonomy rather than invented for the dataset: disease group from the WHO's ICD-11 (27 categories), clinical department from China's Directory of Medical Institution Diagnostic and Therapeutic Categories (36 categories), medical discipline from the Ministry of Education's List of Graduate Education Disciplinary Majors (7 categories), medical competency (4 categories devised in-house by two of the paper's medical-professional co-authors to describe the skill a question tests), and difficulty level (5 tiers, computed — not judged — from the correctness rate of real human exam-takers whose response data was collected alongside the questions). Coverage on the test set is partial for the two taxonomy-matched dimensions: 65.97% of test questions (4,493 of 6,811) got a disease-group label and 72.90% (4,965 of 6,811) a clinical-department label, with the rest marked "N/A" where neither taxonomy fit; discipline, competency, and difficulty are populated for every test question.

Annotation itself is two-stage: GPT-4 produces an initial pass (the paper calls this "GPT-Assisted Annotation"), after testing showed that omitting the ICD-11 category list from the prompt or batching multiple questions per GPT call both degraded accuracy, so the final pipeline supplies explicit category instructions and processes questions with strict output-format guidance. A single expert physician (over two years of clinical experience, from the Second Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University School of Medicine) and one doctoral student from the same institution then review and correct GPT-4's labels; where the two disagree, "a collaborative discussion shall be initiated" until they reach consensus. No inter-annotator agreement statistic is reported for either stage.

// what they found

The results

Models are scored on two tasks: multiple-choice answer prediction (accuracy, weighted F1) and open-ended solution-explanation generation (BLEU-1/4, ROUGE-1/2/L against the single reference explanation). The lineup spans general-domain LLMs used zero-shot (GPT-3.5-turbo 175B, GPT-4 with parameter count undisclosed, ChatGLM-6B, LLaMA-7B, Vicuna-7B, Alpaca-7B), off-the-shelf medical-domain LLMs (Huatuo-7B, MedAlpaca-7B, DoctorGLM-6B), and eleven models the authors themselves fine-tuned directly on the CMExam training set, spanning encoder-only (BERT-0.1B, RoBERTa-0.3B), seq2seq (PromptCLUE-0.1B, Bart-base/large-0.1B), and the same set of 7B/6B decoder-only models retrained with supervision.

On prediction, zero-shot GPT-4 is the best model at 61.6±0.1% accuracy and 61.7±0.1% weighted F1 — a real gap below the 71.6% human-performance baseline (a weighted average of actual exam-taker response accuracy), but a smaller gap than GPT-3.5-turbo's, which trails at 46.4±0.6%/46.1±0.7% (10.0 points below human for GPT-4 versus 25.2 points for GPT-3.5). Fine-tuning pays off cheaply: ChatGLM-CMExam (6B parameters) reaches 45.3±1.4% accuracy — "comparable to GPT-3.5's performance," in the paper's words — using roughly 3% of GPT-3.5's parameter count (6B/175B ≈ 3.4%). Encoder-only fine-tunes are a notably strong, cheap baseline for this classification-style task: RoBERTa-CMExam (0.3B) reaches 37.1±0.1% and BERT-CMExam (0.1B) reaches 31.8±0.2%, both beating several much larger decoder-only fine-tunes such as LLaMA-CMExam (18.3±0.5%, 7B) and Alpaca-CMExam (21.1±0.6%, 7B). A random baseline scores 3.1±0.2%.

The explanation-generation ranking inverts the prediction ranking. Of the eleven CMExam-fine-tuned models, two (BERT-CMExam, RoBERTa-CMExam) are prediction-only and report no BLEU/ROUGE at all; GPT-4 and GPT-3.5 both score below all nine of the remaining fine-tuned models on every one of the five reasoning metrics — GPT-4's BLEU-1 (0.17±0.00) and BLEU-4 (0.06±0.00) are among the lowest of any model reported, similar to off-the-shelf Huatuo's zero-shot scores (0.21±0.00/0.12±0.00) and above only MedAlpaca's near-total collapse (0.00±0.00/0.00±0.00); its ROUGE-1/2/L (29.74/14.84/11.51) sit mid-pack among zero-shot models but still below all nine reasoning-scored CMExam-tuned variants. The authors attribute the weak BLEU/ROUGE showing to GPT-3.5/4's tendency to write short explanations rather than ones that lexically match the single reference. The best reasoning scores instead belong to the CMExam-tuned lightweight models: ChatGLM-CMExam tops BLEU-1 (31.10±0.23), BLEU-4 (18.94±0.12), ROUGE-2 (31.48±0.14), and ROUGE-L (29.39±0.14), while LLaMA-CMExam edges ahead on ROUGE-1 (45.88±0.04 versus ChatGLM-CMExam's 43.94±0.28). DoctorGLM could not be scored on prediction at all (the paper notes it "is unable to return answer options according to the prompt") and is reported only on the reasoning task.

Stratifying by the annotation dimensions surfaces uneven coverage. By disease group (Table 4, the 15 ICD-11 disease groups with >100 test questions — out of 27 in the full taxonomy — plus an N/A bucket for questions the taxonomy does not fit), GPT-4's accuracy ranges from 74.4% (Neoplasms) down to 44.3% (Traditional Medicine Disease Patterns); GPT-3.5 ranges 63.9%→31.0% on the same two categories. By clinical department (Table 5, the 14 clinical departments with >50 test questions — out of 36 in the full taxonomy — again plus an N/A bucket), average accuracy across the four tracked models ranges from 50.9% (Emergency Medicine) down to 13.9% (Intensive Care Medicine, a small-count category with a correspondingly large standard deviation). By discipline (Table 6), Traditional Chinese Medicine, Traditional Chinese Pharmacy, and Pharmacy are the three weakest categories, each averaging below 42% across the four models — consistent with these general-purpose LLMs having limited exposure to TCM-specific corpora. By difficulty (Table 8, tiers computed from human performance), every one of the four tracked models declines monotonically from Easy (56.5% average) to Extremely difficult (19.1% average), mirroring the human difficulty ordering by construction.

// does it hold up?

The eval read

The most striking thing in CMExam's own results table is that its two grading tasks disagree about which model is best. GPT-4 is the clear prediction leader (61.6% accuracy, well above every other model) but scores below all nine CMExam-fine-tuned models that report reasoning scores on every reasoning metric — the other two fine-tuned models, BERT-CMExam and RoBERTa-CMExam, are prediction-only and report no BLEU/ROUGE — and its BLEU scores in particular (0.17/0.06) are among the lowest reported, on par with off-the-shelf Huatuo's and above only MedAlpaca's near-zero collapse. The paper is upfront that this is a metric artifact, not a capability finding: GPT-4 and GPT-3.5 "generated reasonable answers on the answer reasoning task despite low BLEU and ROUGE scores... because they tended to generate short answers with reasonable quality," and the Limitations section concedes "BLEU and ROUGE metrics primarily evaluate the explanation task, but these measures are insufficient for assessing the reasonableness of the answer." That is the correct diagnosis, but it means the paper's own headline reasoning leaderboard — where lightweight CMExam-tuned models sweep BLEU-1, BLEU-4, ROUGE-2, and ROUGE-L — is a ranking of reference-string overlap, not of explanation quality, for exactly the models it's being used to distinguish.

The paper does partially check this with a small human study (Appendix A.4): medical experts hand-verified 50 questions each model answered correctly, sorting the accompanying explanation as reasonable, irrelevant, repeated, or inaccurate. GPT-4 and GPT-3.5 both clear roughly 45+/50 "reasonable" (over 90%, per the paper), versus roughly 40–41/50 for ChatGLM and ChatGLM-CMExam — the opposite ordering from the BLEU/ROUGE table. That is a genuinely useful sanity check, but it is thin evidence to lean on: a single 50-item sample, restricted to questions the model already answered correctly — which for the four models in this study means it says nothing about explanation quality on the ~38-74% of questions where they are wrong (accuracy ranges from GPT-4's 61.6% down to zero-shot ChatGLM's 26.3±0.0%, per Table 3) — and scored by an unspecified number of "medical experts" with no inter-rater agreement reported.

The paper's own Appendix A.5 runs a more direct control for the same metric-artifact argument: giving GPT-4 few-shot examples lifts its BLEU-1 from 0.17 to 5.95 and BLEU-4 from 0.06 to 2.25 (chain-of-thought prompting lifts BLEU-1 further, to 7.29), and lifts ROUGE-1/2/L to 37.24/19.23/17.24 — while prediction accuracy barely moves (61.6%→62.0%). GPT-3.5 shows the same pattern (few-shot BLEU-1/4 3.56→14.62 / 1.49→4.80, ROUGE-1/2/L to 38.08/18.35/18.37). That is strong, paper-internal evidence that the BLEU/ROUGE gap is about output format rather than underlying capability — though it does not fully close: even GPT-4's few-shot ROUGE-1 (37.24) still trails PromptCLUE-CMExam's zero-shot 40.88, the lowest ROUGE-1 among the nine reasoning-scored CMExam-fine-tuned models.

Two further gaps are worth flagging. First, contamination: CMExam's questions are real, publicly available CNMLE exam questions "collected from the Internet," with the paper's own footnotes naming three Chinese exam-prep sites as sources. That is exactly the kind of static, scraped, license-free web content that plausibly leaks into the pretraining corpora of Chinese-capable LLMs, and the train/dev/test split is a random 8:1:1 partition of the same scraped pool rather than a temporal holdout by exam year — so a model that memorized any of these exact questions during pretraining would show inflated accuracy that this splitting procedure cannot detect. The paper runs no decontamination or overlap check. Second, the annotation layer itself — the paper's chief claimed novelty — rests on exactly two human reviewers (one physician with "over two years" of clinical experience, one doctoral student) resolving disagreements by discussion to consensus rather than by any measured agreement statistic; no Cohen's kappa or similar figure appears anywhere in the paper for either the GPT-4 pre-annotation or the human review pass.

baselines

Broad and well-chosen in architecture (encoder-only, seq2seq, decoder-only, 0.1B–175B, general and medical domain), but the two headline proprietary models are frozen mid-2023 snapshots (GPT-3.5-turbo, GPT-4-0314) that have since been superseded, so the leaderboard is a fine fine-tuning ablation but a stale capability ranking.

contamination

Not measured. Questions are scraped from public Chinese exam-prep websites and randomly split rather than held out by exam year, so memorized test items would go undetected.

what's missed

BLEU/ROUGE penalize concise-but-correct explanations, which the paper's own GPT-4 row demonstrates; the one human check that catches this covers only 50 correctly-answered items per model, with no reported inter-annotator agreement for either that check or the five-dimension annotation layer itself.

would raise confidence

An n-gram or membership-style decontamination audit; a reported annotator-agreement statistic (κ) for both the annotation review and the explanation-quality spot check; a larger, answer-agnostic explanation-quality sample; and a refresh against current model snapshots.

// bottom line

CMExam's lasting contribution is the paired MCQ-plus-explanation format and the five externally-anchored annotation dimensions for stratified analysis — not a leaderboard whose top rows are already-deprecated API snapshots and whose reasoning metric actively contradicts the paper's own human-expert spot check.

// takeaways

What to remember

  1. CMExam pairs 68,119 real Chinese National Medical Licensing Examination questions with both a gradeable multiple-choice answer and a free-text explanation, then layers five test-set annotations — ICD-11 disease group, clinical department, discipline, competency, difficulty — each tied to an external authoritative taxonomy rather than dataset-specific labels.
  2. Zero-shot GPT-4 leads every reported model on prediction accuracy (61.6%) but still trails aggregated human performance (71.6%) by 10 points; fine-tuning a much smaller model directly on CMExam closes most of that gap cheaply — ChatGLM-CMExam (6B) reaches 45.3% accuracy, comparable to GPT-3.5-turbo (46.4%, 175B) at roughly 3% of the parameter count.
  3. The paper's own explanation-quality leaderboard runs backwards from its prediction leaderboard: GPT-4 and GPT-3.5 score below all nine CMExam-fine-tuned models that report reasoning scores (two others, BERT-CMExam and RoBERTa-CMExam, are prediction-only) on every reasoning metric, because reference-based n-gram metrics penalize their concise, non-matching explanations — a limitation the authors acknowledge and only partially probe with a 50-question, correct-answers-only human spot check.
