Towards Conversational Diagnostic AI

Google's AMIE — a PaLM 2 model instruction-tuned on real and self-play-simulated diagnostic dialogue — goes head-to-head with 20 board-certified primary care physicians in a blinded, randomized remote OSCE across 149 patient-actor scenarios. It's not a benchmark paper in the usual sense; it's the evaluation protocol itself — a rubric-driven, dual-rater (specialist + patient-actor) OSCE framework — that makes this a load-bearing reference point for anyone building or judging clinical-dialogue agents.

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

The physician-patient dialogue is where most diagnoses actually happen — the paper cites estimates that 60–80% of diagnoses are made through clinical history-taking alone in some settings — yet skilled history-taking is "episodic and globally scarce." If an LLM could reliably run that conversation, it could widen access to diagnostic expertise. The problem is that essentially all prior evidence for LLM medical competence came from a different task entirely: static, single-turn formats. Med-PaLM and Med-PaLM 2 were graded on USMLE-style multiple-choice questions and long-form QA; general-purpose models were graded on the same. None of that measures whether a model can run a diagnostic interview — actively asking questions, building a differential under uncertainty, and maintaining rapport — because in a multiple-choice exam the clinical information has already been collected and handed to the model. The paper's own framing is explicit about this distinction: prior AI-vs-clinician comparisons "typically involved both AI and physicians interpreting the same fixed input," whereas a live consultation requires the system to acquire the information itself.

A second, evaluation-specific gap sits alongside the modeling one: prior medical-dialogue datasets such as MedDialog and MedDG were, at best, evaluated on narrow axes like fluency, relevance, or human-likeness — not the kind of structured, multi-axis, dual-rater rubric that medical education already uses to certify human clinicians. Where a genuine assessment framework did exist, the paper points to a multi-agent framework for scoring LLM conversational capability that was restricted to dermatology, had AI models play both doctor and patient, and evaluated history-taking only as "complete" or not — no equivalent of the Objective Structured Clinical Examination (OSCE) had been applied. Without that scaffolding, "an LLM sounded empathetic" or "got the diagnosis right" are impossible to compare across systems or against a same-task human baseline. AMIE's OSCE-style study is offered as a first attempt at closing that specific gap — not by inventing a new model architecture, but by building the simulated-patient/rubric/rater apparatus needed to make a head-to-head physician comparison legible.

// how it works

The method

AMIE is built on the base LLM PaLM 2, instruction fine-tuned on a mixture of real and simulated data: MedQA (11,450 training / 1,273 test USMLE-style questions, plus 191 questions with clinician-written chain-of-thought), 64 long-form questions from HealthSearchQA/LiveQA/MedicationQA, 65 clinician-written summaries of MIMIC-III notes, and — the largest real-world component — 98,919 de-identified audio transcripts of actual doctor-patient visits (89,027 for training, 9,892 held out), spanning 51 specialties and 168 conditions.

Because real transcripts are noisy and can't cover the full space of conditions, the authors built a self-play simulated-dialogue environment with two nested loops. In the inner loop, three AMIE-played agents — a patient agent, a doctor agent, and a moderator that detects when the conversation has naturally concluded — hold a turn-by-turn consultation seeded by a patient vignette; a fourth AMIE-played critic agent then scores the doctor agent's performance (empathy, question economy, factual grounding, whether at least two differential diagnoses were pursued) and the doctor agent revises twice more against that feedback, keeping its dialogue history each round. In the outer loop, the resulting refined dialogues are folded back into the next fine-tuning iteration, so each new AMIE checkpoint plays a better doctor agent in the next round of self-play. Each iteration produced 11,686 simulated dialogues spanning 5,230 conditions — 4 dialogues each from 613 common conditions (from a Health QA source) and 2 dialogues each from 4,617 rarer conditions (drawn from MalaCards and MedicineNet), with an average simulated conversation length of 21.28 turns. Patient vignettes themselves were synthesized by prompting AMIE with web-search passages on a condition's demographics, symptoms, and management, filtered for relevance by PaLM 2.

At inference time, AMIE uses a three-step chain-of-reasoning before every response: (1) analyze the conversation so far to summarize symptoms/history, produce a running differential, and flag missing information and urgency; (2) formulate the next question or, if confident, present the differential and any escalation recommendation; (3) refine that draft for factual accuracy, avoidance of repetition, and empathetic, well-formatted delivery.

The comparison arm was a randomized, double-blind, crossover remote OSCE: 149 case scenarios sourced from clinical providers in Canada (60 scenario packs), India (75), and the UK (14, via the MRCPUK sample bank), covering cardiovascular (29), respiratory (30), gastroenterology (31), neurology (30), urology/obstetrics/gynecology (15), and internal medicine (14) — pediatrics, psychiatry, ICU, and inpatient case management were excluded entirely. 20 board-certified PCPs (3–25 years post-residency experience, median 7) and 20 trained patient actors (10 each from Canada and India) took part; each of the 149 simulated patients had one synchronous text-chat consultation with a PCP and one with AMIE, in randomized, blinded order, capped at 20 minutes, with neither OSCE agent allowed to reveal whether it was human.

Three rubrics anchor the questionnaires, each adapted from real clinical-assessment instruments rather than invented for this paper: the UK General Medical Council's Patient Questionnaire (GMCPQ), used for physician revalidation; the Practical Assessment of Clinical Examination Skills (PACES), part of the UK's MRCP postgraduate exam; and a checklist built from published Patient-Centered Communication Best Practice (PCCBP) criteria. Patient actors filled in the complete GMCPQ, the PACES items for "Managing Patient Concerns" and "Maintaining Patient Welfare," and the PCCBP "Fostering the Relationship" checklist (26 rated axes total). Specialist physicians — masked to which transcript was AMIE's — separately rated the full PACES and PCCBP rubrics (all six PCCBP categories: relationship fostering, gathering/providing information, decision making, enabling disease/treatment behavior, responding to emotions) plus a Diagnosis & Management rubric covering differential-diagnosis quality, management-plan appropriateness, escalation, investigations, treatment, follow-up, and confabulation (32 rated axes total), and additionally ranked the top-10 differential diagnoses for accuracy against the scenario's ground truth.

// what they found

The results

Diagnostic accuracy (specialist-rated, top-k differential diagnosis, n=149 scenarios). AMIE's top-k accuracy was significantly higher than PCPs' at every k from 1 to 10 (bootstrap, n=10,000 resamples, FDR-corrected, p < 0.05 throughout). Reading directly off Figure 3: matched against the single ground-truth diagnosis (panel a), AMIE scored roughly 77% at top-1 versus roughly 65% for PCPs, converging to roughly 93% vs. roughly 83% by top-10. Matched against the full accepted differential (panel b, a more lenient criterion), AMIE scored roughly 85% at top-1 versus roughly 73% for PCPs, and roughly 93% vs. roughly 83% by top-10. Note this isn't quite an apples-to-apples list length: AMIE always returned a 10-item differential, while PCPs did not (minimum 3, mean 5.39 diagnoses).

To separate where the diagnostic edge came from, the authors re-ran AMIE's differential-diagnosis step on the PCPs' own consultation transcripts (using a Med-PaLM 2-based auto-evaluator) and found AMIE's diagnoses were about as good whether it worked from its own dialogue or from the PCP's — implying AMIE and PCPs were roughly equivalent at acquiring clinical information from the patient actor, but AMIE was better at reasoning over that information into a complete, accurate differential. Consistent with this, conversational turn counts and the number of words elicited from patient actors were similar for both agents, though AMIE's own responses were considerably longer.

Conversation quality, by rater group and axis (n up to 149 per axis; exclusions reduce n where a rater marked "cannot rate"). AMIE was rated significantly better than PCPs (p < 0.05, Wilcoxon signed-rank, FDR-corrected) on 24 of 26 patient-actor-rated axes (GMCPQ, the patient-actor PACES subset, and the PCCBP relationship-fostering checklist) and on 28 of 32 specialist-rated axes (full PACES, full PCCBP, and Diagnosis & Management). Two separate auto-evaluators corroborate the human ratings. A Med-PaLM 2-based DDx auto-evaluator reproduced the same top-k differential-diagnosis accuracy trend seen with specialist raters. Independently, a self-CoT AMIE-based rater — validated by rank-order agreement against the specialist ratings — scored dialogue quality, and using that same rater the authors confirmed that the inner self-play loop's critic-refined simulated dialogues were preferred over the pre-refinement baseline dialogues, evidence that the self-play procedure itself was improving dialogue quality, not just giving AMIE more data to memorize.

// does it hold up?

The eval read

The study is unusually well-instrumented for a clinical-AI paper — a genuine randomized, blinded, crossover design with dual rater groups and rubrics borrowed from real medical-education instruments (GMCPQ, PACES, PCCBP) rather than an ad hoc Likert scale. But the authors' own limitations section does most of the hard work of qualifying the headline result, and it's worth reading the eval design as closely as the numbers.

The comparison is not on neutral ground. AMIE was purpose-built and self-play-trained specifically for synchronous multi-turn text chat, while the 20 PCPs — board-certified, median 7 years post-residency — were "unfamiliar" with this exact modality: the paper notes physicians are more accustomed to telephone or video consultation for real-time history-taking, and to episodic text/SMS or email (not synchronous chat) for asynchronous needs like prescription refills. PCPs got exactly two preparatory pilot sessions to acclimate — the paper is explicit that this "was not a formal training program, nor was it designed to optimize clinicians' performance" — while AMIE got an entire training pipeline optimized for the task. The paper states this plainly: the study "should not be regarded as representative of usual practice in (tele)medicine," and it's plausible the PCPs' apparent underperformance partly reflects an unfamiliar-format penalty rather than a pure diagnostic-skill or communication-skill gap.

Blinding was also imperfect in a way that could bias the conversation-quality ratings specifically: the paper concedes "the style of responses from AMIE was notably different to that by PCPs, which limits the practical extent of blinding," and separately flags that AMIE's responses were "significantly longer... and presented with greater structure" than PCPs' — a stylistic tell that the authors themselves note could inflate empathy/rapport scores independent of any real difference in care quality (citing prior findings that patient satisfaction tracks time spent, i.e. verbosity can read as diligence). Since empathy and communication axes make up the majority of both the 26 patient-actor-rated axes and the 32 specialist-rated axes, this confound sits under a large share of the "28 of 32" and "24 of 26" headline claims — the paper reports which axes were statistically significant but does not report effect sizes for how large each favorable margin was, so a reader can't tell how much of the win is decisive versus narrow.

The most important nuance the headline numbers omit: on four of the specialist-rated Diagnosis & Management axes — Escalation Recommendation Appropriate, Treatment Inappropriate Avoided, Followup Recommendation Appropriate, and, notably, Confabulation Absent — there was no significant difference between AMIE and PCPs (all N=149, no exclusions). "AMIE beat PCPs on 28 of 32 axes" is true, but it obscures that on the safety-critical subset of that rubric — whether the model made things up, over-escalated, or recommended the wrong follow-up — it was merely on par with human physicians, not better. For a system being evaluated as a candidate diagnostic interviewer, "confabulates about as often as a PCP" is a very different sentence than "outperforms PCPs across the board," and it deserves at least as much emphasis as the top-k accuracy chart.

There's no contamination risk in the usual train/test-leakage sense — the 149 OSCE scenarios are freshly authored clinical vignettes (from two OSCE labs plus MRCPUK samples), not scraped benchmark items AMIE could have memorized. The more relevant validity concern is ecological: simulated patient actors, however well-trained, may present symptoms with more structure and completeness than real patients do, and the study explicitly excludes pediatrics, psychiatry, ICU, inpatient, and multi-morbidity scenarios — exactly the messier cases where diagnostic dialogue is hardest and where a text-chat-only, single-encounter design is least representative of real practice.

baselines

Fair in credentials (board-certified PCPs, median 7 years' experience) but not in task-familiarity: PCPs were novices at synchronous text-chat consultation (two pilot sessions), while AMIE was self-play-trained specifically for it — a modality mismatch the authors themselves flag as a major caveat.

contamination

Not a leakage risk — the 149 scenarios are freshly authored OSCE vignettes, not public benchmark items. The bigger validity gap is ecological: trained patient actors and a text-only interface, not real patients or real clinical settings.

what's missed

The "28/32" and "24/26" topline hides that on four safety-relevant Diagnosis & Management axes — including whether the model confabulated — AMIE was statistically tied with PCPs, not better. No effect sizes are reported, only significance, so the size of each favorable margin is unknown; and AMIE's greater response length is a plausible confound for the empathy/rapport ratings specifically.

would raise confidence

A PCP arm given equivalent task-specific training/familiarization with the chat interface; reported effect sizes (not just p-values) per axis; a real-patient or independently-run replication outside Google; and a version of the confabulation/escalation analysis broken out as a primary result rather than folded into an aggregate axis count.

// bottom line

AMIE is a genuine advance in evaluation methodology for conversational medical AI — a rigorous, dual-rater OSCE framework that's harder to game than a static QA benchmark — and the result (higher differential-diagnosis accuracy, better rated communication) is real within that framework. But it is explicitly a research system tested in a simulated OSCE with actors, not a clinically validated tool, and the paper's own limitations section — modality mismatch for PCPs, imperfect blinding, no reported effect sizes, and statistical parity on confabulation and escalation — matters as much as the top-line axis counts for judging what this result actually licenses.

// takeaways

What to remember

  1. AMIE, a PaLM 2 model trained via self-play on simulated diagnostic dialogue, out-diagnosed 20 board-certified PCPs in a blinded, randomized, 149-scenario remote OSCE — significantly higher top-k differential-diagnosis accuracy at every k from 1 to 10 (specialist-rated), and rated better on 28 of 32 specialist-rated and 24 of 26 patient-actor-rated conversation-quality axes.
  2. The edge looks like better reasoning, not better information-gathering: AMIE and PCPs acquired similar information from patient actors (similar turns, similar patient word counts), but AMIE was more accurate when both were later scored on the differential diagnosis they produced from that same information.
  3. The comparison isn't a level playing field, and the paper says so: PCPs were novices at the synchronous text-chat format AMIE was purpose-trained for, AMIE's longer/more structured responses are a plausible confound for the empathy ratings, and on the safety-relevant confabulation/escalation/follow-up axes AMIE was statistically tied with — not better than — PCPs. This is a milestone in evaluation methodology for conversational diagnostic AI, run entirely in a simulated OSCE with actors — not evidence of real-world clinical readiness.
