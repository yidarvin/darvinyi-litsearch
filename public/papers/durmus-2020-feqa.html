FEQA: A Question Answering Evaluation Framework for Faithfulness Assessment in Abstractive Summarization

A QA-based faithfulness metric for abstractive summarization: mask a span in the summary, generate a question for it, then check whether an off-the-shelf QA model recovers the same answer from the source document. Non-matched answers mean the summary said something the source didn't.

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

Neural abstractive summarizers are good at fluency and content selection but bad at staying honest: prior work had already estimated that roughly 30% of generated summaries contain unfaithful information (Cao et al., 2018; Falke et al., 2019; Kryściński et al., 2019), and the standard evaluation stack — ROUGE, BLEU, BERTScore — has no way to catch it, because as conventionally used, all three compare the output against a reference summary, not against the source document. A summary can match the reference's words closely while still hallucinating a fact the source never stated, and none of these metrics would notice.

The paper's own diagnosis sharpens the problem before proposing a fix. It defines four abstractiveness categories for a summary sentence — sentence extraction, span extraction, word extraction, and k-way perfect fusion — and measures them, plus the share of novel n-grams, across five summarization systems (PGC, FASTRL, BOTTOMUP, TCONV, BERTSUM) on CNN/DM and XSum. Then it crowd-sources grammaticality annotations on 1,000 sampled sentence-source pairs per model-dataset pair, and faithfulness annotations on a further 200-sentence random subsample of whichever of those were judged meaningful (with a hierarchical decision process — Figure 1 — designed to separate nonsensical output, minor grammar issues, and genuine unfaithfulness, since the authors found raw inter-annotator agreement on faithfulness alone was unreliable). The result: faithfulness tracks abstractiveness. Near-extractive CNN/DM outputs are mostly faithful; highly abstractive XSum outputs are not — PGC and TCONV each have faithfulness errors in more than half the sentences they generate on XSum. Existing automatic metrics, built around surface overlap or embedding similarity, are exactly the wrong tool for catching this, because the failures show up precisely where overlap with any reference text is lowest. Prior QA-based attempts at automatic evaluation (Eyal et al., 2019; Scialom et al., 2019; Chen et al., 2018) existed, but were built around cloze-style questions — masking an entity and asking a fill-in-the-blank question — which the paper argues limits both the questions' expressiveness and which QA models can answer them.

// how it works

The method

FEQA (Faithfulness Evaluation with Question Answering) turns faithfulness checking into answer verification, in three steps, run per summary sentence:

1. Mask key spans in the summary sentence. A constituency parser (Kitaev and Klein, 2018) extracts noun phrases and the Stanford CoreNLP NER model extracts named entities; each such span becomes a candidate "gold answer."

2. Generate a question for each masked span. Rather than a cloze-style fill-in-the-blank, FEQA fine-tunes a pretrained BART model to turn a declarative sentence with a masked span into a real question — e.g. "Sally was born in <m>1958</m>" → "When was Sally born?" The question generator is trained by adapting the QA2D dataset (Demszky et al., 2018), which pairs declarative sentences with questions in the opposite direction. Because the declarative-to-question transformation is close to rule-based, the authors expect it to generalize across domains without much paraphrasing drift.

3. Verify each answer against the source. An off-the-shelf reading-comprehension model — BERT-base fine-tuned on SQuAD 1.1 or SQuAD 2.0 — answers each generated question using the source document instead of the summary. The paper takes the average F1 between these document-derived answers and the summary-derived "gold" answers as the sentence's faithfulness score; with SQuAD 2.0, the QA model may hypothesize that a question is unanswerable, which the paper treats as equivalent to getting the answer wrong (i.e. unfaithful). This step is metric-agnostic — the authors emphasize FEQA places no constraint on which QA model is used, so its accuracy rides on whatever off-the-shelf reading-comprehension system is plugged in.

FEQA(sentence) = 1|Q| Σq∈Q F1( Adoc(q), Agold(q) ) // Q = questions generated from masked spans in the summary sentence; A_gold = the masked span itself; A_doc = the QA model's answer extracted from the source document

Figure 1 documents the human-annotation protocol that produces the labels FEQA is validated against: annotators first judge whether a sentence is meaningful at all, then whether it's grammatical, and only meaningful sentences are passed on to a faithfulness judgment (further split into "hallucination" vs. "contradiction" when unfaithful). This hierarchy is what lets the paper separate three different failure modes that a flat faithfulness question would otherwise conflate.

// what they found

The results

The abstractiveness-faithfulness tradeoff shows up cleanly in the human annotations (Table 4). On CNN/DM, faithfulness scores range 64.17–95.03 across the four models; on XSum, they drop to 38.96–60.54. PGC and TCONV each fall below 50% faithfulness-score on XSum, meaning the majority of their sampled sentences are judged unfaithful. BERTSUM is the strongest model on both datasets (95.03 CNN/DM, 60.54 XSum), and PGC is the only other model run on both (70.05 CNN/DM, 40.33 XSum) — both of the two models tested on both datasets drop sharply moving to XSum, so the tradeoff isn't confined to the weaker models.

FEQA is the strict maximum in all four columns, leading the next-best metric by at least 10.65 points everywhere — its narrowest margin is on XSum Spearman (FEQA 21.34 vs. BERTScore's 10.69), its widest on CNN/DM Pearson (32.01 vs. ROUGE-2's 13.25). More strikingly, on XSum, FEQA is the only one of the eight metrics whose correlation reaches significance at all (both at p<0.001); every word-overlap and embedding-based metric's XSum correlation is statistically indistinguishable from zero, and several (ROUGE-2, ROUGE-L, BLEU-4) are actually negative. The word-overlap metrics that do work on CNN/DM (all marked ** there) collapse entirely once the summaries stop copying from the source — exactly the abstractive regime the paper set out to target. The entailment metric never reaches significance on either dataset, consistent with the paper's reading that off-the-shelf NLI models trained on "current entailment datasets" rely on lexical-overlap heuristics (McCoy et al., 2019) that make them unreliable for distinguishing faithful from unfaithful abstractive output.

A separate check (Table 8) probes whether content selection and faithfulness are the same thing: correlating human faithfulness scores against ROUGE computed between the output and the reference summary (rather than the output and the source) still reaches significance on CNN/DM (ROUGE-1 15.31**, ROUGE-2 15.10**, ROUGE-L 13.33**) — in fact slightly higher Pearson values than the source-vs-output ROUGE rows in Table 7 (12.02, 13.25, 12.58) — but drops to no significant correlation at all on XSum. The real separation the paper's own numbers support is on XSum, not CNN/DM: high content-selection quality (matching the reference) does not reliably imply faithfulness (matching the source) once summaries get abstractive.

The paper also runs a 100-sample manual audit of FEQA's own machinery: roughly 94% of generated questions are judged grammatical and correctly targeted at the mask, and for 78% of questions the QA system behaves as intended (correctly answering when the sentence is faithful, correctly failing or saying "unanswerable" otherwise). The paper attributes the majority of that remaining error to the QA model mishandling unanswerable questions (14%). Separately — and not part of the error budget — it notes that in 16% of cases the QA answer is correct but F1 under-scores it for not being an exact span match, e.g. “Donald Trump” vs. “the President of the United States Donald Trump.”

// does it hold up?

The eval read

The headline comparison is legitimate: eight metrics, same test sentences, same human labels, and FEQA wins every column by a wide margin — that part of Table 7 checks out arithmetically and the significance markers back it up (FEQA is the only metric significant on XSum at all). But the validation rests on a smaller and less-explained sample than the paper's own annotation protocol implies; its reliability self-check for the QA machinery is a single unreplicated 100-item audit; and one of its own comparative claims is refuted by its own tables.

Start with sample size. §2.3.1 says faithfulness annotation drew 200 sampled sentences per model-dataset pair — up to 4×200=800 for CNN/DM's four models, 3×200=600 for XSum's three. Table 7's correlation is computed over 748 CNN/DM pairs (93% of the ceiling — a small, plausible drop) but only 286 XSum pairs (48% of the ceiling — under half). The paper never explains where the other ~314 XSum sentences went. That matters more than it would for CNN/DM, because XSum is where the paper's headline claim lives: "the only metric that correlates... on highly abstractive summaries." A claim resting on the harder, more abstractive dataset deserves the larger sample, not the one that quietly shrank furthest.

Then there's the QA-machinery audit itself. The paper reports 78% correct QA behavior on its 100-sample manual check, and attributes most of the remaining error to unanswerable-detection failures — 14%, which it calls the majority of the QA system's errors, consistent with an implied ~22% error rate. A second, separately-counted 16% covers cases where the QA model's answer is actually correct but F1 under-scores it for not being an exact span match ("Donald Trump" vs. "the President of the United States Donald Trump") — by the paper's own definition of correct behavior, those sit inside the 78%, not the error budget, so there's no arithmetic tension between the two figures. What the audit does establish, on its own terms: on a single 100-item manual check with no reported confidence interval or second annotator, roughly 30% of sampled QA pairs show one of these two failure modes, meaning a non-trivial share of any individual sentence's FEQA score is QA/scoring noise rather than a verdict on summary faithfulness.

A sharper, genuinely unnamed inconsistency shows up one table over. Right after Table 8, the paper's prose asserts that its ROUGE-vs-reference correlations on CNN/DM are "significantly lower" than the ROUGE-vs-source correlations reported in Table 7. The two tables, printed six lines apart on the same page, say the opposite for Pearson: Table 8's CNN/DM values (ROUGE-1 15.31, ROUGE-2 15.10, ROUGE-L 13.33) are all higher than Table 7's source-vs-output values (12.02, 13.25, 12.58), and only two of the three Spearman comparisons run the direction the prose claims (ROUGE-1 14.92<15.86 and ROUGE-L 13.35<16.49 do; ROUGE-2 16.39>15.99 does not). What the tables do show on XSum is starker and simpler: neither ROUGE variant correlates significantly with human faithfulness — not the reference-based ones in Table 8, and not the source-based ones in Table 7, where four of the six cells are outright negative (R-1 −2.57, R-2 −5.78/−8.47, R-L −6.37/−9.68). FEQA’s 26.31 and 21.34 are the only starred XSum cells in either table.

There's also a structural limitation worth being explicit about, separate from any error in the paper: FEQA's coverage is bounded by what gets masked. Every question comes from a noun phrase or named entity inside the summary sentence itself, so the metric can only test facts that surface as an extractable span — entity substitution, wrong dates, wrong locations, wrong numbers. A distortion that doesn't localize to a single answerable span (a reversed causal relationship between two correctly-named entities, a hedged claim stated as certain, a comparison whose polarity flips) sits outside what mask-and-ask can catch, and the paper doesn't discuss this boundary.

Worth flagging separately, and to the paper's credit: on the abstractiveness-faithfulness tradeoff itself, BERTSUM is a genuine within-CNN/DM exception the paper doesn't name. Its faithfulness abstractiveness score (39.16% novel trigrams) is close to FASTRL's (49.74%) and BOTTOMUP's (42.36%), yet its faithfulness score (95.03) is far above either (68.27, 64.17). The paper's prose only credits BERTSUM with "fewer unfaithful sentences" in general — it never states that faithfulness and abstractiveness decouple for this model specifically, which is a more interesting (and slightly complicating) finding than the paper draws out.

baselines

Reasonably comprehensive for 2020 — word overlap (ROUGE, BLEU), embeddings (BERTScore), and two model-based approaches (relation extraction, NLI entailment). But entailment and relation extraction are each tested with exactly one off-the-shelf model (ESIM/AllenNLP; Stanford OpenIE), so "entailment doesn't correlate with faithfulness" is really "this one 2017-era ESIM checkpoint doesn't" — a stronger 2020 NLI model isn't tried.

contamination

Not applicable in the LLM-era sense (pre-LLM, 2020). The closest analogue is sample-composition risk: the correlation set for XSum (286) is under half the ~600 faithfulness-labeled sentences the sampling protocol could have produced, and the paper doesn't say how the subset was chosen.

what's missed

FEQA can only test what surfaces as a maskable noun phrase or entity in the summary sentence — relational/causal distortions between two individually-correct entities, or a claim whose modality or polarity flips without changing which spans are masked, are structurally invisible to it. Its 100-sample QA-reliability audit is also a single unreplicated pass: no confidence interval or second annotator, on a check that exists precisely to tell genuine summary unfaithfulness apart from QA-model noise.

would raise confidence

An explanation for the 748/286 sample gap, a second, independently-annotated pass over the 100-sample QA-reliability audit, a correction to the "significantly lower" CNN/DM claim after Table 8 (the paper's own Pearson values run the other way), a statement of which QA variant produced the headline row — §3 says the model is BERT-base fine-tuned on both SQuAD-1.1 and SQuAD-2.0, and notes that only the 2.0 model can return “unanswerable,” which it counts as a wrong answer, so the choice is load-bearing and Table 7 reports a single FEQA row without naming it; and a second entailment/RE baseline using a stronger contemporary model to rule out "weak baseline" as the reason those two metrics underperform.

// bottom line

For a 2020 reader, FEQA's core claim holds up on its own numbers: it beats seven other metrics on both datasets, and it's the only one of the eight that says anything statistically meaningful about faithfulness on highly abstractive summaries. For a 2026 reader, treat the specific correlation magnitudes as directional rather than precise — the XSum evidence is thinner than the CNN/DM evidence, the paper's own "significantly lower" claim about its Table 8 correlations doesn't hold on CNN/DM, and the QA-reliability audit is a single unreplicated 100-sample check. What matters more than the exact numbers is that FEQA established the recipe — mask a span, generate a question, verify the answer against a source — that BEGIN, FaithDial, GPTScore, G-Eval, and CRUD-RAG's RAGQuestEval all inherit, even as each of them swaps in a stronger question generator, a stronger QA/judge model, or (in G-Eval and GPTScore's case) an LLM in place of the whole QA pipeline.

// takeaways

What to remember

  1. FEQA reframes faithfulness evaluation as answer verification: mask a span in the summary sentence, generate a question for it with a BART model fine-tuned on QA2D, then check whether an off-the-shelf SQuAD-tuned QA model recovers the same answer from the source document. The average F1 across a sentence's questions is its faithfulness score.
  2. The paper's own human-annotation study establishes the premise FEQA is built to address: faithfulness collapses as summaries get more abstractive. PGC and TCONV each have faithfulness errors on more than half their sampled XSum sentences (scores 40.33 and 38.96); PGC is the only one of the two also run on CNN/DM, where it scores 70.05.
  3. On Table 7, FEQA is the strict maximum on both Pearson and Spearman correlation with human judgment across CNN/DM (32.01 / 28.23) and XSum (26.31 / 21.34), and it's the only one of eight compared metrics whose XSum correlation reaches statistical significance at all — every word-overlap metric is indistinguishable from zero there.
  4. The validation isn't airtight: the XSum correlation set (286 pairs) covers under half of what the paper's own 200-per-model sampling protocol could have produced, unexplained; the paper's own claim that its Table 8 (reference-based) ROUGE correlations are "significantly lower" than Table 7's (source-based) ones doesn't hold on CNN/DM, where Table 8's Pearson values are actually higher; and its 100-sample QA-reliability audit is a single unreplicated check with no confidence interval.
  5. FEQA is the direct methodological ancestor of the QA/LLM-judge faithfulness lineage — BEGIN, FaithDial, GPTScore, G-Eval, and CRUD-RAG's RAGQuestEval all cite it, and all keep its core structure (generate questions, verify answers against a source) while replacing its BART question generator and BERT QA model with stronger models or, later, an LLM prompted to do the whole job at once.
