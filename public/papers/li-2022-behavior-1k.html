BEHAVIOR-1K: A Benchmark for Embodied AI with 1,000 Everyday Activities and Realistic Simulation

909 of BEHAVIOR-1K's 1,000 household activities chosen by a 1,461-respondent preference survey over ~2,090 candidates (the remaining 91 carried over from its predecessor), grounded in 50 interactive scenes and 5,000+ annotated objects, instantiated in OmniGibson — a new NVIDIA Omniverse/PhysX-5 simulator built for fluids, deformables and thermal effects. On three hand-picked activities, raw visuomotor RL scores 0.00 while action-primitive RL reaches 0.42-0.88, and transferring one such policy to a real robot collapses success to 0%.

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

BEHAVIOR-1K is the direct successor to BEHAVIOR-100 (Srivastava et al., CoRL 2021), and its own Table 1 lays out the tradeoff it's trying to break: 22 comparator embodied-AI benchmarks, each strong on one axis and weak on another. Instruction-following benchmarks — VirtualHome and ALFRED — are, in the paper's own framing, "diverse in the number of scenes, objects, and state changes, but offer a limited low-level physical realism." Household-rearrangement benchmarks sit on the other side of that tradeoff: Habitat 2.0 HAB, TDW Transport, and SAPIEN ManiSkill "support realistic action execution and accurate physics simulation for rigid bodies, but only include a handful of tasks" (Habitat 2.0 HAB itself, per Table 1, spans just 1 scene / 6 rooms). SoftGym and RFUniverse are named as the two prior simulators with realism features "closest" to BEHAVIOR-1K's, but the paper says they "lack the task diversity needed to support the development of human-centered general robots."

Underneath that diversity-realism tradeoff is a more basic complaint the introduction makes explicit: benchmarks are typically "designed by researchers," with "it remains unclear if they are addressing the actual needs of humans." BEHAVIOR-1K's proposed fix is to ask people directly — a 1,461-respondent Mechanical Turk survey over roughly 2,090 candidate everyday activities — and let the response scores select which 1,000 activities the benchmark actually covers, rather than researchers picking a shortlist by hand. Read literally, the final list is not purely survey-ranked, though: the paper states it as "the 909 activities with the highest human preference scores and 91 activities from BEHAVIOR-100," so roughly 9% of BEHAVIOR-1K's activities are carried over from the predecessor benchmark rather than selected purely by where they landed in the 2,090-item ranking. The realism half of the gap — simulating the physical states (temperature, wetness, deformation) those activities require — is handed to a new simulator, OmniGibson, built because "realistic simulation of the diverse activities in BEHAVIOR-1K is beyond the capability of iGibson 2.0," the engine BEHAVIOR-100 used.

// how it works

The method

The activity list starts from roughly 2,000 candidate everyday activities sourced from time-use surveys — the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics' American Time Use Survey, the EU's Harmonised European Time Use Surveys, and the Multinational Time Use Study — plus WikiHow articles. On Amazon Mechanical Turk, a pool of 1,461 respondents produced fifty 10-point Likert-scale ratings per activity answering "how much would you benefit if a robot did this for you?", producing a ranked list of 2,090 activities with a Gini index of 0.158 (the paper reads this as evidence of "large statistical dispersion" — real variation in what people want automated, from "scrubbing the bathroom floor" at the top to game-play activities at the bottom, with roughly 200 cleaning and 200+ cooking activities represented). BEHAVIOR-1K's 1,000 activities are then the 909 highest-scoring activities from that ranking plus 91 activities retained from BEHAVIOR-100.

Each of the 1,000 activities is then given a concrete definition in BEHAVIOR Domain Definition Language (BDDL), the same predicate-logic representation BEHAVIOR-100 introduced: an activity is a pair of logical formulas — an initial-condition set and a goal-condition set — over object-state predicates (e.g. cooked(pie1), on(plate1, tablecloth1)). The object and object-state vocabulary underneath those predicates comes from 5,000 WikiHow articles mapped to 1,484 WordNet synsets, annotated (via crowdworkers, students, and GPT-3) with which physical states each object can take — e.g. an apple is tagged as cooked/sliced-capable but not toggledOn-capable, with some properties parameterized (e.g. "cook temperature" per object). Annotators and researchers additionally author transition rules — dough plus sufficient heat becomes a pie — that OmniGibson's Transition Machine later executes. Quality control is reported as "five experienced machine learning annotators verify a subset of all types of annotations," with an approval rate above 96.8%.

Sitting underneath that knowledge base is a 3D-asset library: 50 fully interactive scenes (the 15 house scenes inherited from BEHAVIOR-100 plus 35 newly acquired scenes spanning gardens, offices, restaurants, and stores) and 5,000+ object instances across 1,200+ categories (Table 1 gives the exact figures as 5,215 object models across 1,265 categories), each annotated with physical properties (friction, mass, articulation) and semantic category.

All of this is instantiated in OmniGibson, a new simulator built on NVIDIA's Omniverse and PhysX 5 — chosen, the paper says, because "realistic simulation of the diverse activities in BEHAVIOR-1K is beyond the capability of iGibson 2.0," the engine underlying BEHAVIOR-100. OmniGibson simulates not just rigid-body kinematics but deformable bodies, fluids, and flexible materials, and extends Omniverse with the same style of non-kinematic "extended object states" iGibson 2.0 introduced (temperature, soaked level, toggled, dirtiness), driven by heuristics (e.g. temperature rises near an active heat source). A modular Transition Machine handles physical processes Omniverse can't natively simulate — baking a pie, pureeing vegetables — by specifying custom transitions between object groups once specified conditions are met. The paper states plainly that "without these features, over half of BEHAVIOR-1K activities would not be simulatable." The cost of this realism is rendering speed: OmniGibson's ray/path-traced rendering runs at roughly 60 fps for a house scene of about 60 objects, versus roughly 100 fps in iGibson 2.0.

Table 1's own comparison (columns restricted here to BEHAVIOR-1K and its direct predecessor, since the full 23-benchmark table is too wide to quote reliably in full) puts the scale jump in context: BEHAVIOR-1K is the only one of the 23 compared benchmarks whose activity list is checked off as survey-grounded, at 1,000 activities against BEHAVIOR-100's 100, across 50/306 scenes/rooms (vs. 15/100) and 1,265 object categories / 5,215 models (vs. 391/1,217). On the realism axes specific to this table, BEHAVIOR-1K adds flexible materials, deformable bodies, realistic fluid, and thermal effects — all flagged as absent from BEHAVIOR-100 — while both retain kinematics/dynamics, continuous extended states, and realistic action execution.

The paper's own claim about the value of "realistic simulation" is tested with a small, separate human-subjects study, not the activity-preference survey: 60 participants rank sampled images from five simulators for visual realism on a 1 (least) to 5 (most) scale. OmniGibson scores 3.20 ± 1.23, ahead of Habitat 2.0 (1.74 ± 1.33), AI2-THOR (1.73 ± 1.37), iGibson 2.0 (1.69 ± 1.24), and ThreeDWorld (1.65 ± 1.23).

// what they found

The results

RL baselines are evaluated on three activities the paper calls "paradigmatic," not a sample of the 1,000: CollectTrash (rigid-body manipulation — gather bottles/cups, throw them in a bin), StoreDecoration (articulated-object manipulation — store items in a drawer), and CleanTable (flexible-material and fluid manipulation — wipe a table with a soaked cloth). Three methods are compared: RL-VMC (Soft Actor-Critic, end-to-end visuomotor control from image to joint commands), RL-Prim. (PPO selecting among six sampling-based-motion-planned action primitives — pick, place, push, navigate, dip, wipe), and RL-Prim.Hist. (the same, plus a 3-step observation history).

RL-VMC (visuomotor, no primitives)0.0 ± 0.00.0 ± 0.00.0 ± 0.0
RL-Prim. (action primitives)0.48 ± 0.060.42 ± 0.020.77 ± 0.08
RL-Prim.Hist. (+ 3-step history)0.55 ± 0.050.63 ± 0.030.88 ± 0.02

End-to-end visuomotor control fails completely on all three activities; switching to action primitives brings all three above 40% success, and adding history further improves every cell — most visibly on CollectTrash (0.42 → 0.63), the longest-horizon of the three (the paper notes CleanTable's optimal solution is 6 primitive steps, versus at least 16 for CollectTrash). Efficiency, measured only for CollectTrash, improves monotonically across all three methods on two of its three metrics: distance navigated (27.58m → 17.98m → 15.33m) and simulated time (16.67s → 13.95s → 12.48s). The third metric, kinematic disarrangement, only improves from RL-Prim. to RL-Prim.Hist. (12.34m → 10.82m) — RL-VMC's 0.00 there is a failure-to-act artifact rather than efficiency, since it never successfully manipulates anything (it also scores 0.00 on task success in Table 2).

An ablation isolates two simplifying assumptions baked into RL-Prim.'s primitives: an "assistive" pick that rigidly connects object to gripper once fingers make contact (rather than physically-simulated grasping), and motion primitives that check only the feasibility of a final configuration and teleport there rather than executing the full planned trajectory. Enabling full trajectory execution alone drops success only slightly (0.48/0.42/0.77 → 0.46/0.36/0.73). Going the rest of the way — also enabling fully physics-based grasping, removing the assistive pick — collapses all three activities to 0.00; the paper attributes this specifically to the grasping change, calling it "a radical performance drop after enabling fully physics-based grasping during evaluation," and states plainly that "designing a pick action primitive for arbitrary objects that leverages fully physics-based grasping is by itself an open research problem" left for future work — meaning the 42-88% success numbers above depend on an idealized, non-physical grasp.

A separate real-robot experiment transfers a CollectTrash policy to a real bimanual mobile manipulator (Tiago) in a scanned mock apartment. Three conditions, kept distinct: simulation success with the trained RL-Prim. policy (50 runs, ~40% success); real-world success with an optimal policy — a human choosing which action primitive to execute, with only motion planning and actuation automated (27 runs, ~22% success); and real-world success with the same trained RL-Prim. policy transferred as-is, using a YOLOv3 detector for object localization (26 runs, 0% success).

// does it hold up?

The eval read

BEHAVIOR-1K bundles two contributions — a survey-grounded 1,000-activity dataset and a new simulator, OmniGibson — and both claims deserve to be checked against exactly what was measured. Table 1's comparison — the paper's own phrase, "a new level of diversity" (Table 1's caption) — is built entirely on axes the authors selected for the table: survey-groundedness, scene/object counts, and a specific list of physical-realism flags (fluids, deformables, thermal effects). Elsewhere (Sec. 4) the paper separately claims "unmatched levels" of diversity and realism specifically relative to its direct predecessor BEHAVIOR-100, and calls the acquisition of 35 newly acquired scenes "unprecedented compared to other benchmarks" — a claim about scene diversity specifically, not a general claim about Table 1's comparison. Benchmarks with their own specialties — RLBench and Meta-World's precision at manipulation-skill evaluation, Habitat 2.0's rearrangement rigor — aren't given credit on axes outside this table's chosen dimensions; "a new level of diversity" is true relative to this table's own axes, not a claim the paper makes about every property a benchmark could have.

The "human-centered" framing is largely earned but not absolute: 909 of the 1,000 activities are the top scorers from the 1,461-respondent survey, but the remaining 91 are carried over from BEHAVIOR-100 regardless of where they land in that ranking — a design choice the paper states plainly rather than hides, but one that means roughly 9% of the benchmark's activity list is grounded in continuity with prior work rather than in this survey's scores.

The "realistic simulation" claim rests on two different kinds of evidence that shouldn't be conflated. The engineering claim (OmniGibson simulates fluids, deformables, and thermal effects that iGibson 2.0 couldn't) is a capability description, testable by inspection. The perceptual claim — that it therefore looks more realistic — is measured, via a 60-subject survey scoring rendered stills 1-5 (OmniGibson 3.20 vs. 1.65-1.74 for four comparators). That's real evidence, but it's a perceptual-realism score on static images, not a quantitative validation that OmniGibson's fluid or cloth dynamics match real physical behavior, and the main text doesn't describe how images were sampled or matched across simulators (viewpoint, lighting) for a fair comparison — that detail, if it exists, is in Appendix E.2, which isn't part of this PMLR proceedings PDF.

The RL results are informative about a narrow slice of the benchmark: three "paradigmatic" activities, hand-picked by the authors, out of 1,000 — 0.3% of the activity set. That end-to-end visuomotor RL scores exactly 0.00 on all three, and that RL-Prim. — the only method the grasping ablation was actually run on — sees its own 42-77% success collapse back to 0.00 the moment physically-realistic grasping replaces the assistive pick, are real and well-documented findings about those three activities specifically (the higher-scoring RL-Prim.Hist. variant, which reaches 0.88 on CleanTable, was never evaluated under physics-based grasping) — the paper never claims these findings generalize to the other 997 activities, and the reader shouldn't either. Two further details are unreported in this main text (and may sit in Appendix F, not included in this PDF): how many training seeds or evaluation episodes the ± spreads in Tables 2-4 are computed over, and whether the three baseline activities were trained/evaluated on a single fixed scene instantiation or across OmniGibson's supported randomized instantiations.

The real-robot section is the paper's most careful piece of scoping — it explicitly separates a human-driven "optimal policy" (27 runs, ~22% success) from the actual learned "trained policy" (26 runs, 0% success), and states the simulation figure (50 runs, ~40%) as a third, distinct condition. That discipline is worth crediting. But the sample is thin (roughly 25-50 runs per condition), confined to one activity (CollectTrash) and one scanned apartment, so "Characterizing the Sim-Real Gap" (Figure 5's own title) is really a first look at one gap instance, not a general estimate of how large BEHAVIOR-1K's sim-to-real gap is across its 50 scenes and 1,000 activities — which the paper's own abstract and introduction acknowledge by calling this "an initial study."

baselines

Two RL families (end-to-end visuomotor SAC vs. primitive-based PPO, with/without a 3-step history) evaluated on three hand-picked activities out of 1,000, chosen to represent rigid-body, articulated, and flexible/fluid manipulation respectively — not a random or stratified sample of the benchmark.

contamination

Not the LLM-pretraining-leakage kind — no pretrained language/vision-language agents are evaluated. The nearer analogue: the main text doesn't state whether the three baseline activities were trained/evaluated on one fixed scene instantiation or across randomized instantiations, so it's unclear whether Table 2's numbers reflect generalization across BEHAVIOR-1K's "infinite scene-agnostic instantiation" or fitting one instance of each activity.

what's missed

In the main text: no human or teleoperated performance reference for the three evaluated activities (unlike BEHAVIOR-100's VR-demonstration efficiency normalization); no quantitative physical-accuracy check on OmniGibson's fluid/cloth simulation beyond the human perceptual-realism survey; and no stated seed/episode count behind the reported standard deviations in Tables 2-4.

would raise confidence

RL baselines on a larger, randomly sampled subset of the 1,000 activities rather than three hand-picked ones; a human-performance ceiling for those same three activities; and a larger real-robot trial spanning more than one activity and one scene.

// bottom line

BEHAVIOR-1K's most defensible claim is narrower than "the most realistic, most human-centered embodied benchmark": it is a benchmark whose 1,000-activity list is 91%-grounded in an actual preference survey (with the remaining 9% carried over from its predecessor), instantiated in a new simulator whose extra realism features are real engineering additions and whose perceptual realism is measured, not just asserted, against four comparators. Its evidence about difficulty comes from three hand-picked activities where standard RL — especially with idealized grasping — clears 42-88% success across its two primitive-based variants, and where the no-history variant's own 42-77% success collapses to 0.00 the instant grasping is made physically realistic (the higher-scoring, history-augmented variant was never run under that ablation), and where a small, honestly-scoped real-robot pilot shows a human-driven policy still well below its own simulated performance. Both results are credible as far as they go; neither has been shown to generalize across the other 997 activities or 49 other scenes.

// takeaways

What to remember

  1. BEHAVIOR-1K bundles two contributions: a 1,000-activity DATASET (909 activities top-ranked by a 1,461-respondent preference survey over ~2,090 candidates, plus 91 carried over from BEHAVIOR-100), grounded in 50 scenes and 5,215 objects across 1,265 categories; and OmniGibson, a new NVIDIA Omniverse/PhysX-5 simulator built specifically because its predecessor (iGibson 2.0) couldn't realistically simulate fluids, deformable bodies, and cloths that "over half" of the 1,000 activities require.
  2. RL baselines are tested on just three hand-picked activities (CollectTrash, StoreDecoration, CleanTable), not a sample of the 1,000: end-to-end visuomotor control (RL-VMC) scores 0.00 on all three, while action-primitive PPO reaches 0.42-0.88 depending on the activity and whether a 3-step history is added — and an ablation (run only on the no-history variant, RL-Prim.) shows that its own 0.42-0.77 success collapses back to 0.00 across all three activities the moment the idealized "assistive pick" grasp is replaced with fully physics-based grasping.
  3. The one real-robot test keeps populations cleanly separate: simulated success (~40%, 50 runs) vs. a human-driven "optimal policy" transferred to the real robot (~22%, 27 runs) vs. the actual learned policy transferred to the real robot (0%, 26 runs) — with 44% of the learned policy's real-world errors traced to its vision-based action-primitive selection, and roughly 40% of both real-world conditions' errors traced to grasping.
