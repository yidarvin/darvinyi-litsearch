BRIGHT: A Realistic and Challenging Benchmark for Reasoning-Intensive Retrieval

1,384 real-world queries — StackExchange threads, LeetCode problems, math-competition proofs — paired with documents that are relevant only through reasoning, not lexical or semantic overlap. Every one of 13 retrievers tested does badly; the strongest, gte-Qwen1.5, averages 22.5 nDCG@10.

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

Almost every retrieval benchmark in wide use — Natural Questions, MS MARCO, the 18 datasets folded into BEIR, the retrieval slice of MTEB — is built from queries where relevance is close to the surface: a question shares keywords or a paraphrase's meaning with the passage that answers it. BRIGHT's authors call this "level 1" (keyword) and "level 2" (semantic) retrieval, and argue a large class of real queries sits one level above both: an economist looking for a story explained by the same underlying theory, a programmer trying to match an error message to the right syntax documentation, a student hunting for a previously solved problem that uses the same theorem. In these cases the query and the relevant document can share almost no words or topics at all — the connection is a reasoning chain (a shared theorem, algorithm, or causal mechanism), not an overlap in surface form.

Two prior benchmarks had already tried to target this gap, and BRIGHT positions itself explicitly against both. BIRCO evaluates retrieval on multifaceted objectives, but only in an LLM-reranking setting over a small candidate pool of roughly 100 documents per query — it never tests retrieval over a realistic-sized corpus. RAR-b adapts existing multiple-choice commonsense, math, and code datasets into a retrieval format, but its documents are short (under 10 words), and the paper describes it as "more conceptual than practical, as real-world retrieval typically involves retrieving documents instead of answers." BRIGHT's stated contribution is to be "the first text retrieval benchmark that requires intensive reasoning to retrieve relevant documents" while still sourcing everything — queries, positives, and negatives — from naturally occurring data: 1,384 queries drawn from real StackExchange posts, LeetCode submissions, AoPS competition problems, and TheoremQA questions, matched against full web pages, solved problems, and formal theorem statements rather than short answer strings.

// how it works

The method

BRIGHT is 12 datasets built from three source types. For every query Q, retrievers search a corpus D for the (small) positive set D⁺Q; everything else in D counts as negative:

D⁺Q ⊂ D     D⁻Q = D \ D⁺Q     |D⁺Q| = m ≪ |D| = n // positives connected to Q only via a reasoning trace (theorem, algorithm, causal mechanism) — not lexical or semantic overlap

StackExchange (7 datasets)

Biology, Earth Science, Economics, Psychology, Robotics, Stack Overflow, and Sustainable Living questions are collected the same way. A human annotator browses posts newest-to-oldest and keeps ones with an answer that is either accepted or has more than 5 votes and contains at least one URL. The post's title and body become the query; the annotator visits every linked page and keeps a passage as positive only if it genuinely helps reason through the post — the relevance definition is explicit: "cited in an accepted or highly voted answer and unanimously confirmed by annotators and domain experts that it helps reason through the query with critical concepts or theories." Hard negatives are collected separately: annotators Google the post's title or an LLM-generated keyword summary and keep up to 5 topically-similar but unhelpful pages, split into negative passages. A single computer-science-student annotator does the first pass; two PhD students in the relevant field verify it, and only unanimous approval keeps an example.

Coding (2 datasets)

Pony pairs coding instructions in a rare, low-resource language with the syntax documentation a programmer would actually need to consult — since the problem text rarely mentions the right function by name, retrieving it requires reasoning about what the code needs to do. LeetCode uses problem statements as queries and the site's own "Similar Questions" links as positives, drawn from a large pool combining LeetCode with CodeSearchNet; to keep the task hard, the authors discard problems whose defining algorithm is common enough that it also turns up (as an unrelated false positive) throughout CodeSearchNet — a filtering judgment made by GPT-4 and checked against 80 author annotations at Cohen's κ = 0.73.

Theorem-based questions (3 datasets)

AoPS pairs math-competition problems with other problems from AoPS Wiki that require the same named technique (Fermat's Little Theorem, Ptolemy's theorem, etc.), without rephrasing the problem text — the authors note competition problems are "deliberately written in diverse ways" already. TheoremQA-Q takes TheoremQA's theorem-driven questions and has GPT-4 rewrite each into a more concrete, applied scenario so the theorem's name doesn't leak into the query text (206 of an original 800 questions survive manual author review for solvability and consistency); a document is positive if its solution uses the same theorem. TheoremQA-T instead retrieves the theorem statement itself from ProofWiki's 20K-plus formal definitions, matched by GPT-4 and checked against 50 human-annotated instances at κ = 0.62.

13 retrieval systems are benchmarked: the sparse baseline BM25; small (<1B) dense models BGE, Instructor-Large, and SentenceBERT; large (>1B) dense models E5-Mistral, SFR-Embedding-Mistral, Instructor-XL, GritLM, and gte-Qwen1.5; and proprietary embeddings from Cohere, OpenAI, Voyage, and Google. All are evaluated zero-shot with nDCG@10, the standard IR ranking metric that rewards placing true positives near the top of a 10-document window.

// what they found

The results

The headline number in the abstract is a direct comparison: SFR-Embedding-Mistral tops the MTEB retrieval leaderboard at 59.0 nDCG@10, but on BRIGHT the same model scores 18.3. Across all 13 systems and 12 datasets (Table 2), the best average is gte-Qwen1.5 at 22.5 nDCG@10, followed by GritLM at 21.0 and Google's embedding model at 20.0; BM25 sits at 14.5, in the same band as the smaller open-source dense models — ahead of BGE (13.7) and Instructor-Large (14.2), behind SentenceBERT (14.9) — and well behind every >1B-parameter dense or proprietary model. No single model wins every column: gte-Qwen1.5 leads 6 of the 12 datasets (Biology, Earth Science, Stack Overflow, AoPS, TheoremQA-Q, TheoremQA-T) and the overall average, but Instructor-XL leads Economics (22.4) and Robotics (18.2), Google leads Psychology (27.8), OpenAI leads Sustainable Living (20.5), Voyage leads LeetCode (30.6), and GritLM leads Pony by a wide margin (22.0 vs. the next-best 9.9).

Two interventions help, though not uniformly. Prompting an LLM (GPT-4, Llama-3-70B-Instruct, or GritLM) to write out a chain-of-thought reasoning trace for the query — "(1) identify the essential problem, (2) reason step by step about what the relevant documents should contain, (3) draft an answer" — and using that trace as the new query lifts scores "by up to 12.2 points" when the trace comes from GPT-4, which improves all 13 retrievers, or from Llama-3-70B, which improves 12 of the 13 — it lowers GritLM, 21.0 to 20.9. GritLM-written traces are weaker: the paper notes they "improve BM25 performance but are less effective for other models," and in fact 7 of the 13 retrievers score lower with a GritLM-written query than with their original one — SBERT 14.9→13.9, Qwen 22.5→19.9, GritLM's own score on itself 21.0→18.3, Google 20.0→19.6 among them. BM25 benefits most from the stronger traces: GPT-4-written reasoning queries push it from 14.5 up toward the top of the leaderboard shown in Figure 3, ahead of dense models using their original queries.

Reranking is a mixed bag. The MiniLM cross-encoder — a classic MS MARCO-trained reranker — hurts both retrievers and gets worse as k grows (BM25: 14.3 → 13.1 at k=10 → 8.3 at k=100), which the paper attributes to poor transfer from MS MARCO's easy semantic matches to BRIGHT's reasoning-heavy relevance. LLM reranking helps instead, though not monotonically with k: on top of BM25, Gemini-1.0 raises the score by 1.4 points (14.3 → 15.7) and GPT-4 by 3.1 points at k=10 (→ 17.4), but GPT-4 reranking on BM25 actually falls to 17.0 at k=100; on top of Google's retriever, GPT-4 reranking does keep climbing with k, reaching 22.6 at k=100 — the highest nDCG@10 anywhere in the paper's main-text tables, edging out Table 2's un-reranked ceiling of 22.5 by just 0.1.

Retrieval quality also feeds downstream question-answering. Using Claude-3.5-Sonnet to both answer and grade StackExchange questions, closed-book (no retrieved documents) scores 77.7 average correctness across the 7 StackExchange domains; adding documents from the best retriever, gte-Qwen1.5, raises that to 79.6 (a 1.9-point gain), while the annotated oracle documents reach 81.8 (a 4.1-point gain) — more than double Qwen's real-world lift. Qwen is not uniformly the best retriever for QA, though: on Sustainable Living specifically, both SBERT (74.1) and even BM25 (73.6) edge it out (72.6).

Two robustness checks close out the paper. Continuing to train GritLM on the StackExchange documents in BRIGHT's corpus (language-modeling loss plus contrastive QA-pair training, but never showing the actual query→document mapping) leaves its average nDCG@10 essentially flat — 20.5 before, 20.4 after, across the 7 StackExchange datasets — which the authors read as evidence that BRIGHT resists this kind of pretraining-leakage boost. And converting the StackExchange datasets to a long-context, unsplit-document setting (fewer, much longer documents, scored by recall@1 instead of nDCG@10) still tops out at 27.8, again from gte-Qwen1.5.

// does it hold up?

The eval read

The construction is genuinely careful — every StackExchange example passes through a first-pass annotator and two domain-expert PhD reviewers, with unanimous agreement required to keep it, and the two programmatically-checked subsets (TheoremQA-Theorems, LeetCode) report real inter-annotator agreement against GPT-4's labels (κ = 0.62 and 0.73). But four things are worth flagging before taking the headline framing at face value.

First, and most concretely: the paper's own prose doesn't match its own results table. The Table 2 caption, the opening sentence of Section 4.2 ("Results in Table 2 show that BRIGHT is very challenging, with the best model achieving only 24.3 nDCG@10"), and the Conclusion ("a maximum nDCG@10 score of only 24.3") all state the ceiling as 24.3 — but Table 2's own Avg. column tops out at 22.5 (gte-Qwen1.5), and every row's average re-sums correctly from its own twelve cells. That's not a rounding issue; 24.3 simply isn't the maximum of the printed Avg. column anywhere in the table, and no other main-text table reports a 12-dataset average that reaches it either (Table 6's long-context recall@1, by contrast, states "the highest recall achieved is 27.8" and that number does match Qwen's cell exactly). The 24.3 figure is repeated three times, which means it's shaping the framing of the whole results section rather than being an isolated typo.

Second, the contamination check is narrower than "BRIGHT is robust to pretraining leakage" suggests. It continues training exactly one model (GritLM), on exactly one subset of BRIGHT's domains (the 7 StackExchange sets — not LeetCode, Pony, AoPS, or TheoremQA), using a training recipe the authors themselves designed rather than a pre-registered replication of real-world pretraining exposure. And the reported average (20.5 → 20.4) hides a large per-domain swing: Psychology rises 10.8 points (19.9 → 30.7) while Earth Science falls 7.3 points (32.8 → 25.5) and Robotics falls 4.6 (17.3 → 12.7) — swings that mostly cancel in the average but that the paper's text doesn't discuss or explain. A benchmark whose contamination-robustness claim rests on one model, one training recipe, and an average that masks double-digit domain-level swings is a narrower result than the "robust against potential data leakage during large-scale pre-training" language in the Introduction (echoed in Table 5's caption and the §5.2 heading) implies.

Third, the authors' own appendix flags their most direct competitor comparison as unfinished: Appendix I compares BRIGHT to RAR-b along three axes (dataset construction, document length, practical relevance) and closes with "we believe both datasets effectively assess retrievers' capabilities in handling reasoning-intensive queries, and we'll be adding clearer comparisons of RAR-b in our revision" — an acknowledged gap in the ICLR camera-ready itself, not something we're inferring.

Fourth, the reranking claim has the same self-contradiction in miniature: Section 5.1 says GPT-4 reranking "enhances by 3.1 and continues to improve with higher k," but Table 3 shows the opposite for BM25 — 17.4 at k=10 falling to 17.0 at k=100. Of the two reranked retrievers reported, only the Google row (21.5 → 22.6) actually improves with k; "continues to improve with higher k" is true for one of the two rows it's meant to describe.

And that Google row’s modest gain conceals two large opposite effects. Going from k=10 to k=100 with GPT-4 reranking (Table 42), the StackExchange columns soar — Biology 26.1 → 42.5, Psychology 32.6 → 42.1, Stack Overflow 22.6 → 35.1 — while the code and math columns collapse: LeetCode 24.5 → 5.6, AoPS 8.9 → 2.4, TheoremQA-Questions 22.9 → 6.6. The +1.1 average is what is left after those cancel, and the same shape on BM25 is exactly why its average slips instead. Reading either average as "reranking deeper helps a bit" gets the mechanism backwards: deeper reranking transforms performance on the discussion-forum domains and destroys it on the ones with executable or provable answers.

The contamination result is a single point on a volatile curve. The headline is that fine-tuning GritLM on in-domain StackExchange content moves the average from 20.5 to 20.4 — essentially nothing, which the paper reads as evidence that BRIGHT resists memorization. But Appendix Table 12 prints all ten epochs: 20.5, 19.1, 16.1, 17.5, 20.6, 20.6, 18.3, 18.9, 20.5, 21.0, 20.4. The run swings 4.9 points end to end, dips to 16.1 at epoch 2, and at epoch 9 reaches 21.0 — above the untrained baseline. The reported 20.4 is simply where epoch 10 landed. A flat delta drawn from one arbitrary stopping point is much weaker evidence than the framing suggests, and the paper does not discuss the trajectory.

baselines

13 off-the-shelf retrievers spanning sparse, small dense, large dense, and proprietary — a broad and current sweep for 2024 — but no BRIGHT-tuned baseline is proposed, and the one supervised reranker tested (MiniLM, trained on MS MARCO) actively hurts performance, so the paper can't distinguish "reasoning-intensive retrieval is hard in general" from "these particular off-the-shelf systems weren't trained for it."

contamination

One continued-training experiment, one model (GritLM), 7 of the benchmark's 12 domains (the StackExchange sets only), reported as a single average that hides a Psychology +10.8 / Earth Science −7.3 nDCG swing the text never discusses.

what's missed

The paper's own stated "24.3" ceiling (repeated in the Table 2 caption, Section 4.2, and the Conclusion) doesn't match Table 2's actual 22.5 maximum; the RAR-b comparison is explicitly deferred by the authors to a future revision; StackExchange annotation agreement is reported only as a unanimity requirement, not as a numeric inter-annotator rate (unlike the κ figures given for TheoremQA-T and LeetCode).

would raise confidence

A corrigendum reconciling the 24.3/22.5 discrepancy; running the leakage test on more than one architecture and across the coding/theorem domains, not just StackExchange; and reporting the pre-unanimity disagreement rate among StackExchange annotators so readers can judge how much borderline material the "unanimous only" filter is discarding.

// bottom line

BRIGHT's central claim — that reasoning-intensive queries expose a real, large gap in current retrieval systems — is well supported by the data: a 59.0-to-18.3 MTEB-to-BRIGHT drop for the leaderboard-topping SFR-Embedding-Mistral is a striking, well-documented number, and the 12-dataset construction spans real StackExchange, coding, and math sources with genuine expert verification. But the paper's own summary of its own headline result is wrong by more than a full point on its own table, its contamination-robustness claim rests on a single narrow experiment whose average conceals sizable per-domain swings, and its most obvious related-work comparison (RAR-b) is left as a to-do in the published version. None of that undermines the benchmark's usefulness, but a reader quoting "24.3" or "robust to data leakage" from this paper should check Table 2 and Table 5 themselves first.

// what to remember

What to remember

  1. BRIGHT shows that MTEB's #1 retriever at the time (SFR-Embedding-Mistral, 59.0 nDCG@10 on MTEB) scores only 18.3 on reasoning-intensive queries, and the best of 13 models tested across all architectures (gte-Qwen1.5) averages just 22.5 nDCG@10 over the benchmark's 12 datasets — reasoning-intensive retrieval is a real, large, and currently unsolved gap.
  2. The fixes that work don't look like standard IR tricks, and don't transfer evenly across models: LLM-generated chain-of-thought reasoning queries lift scores by up to +12.2 points — GPT-4 traces improve all 13 retrievers and Llama-3-70B traces 12 of 13 — but the same trick with GritLM-written traces lowers 7 of the 13; LLM reranking helps too — but a classic MS MARCO-trained cross-encoder reranker actively hurts performance, so techniques tuned for level-1/level-2 retrieval don't automatically transfer to level 3.
  3. Check the paper's own arithmetic before quoting its "best score": the Table 2 caption, Section 4.2, and the Conclusion each state a ceiling of 24.3 nDCG@10, but Table 2's own Avg. column — which re-sums correctly row by row — tops out at 22.5.
