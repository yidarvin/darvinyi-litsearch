A Dataset for Research on Short-Text Conversations

628,833 naturally-occurring Sina Weibo (post, response) pairs, roughly 1.5 million candidate responses, and 422 posts pooling-labeled into ~12,427 judged (post, response) pairs — released to make retrieval-based, single-round social-media response modeling trainable and testable at scale, and later adopted as the seed corpus for the NTCIR Short Text Conversation shared tasks.

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

By 2013, work on automatic conversation was split between two traditions, and neither one scaled the way the authors wanted. One family was rule-based or reinforcement-learning spoken-dialogue systems — Cleverbot (Carpenter, 1997), NJFun (Litman et al., 2000), POMDP-based dialogue managers (Williams and Young, 2007; Schatzmann et al., 2006), virtual-museum-guide QA policies (Misu et al., 2012) — which model an entire multi-turn dialogue state and require relatively little training data but a great deal of hand-designed rules or task-specific reward engineering. The other, newer direction tried to exploit the sudden abundance of conversational text on social media: Ritter et al. (2011) applied a statistical-machine-translation model to Twitter post-reply pairs, and Jafarpour and Burges (2010) and Leuski and Traum (2011) had explored similar data-driven chatbot training. But none of this left behind a public, reusable dataset sized for both training and testing a retrieval-based response model — Ritter's Twitter data wasn't released, and the small, hand-built dialogue corpora used by NPCEditor-style systems were far too small to support a candidate-response bank of any real size.

The authors deliberately narrow the problem to make it tractable at scale: rather than model a full dialogue session (their stated point of comparison is the Turing test itself), they take "one step back" to single-round, context-free response — given a self-contained short text, find a natural, relevant, human-like reply, with no requirement that it carry particular content, opinion, or style. That narrower target is exactly what a retrieval-based approach needs: instead of generating a response word-by-word, pick a good one out of a sufficiently large candidate pool. Sina Weibo — China's Twitter-equivalent, at the time hosting over 500 million registered users and 100+ million posts a day, with a 140-Chinese-character limit on both posts and the comments they receive — is a natural, essentially unlimited source of such (post, response) pairs. The paper's contribution is to actually collect, clean, partially label, and release such a dataset, rather than merely propose the idea.

// how it works

The dataset, and the baseline built to prove it out

The dataset comes from a deliberately narrow, topic-focused sampling frame rather than a random crawl of all of Weibo. The authors first identify 10 seed users — NLP researchers active on Sina Weibo, posting at least twice a day on average and with at least 100 followers — then crawl the followees of those seeds to reach a loosely connected community of 3,200 users centered on Chinese NLP/ML researchers, students, and professors. From that community they crawl posts and the comments they received (not necessarily from within the community) over two months, April 5 to June 5, 2013. Because the seed community skews toward NLP/ML people, the resulting topics cluster around research (papers, conferences, researchers), general arts and science, IT technology, and life (travel, food, photography).

Raw (post, response) pairs then go through a four-step filter: 1) drop any post shorter than 10 Chinese characters or any response shorter than 5 characters (too little signal, or too generic to be interesting — e.g. a response of just "wow"); 2) keep only the first 100 responses per post, since responses past that point increasingly address each other rather than the original post; 3) scrub out likely advertisements — long responses that reappear, verbatim, on more than two different posts; and 4) segment the remaining Chinese text into words with ICTCLAS (Zhang et al., 2003) after stripping punctuation and emoticons.

What survives is organized into three parts, illustrated in Figure 2. Original (post, response) pairs — 628,833 pairs actually observed on Weibo, noisy because a genuine reply can still be a spam post or a reply to an earlier comment rather than the post itself. Labeled pairs — for 422 selected posts, human annotators judge roughly 30 candidate responses each (about 12,427 total judged pairs per Table 2), where the candidates are not the post's own original replies but are chosen through a pooling procedure borrowed from IR evaluation (Voorhees, 2002): three baseline retrieval models (described below) each contribute their top-10 responses for a post, the three lists are merged into a reduced set of at most 30, and only that reduced set is hand-labeled suitable/unsuitable. Annotators judge suitability on three criteria — semantic relevance (including whether entities named in the response match those in the post), logic consistency (the response can't just restate or contradict something already settled by the post), and speech-act alignment (a question should get answered or forwarded, not met with an unrelated statement). Pure responses — roughly 1.5 million responses in total, including ones filtered out of the original-pairs bucket by the cleaning steps above, kept as extra candidate material even though they're not tied to a specific post.

To validate that the dataset actually supports training and testing a response model, the authors build a two-stage retrieval-based system (Figure 5). Given an input post x, Stage I runs three fast baseline matching models to cheaply retrieve a reduced candidate set from the full response bank: post-response semantic matching, a bilinear model that scores a post/response pair via low-dimensional embeddings LX, LY learned with a large-margin variant of Wu et al. (2013)'s bilinear matching; post-response similarity, plain vector-space cosine similarity between post and response; and post-post similarity, cosine similarity used to find posts like x and borrow their responses as candidates. Stage II then reranks the reduced candidate set with a learned linear scoring function:

score(x, y) = Σi∈Ω wi · Φi(x, y) // Ω = 15 matching features (built from the three Stage-I models, e.g. longest-common-substring length, or semantic-match score weighted by post-post similarity); weights wᵢ trained with RankSVM (Joachims, 2002), 5-fold cross-validation over the 422 labeled posts, fixed slack penalty
// what they found

The results

On 5-fold cross-validation over the 422 labeled posts, adding features incrementally to the RankSVM ranker gives the pattern in Table 3: post-response similarity alone (P2R) reaches MAP 0.565 / P@1 0.489; adding post-post similarity (P2R+P2P) is the biggest single jump, to MAP 0.621 / P@1 0.567; adding the semantic-matching feature instead (P2R+MATCH) reaches MAP 0.575 / P@1 0.513; and combining all three (P2R+P2P+MATCH) reaches MAP 0.621 / P@1 0.574 — the same MAP as P2R+P2P alone, with a small additional P@1 gain. The authors read this as semantic matching contributing a modest but real improvement in top-1 precision on top of the similarity-based features.

To mimic a more realistic deployment where the system can choose whether to answer at all, the authors let it respond only when the top candidate's score clears a threshold: restricting to the 50% of posts it's most confident about lifts P@1 to 0.76, and restricting further to the top 25% of posts pushes P@1 to 0.81. The qualitative case study (Section 6.2, Figure 6) is consistent with the quantitative pattern: the retrieval model is fairly good at surfacing semantically relevant responses — including ones that share no words at all with the post, credited to the semantic-matching feature (e.g. matching a post about "Eight England players... penalty area" to "What a classic match" via the shared soccer topic rather than shared vocabulary) — but it visibly struggles with logic consistency, e.g. responding "wow, Wang Fengyi is your great grand-father" to a post that explicitly denies that relationship.

// does it hold up?

The eval read

Read as a benchmark paper rather than as a modeling paper, this is really two separate contributions bundled together — a dataset, and a baseline model used only to demonstrate the dataset is usable — and the eval rigor that matters most for its legacy is around the former, not the latter.

baselines

Every number in Table 3 is a combination of the paper's own three matching signals (P2R, P2P, MATCH) compared only against each other — there is no external baseline in the results table at all: no plain BM25/TF-IDF retrieval score, no comparison to Ritter et al.'s (2011) contemporaneous SMT-style Twitter response model that the introduction explicitly cites as the closest prior work, and no reported number for the "randomly selected pairs" the paper mentions are over 98% negative. Readers can see that stacking these three features helps each other, but not how much of the P@1 gain is "any sensible retrieval signal" versus something distinctive about this feature set.

contamination

Not contamination in the modern train/test-overlap sense, but a closely related external-validity gap: the entire dataset is sampled by crawling followees of just 10 seed NLP/ML researchers out to a 3,200-user community, so both the training pairs and the 422 labeled evaluation posts are drawn from one homogeneous, topically narrow social circle (research, IT, general arts/science, life/travel). The paper never checks whether its 5-fold CV splits accidentally group correlated posts (e.g. many posts about the same conference or the same "invited talk" template) across folds, and it makes no claim — and offers no evidence — about whether matching patterns learned here transfer to Weibo conversation outside the NLP-research bubble.

what's missed

MAP/P@1 are computed only against the pooled ~30-candidate labeled set per post, and that pool is built exclusively from the top-10 outputs of the same three Stage-I models being evaluated (classic IR pooling bias, which the paper cites Voorhees 2002 for but never quantifies) — a genuinely good response that none of the three baseline models would have surfaced can never be credited as correct, which mechanically caps how much better any smarter Stage-II ranker can be shown to be. There is also no inter-annotator agreement figure reported anywhere for the semantic-relevance / logic-consistency / speech-act labeling — unlike some later dialogue-dataset papers that at least report one aggregate agreement number, this paper reports none, so we don't know how reliable the "suitable/unsuitable" ground truth itself is. And beyond the reported P@1/MAP numbers, the only evidence of response quality is a handful of hand-picked qualitative examples (Section 6.2) — not a scored human evaluation.

would raise confidence

A genuine external retrieval baseline (plain cosine/BM25, or a re-implementation of Ritter et al.'s translation-model approach) run on the same labeled pool; a reported inter-annotator agreement statistic for the suitability labels; an estimate of how often a "gold" response falls outside the pooled candidate set (i.e., the pooling recall ceiling); and a second labeled sample drawn from a broader, non-NLP-community slice of Weibo to test whether the matching features and the response-rate/P@1 tradeoff (0.76 at 50% coverage, 0.81 at 25%) hold up outside the sampling frame the dataset was built from.

// bottom line

The 2013 MAP/P@1 numbers themselves are a modest, self-referential ablation, not a rigorously benchmarked result — but that was never really the point. The paper's lasting contribution is that it collected and released a public, at-scale, naturally-occurring short-text conversation resource (628,833 pairs, ~1.5M responses, a pooled-and-labeled evaluation subset) when essentially nothing like it existed, and the Sina Weibo data it built went on to seed the NTCIR Short Text Conversation shared tasks — the direct evaluation-campaign lineage this dataset created — while later curated dialogue datasets like DailyDialog explicitly positioned themselves against "noisy social-media" corpora of exactly this Weibo/Twitter kind as the problem they were built to fix.

// takeaways

What to remember

  1. First public, at-scale, naturally-occurring short-text conversation dataset: 628,833 Sina Weibo (post, response) pairs, ~1.5 million candidate responses, and 422 posts pooling-labeled into ~12,427 judged pairs — built specifically so a retrieval-based response model could be both trained and tested on real social-media exchanges rather than small hand-built dialogue corpora.
  2. Establishes retrieval, not generation, as the practical baseline for single-round social-media response: a two-stage pipeline — three fast matching signals (post-response similarity, post-post similarity, a learned bilinear semantic match) narrow a huge candidate pool, then a RankSVM reranker with 15 features picks the best one — reaching MAP 0.621 / P@1 0.574 combined, and P@1 up to 0.81 when the system is allowed to decline to answer 75% of posts.
  3. The dataset's real legacy is as infrastructure: it seeded the NTCIR Short Text Conversation shared-task series, and it's the canonical example of the "noisy social-media dialogue" category (alongside Twitter-derived corpora) that later curated benchmarks like DailyDialog explicitly built themselves to be a cleaner alternative to.
