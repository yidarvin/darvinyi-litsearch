LEXTREME: A Multi-Lingual and Multi-Task Benchmark for the Legal Domain

Eleven legal-NLP datasets, folded into 18 task configurations across 24 European languages — the multilingual successor to the English-only LexGLUE. Instead of one leaderboard number, LEXTREME reports two harmonic-mean aggregates (by dataset, by language) and combines them into a single score: the best baseline, XLM-R Large, reaches 41.3, and zero-shot ChatGPT (gpt-3.5-turbo, June 2023) loses to it on all eight validation tasks it was tried on.

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

By early 2023, general-purpose NLU benchmarking had gone multilingual — XTREME covered 40 languages across 6 cross-lingual tasks over 9 datasets (LEXTREME's own Table 1; its related-work prose says ten), CLUE and CBLUE did the same for Chinese general and biomedical text — but legal NLP hadn't. The closest thing to a legal-domain GLUE was LexGLUE (Chalkidis et al., 2022), which the LEXTREME authors describe accurately: it "is the first benchmark for the legal domain," bundling ECtHR, SCOTUS, EUR-LEX, LEDGAR, UNFAIR-ToS and CaseHOLD into one English-only leaderboard. LexGLUE's own authors had flagged the gap — LEXTREME quotes them noting "there is an increasing need for developing models for other languages" — and Ilias Chalkidis, LexGLUE's lead author, is LEXTREME's last author here, making this an explicit, self-authored sequel rather than a coincidental resemblance. Two smaller multilingual legal efforts existed by then (FairLex: 4 tasks/4 datasets/5 languages; LBOX: 5 tasks/5 datasets, Korean-only), but nothing spanned Europe's language diversity.

Law is also a domain where a translated GLUE genuinely isn't enough: legal texts carry jurisdiction-specific lexicons, citation conventions and document structures that general-domain multilingual pretraining doesn't necessarily capture. So the authors set out to do for multilingual legal NLU what LexGLUE did for English: survey the literature (108 candidate papers found via keyword search across ACL, EACL, NAACL, EMNLP, LREC, ICAIL and the NLLP workshop, 2010–2022), filter by four inclusion criteria (legal text; a legal or legal-NLU task; a European language, for now; human-derived labels) and four exclusion criteria (not public; no redistribution license; ML-generated labels; not peer-reviewed), and land on 11 datasets spanning 24 languages from 8 language groups — LEXTREME.

// how it works

The method

The 11 datasets split into 18 task configurations of three types — single-label text classification (SLTC), multi-label text classification (MLTC), and named entity recognition (NER): BCD (Brazilian Court Decisions, 2 SLTC configs — approval/dismissal and judge unanimity), GAM (German Argument Mining, SLTC), GLC (Greek Legal Code, 3 SLTC configs at volume/chapter/subject granularity), SJP (Swiss Judgment Prediction, SLTC, ~85K cases), OTS (Online Terms of Service, 1 SLTC + 1 MLTC config), C19 (COVID-19 Emergency Measures / EXCEPTIUS, MLTC across 7 European jurisdictions), MEU (MultiEURLEX, MLTC over the EUROVOC taxonomy at 3 label-granularity levels, all 24 languages), GLN (Greek Legal NER), LNR (Romanian legal NER), LNB (Brazilian legal NER), and MAP (MAPA, coarse- and fine-grained multilingual legal NER, all 24 languages). Documents that exceed the 512-token limit of the encoders tested (up to 4096–8192 tokens for MEU and SJP) are handled with a hierarchical variant: 128-token chunks are encoded in parallel, their [CLS] embeddings are fed through a 2-layer transformer to make them context-aware, and max-pooling over that produces the document representation.

Every config is scored with macro-F1 specifically because — unlike accuracy or MCC — it is computable uniformly across both classification and NER. Rather than a single leaderboard number, LEXTREME computes two intermediate aggregates and then combines them:

LEXTREME = 2 · D · LD + L // D = dataset aggregate (harmonic mean over languages-in-config → configs-in-dataset → datasets); L = language aggregate (harmonic mean over configs-in-dataset → datasets-available-per-language → languages); the final score is itself their harmonic mean, following Shavrina & Malykh (2021) and Chalkidis et al. (2022a)

The point of weighing datasets and languages separately before combining is fairness: a simple average over all 18×24 language–config cells would let the handful of configs and languages with the most data samples (MEU and MAP alone touch all 24 languages) dominate the score. The authors are explicit that this scheme still leaves one dimension unaddressed: "We do not address the dimension of the jurisdiction, which we consider beyond the scope of this work."

Five multilingual encoders are fine-tuned as baselines — MiniLM (118M params), DistilBERT (135M), mDeBERTa-v3 (278M), XLM-R Base (278M) and XLM-R Large (560M), all pretrained on ≥100 languages and capped at 1B parameters "due to resource constraints." Separately, monolingual models are fine-tuned per language whenever a language has data in at least three datasets, split into general-purpose NativeBERT and legal-pretrained NativeLegalBERT (the specific checkpoint per language is catalogued in the paper's Table 8). Finally, gpt-3.5-turbo is evaluated zero-shot, but only on the 8 classification configs with fewer than 50 labels — MultiEURLEX is excluded outright because its labels are non-textual taxonomy codes and its 24-language inputs would make a cost-reasonable comparison impossible — on the validation split (not test, "to be careful not to leak test data into ChatGPT"), capped at 1,000 samples per task to control API cost.

// what they found

The results

XLM-R Large is the strongest multilingual baseline on both aggregates (48.5 dataset-agg., 36.0 language-agg.), for a combined LEXTREME score of 41.3 — modest, and clearly short of saturation. (Table 6's Agg. column is actually topped by NativeBERT at 39.1 — see the monolingual comparison below — but NativeBERT and NativeLegalBERT are never evaluated on the dataset-aggregate table, so they have no LEXTREME combined score to compare against 41.3.) (Note: the arXiv abstract for this paper — including the live v3 listing that the source ↗ link above points at — still states “61.3” as this figure; that number appears nowhere in the EMNLP camera-ready as an aggregate or LEXTREME score — it survives only as unrelated per-task cell values in three appendix tables (pp. 23, 25, 28 — two of those cells are accuracy, not macro-F1) — and doesn't reconcile with either aggregate table or Figure 1. That abstract reports both aggregates as 61.3, which is why its harmonic mean is 61.3 too — and arXiv's abstract metadata was never revised across any version: the v1 and current listings are byte-identical. The camera-ready PDF's own abstract, by contrast, states no number at all. The correct, cross-checked value is 48.5/36.0 → 41.3.)

The aggregate hides real per-dataset and per-language churn. On the dataset-aggregate table, XLM-R Large tops the overall column but wins outright on only 5 of the 11 datasets (GLC, OTS, MEU, LNR, LNB); XLM-R Base wins 3 (BCD, SJP, GLN), mDeBERTa v3 wins 2 (C19, MAP), and MiniLM wins 1 — it edges out even XLM-R Large on GAM, 73.3 to 73.1. The spread between best and worst model is as wide as 52 points on OTS but under 6 points on GAM, SJP and MAP. On the language-aggregate table, XLM-R Large does better relatively — it's the best of the five multilingual models on 21 of 24 languages, losing only to mDeBERTa v3 on Irish, Hungarian and Dutch.

The more striking result sits in the monolingual comparison. A generic, non-legal monolingual BERT (NativeBERT) is the single best model in the whole table — beating every multilingual model and every legal-pretrained monolingual model — on 13 of the 19 languages where one exists (the paper names 4 exceptions — Bulgarian, Spanish, Polish, Slovak — though Irish and Italian are also available-but-non-winning languages it doesn't name). And on the 4 languages where a legal-domain-pretrained NativeLegalBERT checkpoint exists (English, Spanish, Italian, Romanian), the generic NativeBERT beats it head-to-head on 3 of 4 — losing only on Spanish (40.3 vs. 33.3).

Against zero-shot ChatGPT (Table 7), XLM-R Large wins all 8 of the 8 validation tasks it was compared on, with the largest gaps on GAM (73.0 vs. 35.5, +37.5) and both OTS configs (+64.7 and +51.8):

// does it hold up?

The eval read

The two-aggregate design is a genuine methodological contribution — reporting dataset- and language-level harmonic means separately, rather than one blended number, is exactly the right instinct for a benchmark this heterogeneous, and it's what makes the per-dataset/per-language churn above visible at all instead of being averaged away. But the "24 languages" framing needs a footnote the paper doesn't quite give it: of the 11 datasets, only MultiEURLEX and MAPA actually have data in all 24 languages; the other 9 are jurisdiction-limited — drawn from one or a handful of national/EU jurisdictions rather than all 24 — and cover between 1 and 7 languages each (Portuguese-only, German-only, Greek-only, Romanian-only, the 3-language SJP, or the 7-jurisdiction/7-language C19 and 4-language OTS sets). In task-config terms, 5 of the 18 configs carry the entire 24-language claim; the rest of the benchmark's headline task variety — judgment prediction, argument mining, and the three monolingual NER sets (Greek, Romanian, Brazilian-Portuguese) — never touches most of the 24 languages at all (MAPA's coarse/fine NER, unlike those three, does reach all 24). The authors are upfront that they "do not address the dimension of the jurisdiction," but that disclaimer undersells how load-bearing it is: for most of the 24 languages, the language-aggregate score in Table 6 is really a EUROVOC-classification-and-NER score, not a legal-NLU score in the fuller sense the dataset list implies.

The paper's own results tables also contain two small, checkable errors that are worth naming precisely, since they're the kind a reader would otherwise take on faith: the results discussion states XLM-R Large "has the best performance in six out of eleven datasets," but Table 5 itself shows five (GLC, OTS, MEU, LNR, LNB); and Table 3's caption asserts "the 24 EU languages are: bg, cs, da, de, el, en, es, et, fi, fr, ga, hu, it, lt, lv, mt, nl, pt, ro, sk, sv" — which is only 21 codes, omitting Croatian, Polish and Slovenian, even though those three do have real, populated columns in Table 6. Neither changes the substantive findings, but both suggest the tables were assembled faster than they were proofread.

The construct-validity question — what does winning on macro-F1 actually certify here — is one the authors partly address themselves. In the closing paragraph of the Limitations section, they note that outcome-prediction datasets like BCD and SJP predict a case's result from the court's own decision text, not the parties' original filings, citing Semo et al. (2022) as a rarer, more realistic alternative that uses actual complaints as input — but the same paragraph pushes back, arguing the task "may still require legal reasoning capabilities from models" because of the need to pick out the correct information from that text. Read together, that's a fair complication rather than a concession: a high macro-F1 on BCD or SJP likely reflects some mix of recovering signal the judge already wrote into the ruling and genuine extraction of the decisive facts, and the paper offers no way to tell the two apart. Separately, the NativeBERT-beats-NativeLegalBERT result above cuts against the implicit premise that this domain rewards legal-specific pretraining: on this benchmark's tasks, generic monolingual pretraining is at least as strong as legal-domain pretraining wherever both exist to compare.

Finally, the ChatGPT comparison is honestly scoped by the authors — validation split (explicitly to avoid leaking test data into a closed API), an 8-of-18 subset chosen by label count and cost, a 1,000-sample cap, dated June 15, 2023 — but a single zero-shot pass with gpt-3.5-turbo is a narrow, already-dated data point. It supports "in mid-2023, one non-legal-specialized, non-fine-tuned chat model underperformed a fine-tuned 560M-parameter encoder on 8 legal classification tasks in several languages" — not a durable claim about frontier LLMs and legal NLU generally.

baselines

Five multilingual encoders (100M–560M params, all pre-2022 or early-2022 architectures) plus per-language monolingual models are a fair, representative baseline set for the fine-tuning era, but the paper caps everything at 1B parameters "due to resource constraints" and includes only one LLM data point (zero-shot gpt-3.5-turbo, not fine-tuned, not instruction-tuned for legal text) — there's no fine-tuned-LLM or larger-multilingual-encoder comparison.

contamination

Handled carefully for the one place it's addressed: ChatGPT is scored on validation, not test, specifically to avoid leaking held-out data into a closed model. Left unaddressed for the five fine-tuned encoders themselves — none of MiniLM/DistilBERT/mDeBERTa/XLM-R's CC100 or Wikipedia pretraining corpora are checked against the (mostly public, web-sourced) legal text in MEU, GLC, or SJP.

what's missed

The "24 languages, 11 datasets" headline obscures that only 2 datasets (MultiEURLEX, MAPA) actually reach all 24 languages; macro-F1 papers over the difference between "recovering the judge's own reasoning from the ruling text" (BCD, SJP) and open-ended legal NER/classification; and the paper's own results narrative undercounts XLM-R Large's dataset wins (says 6, table shows 5) and Table 3's caption undercounts its own 24-language list by 3.

would raise confidence

A pretraining-corpus contamination check against the (largely public) legal source texts; explicitly separating "all-24-language" results from the jurisdiction-specific subset when reporting the language aggregate; and a rerun of the LLM comparison with a more capable, ideally fine-tuned or legal-instruction-tuned model on the test split now that the benchmark is public and non-novel.

// bottom line

LEXTREME's real contribution is methodological — two harmonic-mean aggregates instead of one leaderboard number, which is precisely what makes visible that no single baseline dominates and that generic pretraining sometimes beats legal-specific pretraining. But its "24-language" framing is doing more rhetorical work than 9 of its 11 datasets can support, and two small self-inconsistencies in its own tables (the "six of eleven" claim, the 21-not-24-language caption) are worth a published erratum even though they don't change the paper's conclusions.

// takeaways

What to remember

  1. LEXTREME is explicitly the multilingual sequel to LexGLUE — same last author (Ilias Chalkidis), same aggregation philosophy borrowed and extended — scaling from 7 tasks/6 datasets/English-only to 18 configs/11 datasets/24 languages, reported as two separate harmonic-mean aggregates (by dataset, by language) rather than one blended leaderboard number.
  2. XLM-R Large is the best overall baseline (41.3 combined score) but wins only 5 of 11 datasets and 21 of 24 languages among the five multilingual models tried — and a generic, non-legal monolingual BERT beats every multilingual model and every legal-pretrained monolingual model outright on 13 of its 19 available languages.
  3. Zero-shot ChatGPT (gpt-3.5-turbo, June 2023) loses to fine-tuned XLM-R Large on all 8 validation tasks it was tried on — a real but narrow, dated, validation-only result, not a general verdict on LLMs and legal NLU; and only 2 of the paper's 11 datasets (MultiEURLEX, MAPA) actually reach all 24 claimed languages.
