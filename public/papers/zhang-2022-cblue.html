CBLUE: A Chinese Biomedical Language Understanding Evaluation Benchmark

The first Chinese biomedical NLU benchmark: eight tasks built from real clinical trials, EHRs, medical forums and search logs, left in their natural, long-tailed distribution rather than balanced for convenience. Eleven Chinese pretrained language models — including a purpose-built medical BERT — are fine-tuned on it, and every one of them trails trained amateur annotators on every single task.

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

By 2021, multi-task leaderboards had become the standard way to measure general language understanding — GLUE, its harder sequel SuperGLUE, and the Chinese general-domain CLUE all bundled several tasks behind one score so different models could be compared apples-to-apples. Biomedical NLP had its own version in English: BLURB, PubMedQA, and the BioASQ shared tasks gave researchers a way to measure progress on clinical and biomedical text. But every one of those biomedical resources was English-only. Chinese — spoken by roughly a quarter of the world's population — had no biomedical language-understanding benchmark at all, despite a real and urgent need: Chinese electronic health records, clinical trial registries, and medical search logs were piling up, and there was no shared, standardized way to evaluate whether a model could actually read them.

The authors also identify a second, more specific gap: even the general-domain Chinese benchmark CLUE doesn't transfer to biomedical text, because it was built with uniform, i.i.d. data — convenient for benchmarking, but not representative of how medical text actually looks. Biomedical entity types are naturally long-tailed (a few common diagnoses dominate, most conditions are rare), and generalizing across different diseases or across a training/deployment split is itself part of the challenge. CBLUE (Chinese Biomedical Language Understanding Evaluation) is built to close both gaps at once: the first Chinese biomedical benchmark, and one that deliberately keeps the real-world, long-tailed, non-i.i.d. character of clinical data instead of smoothing it away.

// how it works

The method

CBLUE assembles eight tasks from four real-world Chinese biomedical sources: clinical trials (ChiCTR eligibility-criteria text), electronic health records from Class-A tertiary hospitals, medical textbooks and online consultation forums, and search logs from Alibaba's KUAKE medical search engine. Five of the eight datasets began life as CHIP2019/CHIP2020 shared tasks; of the eight, three carry a CHIP- prefix, three carry a KUAKE- prefix from the search-log source, and two (CMeEE, CMeIE) carry neither:

The task set spans sequence labeling (CMeEE, CMeIE), single-sentence and sentence-pair classification, and a normalization task that maps free-text diagnoses onto a fixed vocabulary. All data is anonymized and IRB-reviewed, and — deliberately — none of it is up- or down-sampled to balance classes: entity frequencies in CMeEE follow a long-tailed Zipf-like curve (Figure 1a), and CMeIE's 53 relations carry a two-level hierarchy of coarse relation types (Disease-Disease, Disease-Symptom, Disease-Sociology, Disease-Drug) and finer subtypes (Figure 1b). CHIP-STS goes a step further and builds in an explicit transfer-learning stress test: its question pairs cover five diseases (diabetes, hypertension, hepatitis, AIDS, breast cancer), and the diseases represented in the training set differ from those in the test set, so a model has to generalize its notion of "similar meaning" across disease types it never saw paired during training rather than merely memorizing surface patterns.

On top of the tasks, CBLUE evaluates 11 publicly available Chinese pretrained language models, each fine-tuned per task with an added task-specific output head: BERT-base, BERT-wwm-ext-base (whole-word masking), RoBERTa-large, RoBERTa-wwm-ext-base/large, ALBERT-tiny/xxlarge, ZEN (n-gram-enhanced BERT), MacBERT-base/large, and PCL-MedBERT, a medical-domain model from the Peng Cheng Laboratory that is the one baseline built specifically for this domain. Alongside the models, the authors build a human baseline following SuperGLUE's protocol: amateur annotators with no medical training are first trained on the development set (correcting mistakes against the gold standard until they master each task), then annotate the held-out test set; the majority vote across three trained annotators becomes the "Human" score reported against the models.

// what they found

The results

Table 3 reports each model's score on all eight tasks plus an "Avg." column that is the unweighted mean of the eight raw per-task numbers — mixing Micro-F1, Macro-F1 and Accuracy directly on their 0–100 scale. Trained amateur humans beat every one of the 11 models on every one of the eight tasks, with the gap widest on relation extraction (CMeIE: 66.0 human vs. 55.9 best model, a 10.1-point gap) and narrowest on search-intent classification (KUAKE-QIC: 88.0 vs. 85.5, a 2.5-point gap). By Avg., the best single model — RoBERTa-wwm-ext-large at 70.0 (MacBERT-large is next-best among the shown rows at 69.6, not a tie) — trails the human majority-vote average of 77.1 by 7.1 points.

Two results cut against the intuitive story that "bigger and more domain-specific is better." First, PCL-MedBERT — the only model pretrained specifically on medical text — finishes near the bottom of the 11 baselines (67.9 avg.), behind even the plain, general-domain BERT-base (69.1) and every whole-word-masking variant; the authors call this "not perform[ing] as well as we expected" but do not diagnose why (the paper discloses a parameter count for only one of the eleven models — BERT-base, at 110 million — and a pretraining-corpus size for none of them, so PCL-MedBERT's own architecture and training-data scale are never disclosed). Second, the tiny ALBERT-tiny closes most of its gap to BERT-base on CHIP-CDN, CHIP-STS, KUAKE-QTR and KUAKE-QQR (trailing by only 3.3–5.2 points on those four) while trailing by 8.2–18.1 points on the other four tasks (CMeEE, CMeIE, CHIP-CTC, KUAKE-QIC) — so a much smaller model is competitive specifically on the classification-style tasks, not across the board. The paper also notes, in its own words, that whole-word-masking variants "do not always yield better performance than others" specifically on CHIP-CTC, KUAKE-QIC, KUAKE-QTR and KUAKE-QQR — the authors read this as evidence that CBLUE's tasks resist easy wins from off-the-shelf pretraining tricks and call for more sophisticated technology.

An error analysis on two representative tasks (Figure 2) breaks down what actually trips the models up. In CMeEE, ambiguity (24%) and entity overlap (22%) are the two largest failure categories — the paper notes that overlapping entity spans are a frequent and genuine source of NER confusion — followed by needing domain knowledge (18%), annotation error (12%), wrong entity boundaries (9%), needing syntactic knowledge (7%), and other causes like long sequences and rare words (8%). In KUAKE-QIC, roughly half of the errors (48.7% combined) come from multiple trigger words (26.2%) and colloquial phrasing (22.5%) — the authors point out that colloquialism is simply natural in search queries, a mismatch that clean, edited pretraining text doesn't prepare a model for — with ambiguity (13.7%), rare words (11.2%), annotation error (10.0%), needing domain knowledge (8.7%) and irrelevant descriptions (7.5%) making up the rest.

// does it hold up?

The eval read

CBLUE's core contribution — a real, diverse, expert-curated Chinese biomedical benchmark where no model comes close to human performance — is genuine and was badly needed. But read as a piece of evaluation design, three things are worth flagging. First, the headline "Avg." score blends three incommensurate metrics (Micro-F1 on three tasks, Macro-F1 on two, raw Accuracy on three) into one unweighted mean with no normalization or discussion of why that's a reasonable thing to do. A model that gains five points on an easy Accuracy task and loses five on a hard Macro-F1 task looks unchanged in Avg., even though those two five-point moves are not directly comparable quantities — this is the same aggregation choice GLUE was later criticized for, inherited here without comment.

Second, the human ceiling is an amateur ceiling: the "Human" row comes from lay annotators with no medical background who were trained to competence on the dev set, not from board-certified clinicians. That is a reasonable and transparent design (it mirrors SuperGLUE's own protocol), but it means the reported 7.1-point Avg. gap between the best model and "Human" is a floor on the real gap to expert-level performance, not the gap itself — a genuinely expert human ceiling could plausibly be higher still, especially on CMeIE and CHIP-CDN, which require the domain knowledge the paper's own error analysis flags as a real bottleneck (18% of CMeEE errors, and CDN's task is literally to map free text onto standardized clinical vocabulary). Third, the paper reports a single run per model with no seeds, no repeated trials, and no variance — so a 0.3–0.4 point gap between two models (e.g. RoBERTa-wwm-ext-large's 70.0 Avg. vs. MacBERT-large's 69.6, or BERT-base's 69.1 vs. BERT-wwm-ext-base's 69.4) cannot be distinguished from training noise.

baselines

11 Chinese BERT-family encoders fine-tuned per task (BERT/RoBERTa/ALBERT/ZEN/MacBERT variants plus the medical PCL-MedBERT) — a fair, representative lineup for mid-2021 Chinese NLP, but entirely fine-tuning-era: no generative or few-shot model is evaluated, and PCL-MedBERT's own parameter count and pretraining corpus are never disclosed, so its below-BERT-base finish (67.9 vs. 69.1 Avg.) can't be attributed to size, data, or architecture.

contamination

Not directly discussed. CHIP-STS's disease-type train/test split is a genuine, explicit generalization control — a real strength — but there is no equivalent check for whether the 11 general-purpose Chinese PLMs' own large pretraining corpora overlap CBLUE's clinical-trial, EHR, or search-log sources.

what's missed

The Avg. column averages Micro-F1, Macro-F1, and Accuracy without normalization, so a fixed point-gap means different things on different tasks; the human baseline is amateur, not expert, performance; single-run results carry no variance estimate; and the benchmark itself, per the paper's own Limitations section, omits medical dialogue generation and diagnosis-support tasks and is static, so a model can look strong on it while still failing on out-of-distribution real-world inputs.

would raise confidence

Report per-metric-family breakdowns alongside (or instead of) the blended Avg.; disclose PCL-MedBERT's architecture/parameter count; run multiple seeds and report variance; add an expert (clinician) human baseline alongside the amateur one; and audit pretraining-corpus overlap the way CHIP-STS already audits train/test disease overlap.

// bottom line

CBLUE is a genuine first — the first Chinese biomedical multi-task benchmark, built from real, IRB-reviewed clinical data left in its natural long-tailed distribution, and it earns its headline claim that state-of-the-art Chinese PLMs still trail human performance across the board. But its one-number leaderboard inherits GLUE's original sin of averaging across metrics that aren't the same kind of number, its human ceiling is an amateur floor rather than an expert ceiling, and its most interesting empirical result — a purpose-built medical model losing to a plain general-domain BERT — is reported without the disclosure needed to explain it.

// takeaways

What to remember

  1. First Chinese biomedical NLU benchmark. Eight tasks — NER (CMeEE), relation extraction (CMeIE), diagnosis normalization (CHIP-CDN), sentence classification/similarity (CHIP-CTC, CHIP-STS), and search-intent/relevance (KUAKE-QIC/QTR/QQR) — built from real clinical trials, EHRs, medical forums, and search logs, left in their natural long-tailed, non-i.i.d. distribution rather than balanced.
  2. Every model trails humans on every task. Across 11 Chinese pretrained encoders, the best single-model Avg. (70.0, RoBERTa-wwm-ext-large) sits 7.1 points below the amateur-human majority-vote Avg. (77.1) — and the gap holds task-by-task, from 2.5 points on KUAKE-QIC to 10.1 points on CMeIE. The one domain-specific model, PCL-MedBERT, actually finishes below general-domain BERT-base (67.9 vs. 69.1), a puzzle the paper flags but doesn't explain.
  3. The single "Avg." score blends three different metrics. It is an unweighted mean of Micro-F1 (3 tasks), Macro-F1 (2 tasks) and Accuracy (3 tasks) with no normalization — useful for ranking models at a glance, but not a single measurable quantity. Trust the per-task, same-metric comparisons; treat Avg. as a convenience score, not a scientific one.
