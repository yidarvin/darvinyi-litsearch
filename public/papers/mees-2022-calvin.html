CALVIN: A Benchmark for Language-Conditioned Policy Learning for Long-Horizon Robot Manipulation Tasks

A from-scratch PyBullet benchmark that pairs 24 hours of unscripted, teleoperated "play" across four structurally related tabletop rooms with a deliberately hard test: chain up to five free-form language instructions in a row, from raw onboard sensors, with zero-shot transfer to a room the policy never trained in.

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

By late 2021, a growing line of work had shown that robots could learn language-conditioned continuous control — following a natural-language instruction end-to-end to move a real or simulated arm — but almost every one of these systems had been built and evaluated on its own private setup. The authors single out two shortfalls in prior benchmarks. Vision-and-language navigation and manipulation datasets like ALFRED forced the agent to choose among "seven predefined action primitives" rather than produce continuous, closed-loop motor commands, so they tested language grounding over a discrete action vocabulary rather than the raw control problem itself. And the tabletop imitation-learning work most directly comparable to CALVIN — Lynch and Sermanet's multi-context imitation learning (MCIL), trained on "unstructured 'play' data" — had never been packaged as a reusable benchmark: it covered only 18 subtasks with long-horizon chains of at most 4 instructions in a single environment, and, in the authors' words, "although considered a state-of-the-art approach, no public implementation of MCIL is available," so nobody else could reproduce or build on it.

CALVIN's stated aim is to close that gap directly: an open-source, from-scratch simulated benchmark "to learn long-horizon language-conditioned tasks," which the authors describe as "the first public benchmark of instruction following, to our knowledge, that combines: natural language conditioning, multimodal high-dimensional inputs, 7-DOF continuous control, and long-horizon robotic object manipulation" — four things, note, that do not include generalizing to a new scene. That comes separately: the authors say they "include a challenging zero-shot evaluation by training on large play corpora covering three environments and testing on an unseen scene," the split that asks whether a policy can act in a room it never trained in at all. The authors call it "the hardest combination since the policy has never seen the test environment during training," and the results bear that out on average: averaged over the four sensor configurations, MTLC runs 37.8% zero-shot against 43.5% multi-environment and 51.5% single-environment. Not uniformly, though — with static RGB alone the zero-shot split actually edges the multi-environment one, 38.6% to 35.6%.

// how it works

The method

CALVIN is built from three pieces: an environment, a dataset, and a challenge (evaluation protocol). The environment is four structurally identical-but-distinct tabletop rooms (A, B, C, D), each simulated in PyBullet with a 7-DOF Franka Emika Panda arm and parallel gripper, a desk with a sliding door and a drawer, a button that toggles a green LED, a switch that controls a light bulb, and three colored, unicolored rectangular blocks. The desk, robot, and static camera sit in the same relative pose in every room; what differs across A–D is texture and the exact position of the sliding door, drawer, button, and switch. The paper is explicit that this is a deliberate first-version scoping choice, not an oversight: "Due to the general difficulty of language-conditioned multi-task closed-loop control, we reduced the complexity of the objects to unicolored primitive shapes. If future advances in this field require new challenges we will reflect this by extending CALVIN to environments with more realistic and diverse objects."

Every policy under test perceives the scene through a defined, swappable sensor suite: RGB-D from a static camera (200×200) and a wrist-mounted gripper camera (84×84), a vision-based tactile sensor built on TACTO, and proprioceptive state (end-effector position/orientation, gripper width, seven joint positions, gripper action). Actions can be specified as an absolute Cartesian pose, a relative Cartesian displacement with respect to the gripper frame, or direct joint-space targets, all streamed to the robot at 30 Hz.

The dataset underneath all of this is 24 hours of teleoperated "play" — six hours per environment — collected with an HTC Vive headset by three untrained operators whose only instruction was to "explore the environment without dropping objects from the table." This yields roughly 2.4M interaction steps and roughly 40M short-horizon (1–2 second) windows usable for relabeled goal-conditioned imitation learning. Because play data is unscripted, only a slice of it is worth labeling with language: the main text says the authors collect "over 400 crowd-sourced natural language instructions corresponding to over 34 tasks," while the appendix separately states "389 unique language instructions for 34 tasks" — the paper gives these two different figures in two different places and never reconciles them (389 is below the "over 400" the main text claims, and 34 is not "over 34"). The labeling procedure then detects, in the recorded environment state, which of the 34 defined tasks a given window actually accomplishes, attaching one of roughly 11 synonymous phrasings per task. By design, only about 1% of the total recorded interaction data ends up paired with a language label, meant to mirror a real deployment where exhaustive annotation of robot experience isn't realistic. Precomputed MiniLM sentence embeddings (384-dim, 30,522-word vocabulary) ship alongside the raw instruction text.

All 34 tasks (rotating, pushing, lifting, and placing the three blocks; opening/closing the drawer; moving the slider; stacking/unstacking blocks; toggling the light bulb and LED) are defined purely as a change in simulator state between the first and last frame of a sequence, which lets the environment auto-detect task completion in any recorded or generated window without needing per-step reward shaping. The CALVIN Challenge then composes these into two evaluation regimes. Multi-Task Language Control (MTLC) resets to a valid unseen demonstration's start state and runs 10 rollouts per task across all 34 tasks, using test-time instructions that never appeared in training. Long-Horizon MTLC (LH-MTLC) chains five of these subtasks into a single rollout, filtering the space of possible chains for unfeasible sequences ("close the drawer" → "place in drawer"), cyclic ones ("move slider right" → "move slider left" → "move slider right"), and near-duplicate ones, down to 1,000 unique instruction chains; the robot resets to a neutral pose between chains specifically to prevent the policy from inferring the task from its start pose rather than from language. Both regimes are run under three train/test splits of increasing difficulty — Single Environment (train and test on D), Multi Environment (train on A,B,C,D, test on D), and Zero-Shot Multi Environment (train on A,B,C only, test on the never-seen D) — crossed with four sensor combinations, giving the full grid reported in the results.

To establish a first number on this grid, the authors re-implement Lynch and Sermanet's multi-context imitation learning (MCIL) themselves, since no public code existed. MCIL relabels the unsegmented play stream into short goal-reaching demonstrations and trains a sequence-to-sequence conditional variational autoencoder that infers a latent "plan" z for getting from the current state to a goal, with the maximum-likelihood objective

LLfP = 𝔼(τ,xg)∼Dplay [ Σt=0|τ| log πθ(at | xt, xg) ] // goal-conditioned imitation objective over relabeled play windows (τ, x_g); at test time the goal is a language embedding rather than an image, and π_θ additionally infers and follows the latent plan z

relative to the original setup, CALVIN's version of the benchmark is harder along exactly the two axes its introduction promised: "more subtasks (34 vs 18), longer long-horizon evaluation sequences (5 vs 4)," plus a sensor suite and a hard zero-shot room the original MCIL paper never tested against.

// what they found

The results

The full baseline grid — four sensor combinations × three train/test splits × six columns (MTLC plus LH-MTLC at chain positions 1 through 5) — is reported as a single table (reproduced below). Two headline patterns hold across every sensor combination. First, single-instruction performance is moderate but far from solved: the best MTLC number anywhere in the grid is 54.2% (static camera + tactile sensing, single-environment D→D), and the other three single-environment MTLC numbers range from 46.1% to 53.9%. Second, chaining instructions is where the benchmark bites: the best full five-instruction LH-MTLC success rate reported anywhere in the paper is 0.08%, achieved only twice — the static-RGB-only and static+gripper-RGB configurations, both in the single-environment (D→D) setting — while every multi-environment (A,B,C,D→D) and zero-shot (A,B,C→D) configuration drops to 0% by the fourth or fifth chained instruction.

Adding more sensors does not reliably help, and sometimes hurts: adding the gripper camera to the static-RGB-only baseline moves single-environment MTLC from 53.9% to 51.8%, and the full static+gripper RGB-D combination (the richest input tested) is the weakest single-environment MTLC number of the four sensor configurations at 46.1%. The authors' own reading is direct: "The success rate stays comparable when including a gripper camera, depth channels or tactile sensing." They hypothesize the policy may need relative (gripper-frame) rather than absolute (world-frame) actions to benefit from the gripper view — a hypothesis the paper states but does not test in this version of the benchmark. On the qualitative failure side, the paper reports that the policy's performance "depends significantly on the initial position of the robot" and that it "sometimes correctly executes block manipulation tasks, but confuses the red and blue block colors in the instruction," attributing the latter to the language encoder embedding "red" and "blue" similarly — again offered as a hypothesis rather than something isolated by a controlled ablation in this paper.

// does it hold up?

The eval read

CALVIN is a genuinely new benchmark rather than a repackaged one — its own environments, its own play-data collection protocol, its own task-completion detector — so the usual worry about test-set contamination from internet pretraining simply doesn't apply here: nothing in the 24 hours of teleoperated data or the 389 language instructions could have leaked into an LLM's or vision model's pretraining corpus, since it didn't exist before this paper. The more relevant validity question for a simulation benchmark like this is scene-level leakage between "training" and "held-out" environments, and CALVIN's zero-shot protocol is reasonably careful about it: environment D is withheld entirely at both the pixel level (different textures) and the object-placement level (different door/drawer/button/switch positions) from the A,B,C→D split, and the paper is careful to note this is the hardest of its three splits precisely because "the policy has never seen the test environment during training."

Two things temper how much a single number from this benchmark should be trusted, though. First, only one baseline family is reported — a re-implementation of MCIL, built from scratch because "no public implementation of MCIL is available" — and no reinforcement-learning agent is run against the same 34-task/1,000-chain protocol, even though the paper itself explicitly flags that "there is no constraint to use imitation learning approaches to solve CALVIN tasks, as approaches that use reinforcement learning to learn language-conditioned policies can also be applied." The environment already exposes the sparse task-completion signal an RL agent would need (the same automatic per-task detector that scores MTLC), so this isn't a fundamental limitation of the benchmark, but it does mean the launch paper establishes only an imitation-learning floor, not a cross-paradigm one. Second, the paper's own explanations for its failure modes — initial-pose dependence, red/blue color confusion, and a suggested link to "causal confusion between the proprioceptive information and the target actions" — are all offered as qualitative hypotheses in the discussion, not as isolated, ablated findings; a reader taking the 0.08% ceiling on LH-MTLC at face value should note that the paper does not yet tell you which of these mechanisms is actually driving it, or by how much.

The paper also carries an unreconciled internal contradiction on a basic count: the main text describes the language collection as "over 400 crowd-sourced natural language instructions corresponding to over 34 tasks," while the appendix's own detailed accounting gives "389 unique language instructions for 34 tasks" (consistent with the appendix's separately stated "approximately 11 synonymous instructions per task" × 34 tasks). Both halves of the main-text figure are contradicted, not merely rounded: 389 is below the "over 400" lower bound the main text asserts, and the task count is exactly 34, not "over 34." The paper offers no explanation for either gap, and none is available from the text to reconstruct one.

Two smaller internal inconsistencies are worth flagging because they cut at the numbers a reader is most likely to quote. First, the headline long-horizon cells do not divide. Fig. 8 labels the LH-MTLC columns "No. Instructions in a Row (1000 chains)," but a success count out of exactly 1,000 can only land on a multiple of 0.1% — and the table prints 0.08% in six cells and 0.17% in two. Neither is reachable from a 1,000-chain denominator (they would require 0.8 and 1.7 successful chains), and the paper names no other denominator anywhere. The cells are internally consistent with a denominator of 1,200 — 1/1200 rounds to 0.08%, 2/1200 to 0.17% — but that figure appears nowhere in the paper, so it is a reconstruction, not a reading. Second, the introduction says the baseline "achieves up to 53.9% success rate in short horizon tasks," yet 53.9% is one specific cell (static-RGB-only, train and test on D); the actual maximum in the paper's own table is 54.2%, with static RGB plus tactile. The experiments section states the coordinate correctly; only the summary drops it.

And the priority claim moves between sections. The introduction is careful — "the first public benchmark of instruction following, to our knowledge," scoped to four listed properties, with the zero-shot evaluation introduced separately a few lines later. The conclusion restates it as "the first public benchmark of instruction following that combines" the same four properties "in both seen and unseen environments," dropping the hedge and folding in the generalization claim the introduction kept apart. A reader working from the conclusion gets a materially stronger claim than the one the paper actually argues for.

baselines

Exactly one baseline family (a from-scratch MCIL re-implementation) across four sensor combinations; no RL agent is run against the same protocol despite the paper explicitly noting RL is a viable alternative and the environment already providing the sparse signal an RL agent would need.

contamination

Not applicable in the pretraining-leakage sense — a purpose-built simulation with fresh teleoperated data. The relevant risk is scene leakage between train/test rooms, which the zero-shot A,B,C→D split is explicitly designed to control for by holding out an entire room's textures and fixture positions.

what's missed

Pass/fail rollout counts give no attribution of failure cause; the paper's own hypotheses (pose dependence, color confusion, causal confusion) are stated qualitatively, not isolated via ablation in this version of the benchmark.

would raise confidence

A second baseline family (e.g. an RL agent using the built-in task-completion signal) run on the identical 34-task/1,000-chain grid, plus a controlled ablation targeting the color-confusion and causal-confusion hypotheses the discussion only speculates about.

// bottom line

CALVIN is honest about being hard by design — its own baseline tops out at 0.08% on the full five-instruction chain and 0% the moment training crosses environments — which is exactly the kind of headroom a benchmark needs to stay useful for years. What it doesn't yet give a reader is a second reference point (an RL baseline, or an ablation isolating why the imitation baseline fails) to know whether that ceiling reflects the task's genuine difficulty or specifically the limits of the one imitation-learning recipe tested at launch.

// takeaways

What to remember

  1. CALVIN pairs 24 hours of unscripted, teleoperated "play" data (six hours per room, four PyBullet rooms A–D sharing the same desk/robot layout but different textures and fixture positions) with only ~1% of that data language-labeled — 389 unique instructions across 34 tasks — meant to mirror how little a real deployment could exhaustively annotate.
  2. Its evaluation is deliberately unforgiving: a re-implemented multi-context imitation learning (MCIL) baseline tops out at 54.2% on single-instruction control (MTLC) in its easiest single-environment setting, but MTLC swings widely with sensors and split — as low as 46.1% across the four single-environment sensor variants, and down to 30.8% across the full 12-condition grid — while the best it ever manages on the full five-chained-instruction test (LH-MTLC) is 0.08%, and that only in the easiest, single-room setting — every multi-room or zero-shot configuration falls to 0% by chain position four or five.
  3. The zero-shot test (train on A,B,C, evaluate on never-seen D) is a real generalization check, but a scoped one — new textures and fixture placements, not new objects or skills — a limitation the authors state directly rather than hide, alongside a promise to extend the object set "if future advances in this field require new challenges."
  4. Only one method family (imitation learning) is benchmarked at launch, despite the paper itself noting reinforcement-learning approaches are equally applicable to the same sparse task-completion signal CALVIN already provides — so the launch numbers establish a floor for one paradigm, not a comparison across paradigms.
