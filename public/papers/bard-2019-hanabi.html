The Hanabi Challenge: A New Frontier for AI Research

Hanabi is a purely cooperative card game for two to five players where you can see everyone's hand but your own — success depends on reading intent from a partner's limited hints and actions. The authors package it into a benchmark with two regimes, self-play and ad-hoc teams, release an open-source Hanabi Learning Environment, and show that even a population-based RL agent trained for 20 billion self-play steps falls short of independently-built hand-coded bots, then collapses to near-zero when paired with a stranger trained the same way.

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

By 2019, game domains had driven a string of superhuman AI milestones — chess, checkers, Go, backgammon, and heads-up poker — but every one of them was either a single-agent problem or an adversarial, two-player zero-sum game. In zero-sum games, agents can chase a meaningful worst-case guarantee just by finding any equilibrium policy, since different equilibria in a two-player zero-sum game are interchangeable. Hanabi is neither exclusively two-player nor zero-sum: it is purely cooperative for two to five players, so the value of an agent's policy depends entirely on the (possibly very different) policies its teammates happen to have learned, and there can be multiple locally-optimal equilibria that are much worse than the best one — exactly the kind of coordination failure that trips up independent learners in the multi-agent RL literature.

The game's other twist is its information structure: each player can see every hand except their own, and the only sanctioned communication — a "hint" pointing out all of a partner's cards of one rank or colour — consumes a shared, finite resource (the group starts with eight information tokens total). There are provably too few hints to fully identify all 25 playable cards through explicit information alone, so competent play requires reading a partner's intent from the specific hint or action they chose, not just the literal information it conveys — the kind of theory-of-mind reasoning humans use in everyday social inference, and something largely absent from prior multi-agent RL testbeds. Existing communication-focused multi-agent setups (Foerster et al.'s DIAL, Sukhbaatar et al.'s CommNet, Lewis et al.'s negotiation dialogues) instead give agents a free, separate "cheap talk" channel disjoint from their environment actions, which sidesteps the harder problem of communicating implicitly through actions that also have to accomplish something in the world — closer in spirit to the bidding round of bridge than to an unconstrained side-channel. The paper argues Hanabi is the first widely-accessible benchmark that combines cooperative reward, genuine imperfect information about your own state, and action-constrained communication in one place, and proposes it — together with an open-source environment and a concrete evaluation protocol — as a new frontier once the classic adversarial and single-agent testbeds started running out of headroom.

// how it works

The method

Hanabi is played with a 50-card deck: five colours (red, green, blue, yellow, white), each with three 1s, two each of 2s/3s/4s, and a single 5. Each player holds 4 cards (5 with two or three players) facing outward, so — uniquely among common card games — every player can see everyone's hand except their own. On a turn the active player either hints (names a rank or colour and points out every one of a partner's cards matching it, at a cost of one of the group's shared information tokens, of which there are 8 at the start), plays a card from their own hand onto the matching colour stack (successful only if it is the next rank up; failure discards the card and costs one of 3 shared lives), or discards a card to recover a token and draw a new one. The game ends when all five stacks are completed, three lives are lost, or the deck runs out and every player has taken one final turn; the score is the total number of cards across all five stacks (max 25) if the group hasn't run out of lives, and zero otherwise — a scoring convention the paper is explicit about departing from, since much prior Hanabi work instead scored a failed game as the number of cards successfully played before the failure.

The paper proposes two distinct challenges for evaluating agents, plus an open-source codebase, the Hanabi Learning Environment (Python/C++, an OpenAI-Gym-like interface), to run them: self-play, learning one joint policy that all seats share, split further into a sample-limited regime (at most 100 million environment steps, modelled on the Machado et al. Atari evaluation protocol) and an unlimited regime (no compute/sample cap, for asymptotic performance); and ad-hoc team play, where an agent is evaluated with teammates drawn from a held-out pool it has never trained with. For ad-hoc evaluation, the recommended protocol gives the agent ten self-play games of its held-out teammates' strategy to observe before playing a single game in their place, repeated across at least 1000 trials and at least 100 different sets of the ten "peek" games, with pairwise crosstables recommended to show how diverse the pool actually is.

Two families of agents are benchmarked. The rule-based bots — SmartBot (tracks public belief about every player's hand to reason about what a partner already knows), HatBot (uses modular "hat puzzle" arithmetic to encode a joint recommended action for every other player into a single hint) and WTFWThat (a variant of the same hat-puzzle technique that instead directly encodes each player's own card information into the hint rather than a recommended action, at the cost of extra bookkeeping the paper calls impractical for humans), and FireFlower (human-style conventions plus a 2-ply search over an evaluation function) — were built independently of this paper and stand in for the quality of play achievable with hand-coded domain knowledge. The learning agents are ACHA (Actor-Critic-Hanabi-Agent: an IMPALA/V-trace-style population-based actor-critic with a 2-layer, 256-unit LSTM policy, population size 30-50, trained up to 20 billion environment steps — roughly 100 CPU-years — for the unlimited regime), Rainbow (a feedforward, 2-layer 512-unit distributional DQN variant from the Dopamine framework, trained for the 100-million-step sample-limited regime in about 7 days on one V100 GPU), and BAD (the Bayesian Action Decoder, evaluated only in the two-player setting, which conditions a Bayesian public-belief update directly on the acting agent's own policy rather than relying on an implicit RNN belief state).

// what they found

The results

Table 1 of the paper lays out mean self-play score (with standard error), plus percentage of perfect (25-point) games, for every agent at every player count. Neither Rainbow nor ACHA reaches the performance of the best hand-coded agent, SmartBot, in the two-player game (22.99, 29.6% perfect); neither reaches any of the rule-based bots once there are more than two players. ACHA — despite roughly 20 billion training steps per learner — scores 22.73 (2P, 15.1% perfect), 20.24 (3P, 1.1%), 21.57 (4P, 2.4%), and 16.80 (5P, 0% perfect); Rainbow, trained for only 100 million steps, scores 20.64 (2P, 2.5%), 18.71 (3P, 0.2%), 18.00 (4P, 0%), and 15.26 (5P, 0%) — consistently behind ACHA at every player count, and both agents decline sharply as player count rises to five. By contrast, WTFWThat's hat-encoding scheme reaches 24.83 (4P, 87.2% perfect) and 24.89 (5P, 91.5% perfect) — essentially solving the game — and even FireFlower's human-style conventions clear 21-22 points with 26-53% perfect games at two through four players. BAD, evaluated only in the two-player game, posts the best learned self-play score in the paper at 23.92 (58.6% perfect games) — the best score of any agent, learned or rule-based, in the 2P column of Table 1 (ahead of SmartBot's 22.99, FireFlower's 22.56, and WTFWThat's 19.45 there); only WTFWThat's three-to-five-player scores (24.20-24.89), a different regime than the one BAD reports, exceed it.

The paper also surfaces a qualitative split in how the two RL agents fail. Independent ACHA training runs land in a wide array of different local minima — roughly a third of independent two-player runs plateau below 15 points and stop improving — and different final policies encode genuinely different, idiosyncratic conventions for the same information (e.g., one agent's colour hints mean "your 4th card is discardable," another's mean something else entirely). Rainbow runs, in contrast, show low variance across seeds and converge on nearly identical strategies — hinting for colour 3-4 times less often than ACHA, with no evidence of colour-specific conventions, and relying almost entirely on rank hints to mean "your most recently drawn card is playable."

// does it hold up?

The eval read

The self-play numbers are honest but easy to over-read as "how close to solved is Hanabi." The strongest rule-based scores (WTFWThat's 91.5% perfect games at five players) come from an encoding scheme that packs each player's own card information — not a recommended action — into a single hint via modular hat-puzzle arithmetic, at a bookkeeping cost the paper itself calls "impractical for humans to use" — a strategy no human pair or team could plausibly execute, and one the authors themselves flag as "provid[ing] a lower-bound for optimal play [rather] than a baseline suggestive of human performance." Treating "beats SmartBot" or "beats WTFWThat" as the bar for a "solved" Hanabi risks conflating brute-force combinatorial encoding with the theory-of-mind reasoning the paper actually wants agents to develop.

The paper's real headline, though, is the ad-hoc-team crosstable in Figure 5, and it is far more damning than the self-play table alone would suggest. Ten independently-trained ACHA agents plus the best Rainbow agent all score above 20 in two-player self-play (diagonal entries from 20.52 to 22.84) — reasonable, competitive Hanabi play by the paper's own self-play standard. But pair any two of them together and performance collapses: agent 3, which scores 21.67 with itself, manages just 0.01 points when teamed with agent 1; agent 8 (20.70 self-play) scores 0.10 or less against seven of its ten possible partners. The four-player crosstable tells the same story at a slightly less extreme scale — self-play scores of roughly 18-20.6 fall to single digits against almost every other agent, with most off-diagonal cells in the 0.1-5 range and only a handful reaching as high as 7.28. Averaged over its ten actual ad-hoc partners, agent 1's two-player score falls from 22.84 in self-play to a stranger-only mean of just 1.32 (Figure 5a's printed row average of 3.27 blends that stranger performance back in with the 22.84 self-play cell, so it understates the collapse) — an order-of-magnitude drop that a self-play-only report would never reveal.

baselines

The rule-based bots (SmartBot, HatBot, WTFWThat, FireFlower) are independently authored community implementations, not strawmen built by the paper's team — a genuine strength. But WTFWThat and HatBot are combinatorial encoding schemes rather than plausible teammates for anything, human or learned, so "beats the rule-based bots" is not one uniform bar; the paper is candid about this distinction, but a reader skimming Table 1 could easily miss it.

contamination

Not applicable in the train/test-leakage sense — there is no held-out test set to leak into. The relevant conflict instead is authorship: the DeepMind-heavy author list both designed the benchmark/protocol and built ACHA and BAD, the two strongest learning agents reported. The paper mitigates this by centering its narrative on how badly its own agents underperform independent hand-coded bots, which is a self-critical framing rather than a self-serving one.

what's missed

A single self-play mean score hides two failure modes entirely: (1) it does not reveal that failed games are scored zero here versus "cards played before failure" in most prior Hanabi papers (footnote 9), a scoring change the authors say "has considerable impact in the ad-hoc teamwork setting" — meaning this paper's numbers are not directly comparable to earlier Hanabi results like Bouzy's 24.92/92% or the CIG-2018 competition's 17.52-20.57 averages without accounting for it; and (2) it says nothing about robustness to an unfamiliar partner, which is exactly what the ad-hoc crosstable exposes as near-total failure.

would raise confidence

A larger, strategically diverse ad-hoc pool — the ten-ACHA-seeds-plus-one-Rainbow pool is drawn from only two algorithms, and hand-coded bots were excluded purely for engineering reasons ("SmartBot crashing" when paired with ACHA), not principled ones. Reconciling the paper's zero-on-failure scoring with the older played-cards convention would also let its self-play numbers be compared directly against the pre-2019 Hanabi literature it otherwise only discusses qualitatively.

// bottom line

Hanabi earns its place as a benchmark not because its self-play scores are dramatic — ACHA's 22.73 at two players and steady decline to 16.80 at five is a fairly ordinary "learning agents underperform hand-coded rules" story — but because the ad-hoc-team crosstable turns that ordinary story into a sharp one: agents that look competent by every self-play metric collapse to near-zero the moment their partner wasn't also in on the same training run. That is the paper's actual contribution to the field's understanding of self-play RL, and it is a critique that generalizes well past Hanabi itself.

// takeaways

What to remember

  1. Hanabi packages purely cooperative, 2-5 player, imperfect-information-about-your-own-hand gameplay with a scarce shared communication resource (8 information tokens) into a benchmark that is neither zero-sum nor single-agent — a genuinely different challenge from the era's Go/chess/Atari/poker milestones — and ships it with an open-source Hanabi Learning Environment plus concrete self-play and ad-hoc evaluation protocols.
  2. Even with roughly 20 billion self-play steps (~100 CPU-years) via a population-based actor-critic (ACHA), the best RL agents fall short of independently-built hand-coded bots at every player count beyond two, and every learning agent's score declines as the player count rises from two to five.
  3. The paper's sharpest finding is the ad-hoc-team crosstable: independently-trained agents that each score above 20/25 in self-play collapse to single digits, and often below 1 point, when paired with a stranger trained the same way — exposing that today's self-play RL learns brittle, agent-specific conventions rather than anything resembling a robust, human-compatible communication protocol.
