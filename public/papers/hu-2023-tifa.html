TIFA: Accurate and Interpretable Text-to-Image Faithfulness Evaluation with Question Answering

Turn a text-to-image prompt into a set of GPT-3-generated, UnifiedQA-filtered question-answer pairs, then score a generated image by whether an off-the-shelf VQA model can answer them correctly — no CLIP embedding, no captioning model in the loop, and a Spearman correlation with human judgment nearly double CLIPScore's (0.597 vs. 0.332).

What problem is this solving?

By early 2023, text-to-image models routinely failed to render what their prompts actually said — miscounting objects, dropping attributes, binding the wrong colors to the wrong things — and the field had no automatic metric that could say which element went wrong, only a single opaque score. The incumbent, CLIPScore, takes the cosine similarity between a frozen CLIP's image and text embeddings; the paper's own Figure 1 walks through a case where CLIPScore rates a worse Stable Diffusion v1.5 image (missing the horse-jumping-a-gate) higher than a better v2.1 image, because CLIP is known to struggle at counting and compositional reasoning. Captioning-based alternatives run an image captioner and score the resulting caption against the text input with n-gram metrics (CIDEr, SPICE); the authors argue these inherit two problems at once — a captioning model can ignore salient regions or invent irrelevant ones, and text-generation evaluation is itself unreliable. A third family, DALL-Eval, uses object detectors to check faithfulness, but only works on synthesized text templates and covers a narrow set of axes (object, counting, color, spatial relation) — it has no way to ask about material, shape, activities, or context. TIFA's target is a metric that is reference-free (no gold image or gold caption required), fine-grained (a per-element accuracy breakdown rather than one number), and grounded directly in whether the generated pixels answer questions a human would ask of the prompt.

// how it works

The method

Given a text input T, TIFA first generates N multiple-choice question-answer tuples {Qi, Ci, Ai} with GPT-3 (specifically code-davinci-002): a single in-context-learning inference, conditioned on 15 hand-annotated examples, walks through element extraction (pull noun phrases, verbs, adjectives, adverbs, and short parse-tree spans — for "a photo of three dogs" that's photo, three, dogs), element-category classification into one of 12 fixed categories (object, activity, animal, food, counting, color, material, spatial, location, shape, attribute, other), and question generation — two questions per element, one binary ("are there three dogs?" → yes/no) and one multiple-choice ("how many dogs are there?" → 1/2/3/4). A second model, UnifiedQA, then filters the GPT-3 output: a pair is kept only if UnifiedQA's multiple-choice answer matches the gold answer and the word-level F1 between UnifiedQA's free-form answer and the gold answer exceeds 0.7. On a human check of 1,000 filtered pairs, only 7 were judged unreasonable.

To score a generated image I, a VQA model answers each surviving question by picking the highest-probability choice, and faithfulness is simply the accuracy of those answers against the gold answers inferred from the text:

faithfulness(T, I) = 1N Σi=1..N 𝟙[AiVQA = Ai] // A_i^VQA = the VQA model's chosen answer to question i given image I; range [0,1]

The paper tests five open-domain vision-language backbones as the VQA component — GIT-large, VILT-B/32, OFA-large, mPLUG-large, and BLIP-2 FlanT5-XL — and, weighing accuracy, correlation with human judgment, and runtime, recommends mPLUG-large as the default (Section 5.4). Because GIT, VILT, OFA, and mPLUG were never trained to answer multiple-choice questions, TIFA first decodes a free-form answer and then picks whichever choice is closest by SBERT similarity; BLIP-2's frozen FlanT5 decoder can pick a choice directly.

TIFA v1.0 packages this method as a fixed, reusable benchmark: 4,081 text inputs — 2,000 image captions from the COCO validation set (which have real gold images, useful as a reference point) and 2,081 free-form prompts drawn from DrawBench (161), PartiPrompt (1,420), and PaintSkill (500) — paired with 25,829 pre-generated, pre-filtered question-answer tuples (17,226 binary, 8,603 multiple-choice; 6.3 questions per prompt on average) spanning the same 12 element categories. Because the questions are pre-generated and frozen, benchmarking a new text-to-image model with TIFA v1.0 is deterministic: a user only has to generate images for the 4,081 prompts and run VQA, never regenerate the questions. Anticipating that code-davinci-002 itself might become unavailable, the authors separately fine-tune and release a LLaMA-2-7B checkpoint that reproduces the question-generation step for anyone building a new, custom TIFA-style benchmark.

// what they found

The results

The paper's central validity claim rests on a human study: 20 graduate-student annotators rated 800 generated images (from 160 TIFA v1.0 prompts, one image each from VQ-Diffusion, minDALL-E, and Stable Diffusion v1.1/v1.5/v2.1) on a 1–5 faithfulness Likert scale (2 annotations per image, Krippendorff's α = 0.67 — "substantial" agreement, not perfect). Correlating each automatic metric's scores against these human ratings, TIFA beats every prior metric on every VQA backbone tested, on both correlation statistics reported:

Every one of the five TIFA variants (49.3–59.7 Spearman) clears every one of the five prior metrics (18.3–34.0 Spearman) — the weakest VQA backbone still beats the strongest baseline by 15 points. The paper's own headline framing (Spearman's ρ = 0.60 vs. 0.33 for CLIPScore, vs. 0.34 for the best captioning metric, METEOR) is the mPLUG row against the best of the rest. TIFA (mPLUG) is printed in bold in Table 2, the only bolded data row (the "Caption-Based"/"Ours" group labels are bold too, but those are headers, not results) — it's the paper's recommended default, not an "our metric wins by fiat" marker, since every row in that half of the table is a TIFA variant. Bold does mark a genuine per-column winner elsewhere in the paper: in Appendix Table 4, minDALL-E and VQ-Diffusion also carry bold cells on the categories they win, so the convention is a real winner-marker throughout, just one that happens to single out mPLUG alone within Table 2's all-TIFA half.

Applied to seven text-to-image models spanning 2017–2023 (AttnGAN, X-LXMERT, VQ-Diffusion, minDALL-E, and Stable Diffusion v1.1/v1.5/v2.1), TIFA v1.0 reproduces the field's known ordering — TIFA score climbs from roughly 58 (AttnGAN, X-LXMERT) to 82.0 (Stable Diffusion v2.1) — but also breaks the aggregate apart by category (Appendix Table 4): Stable Diffusion v2.1 has the highest overall TIFA score and wins 8 of the 12 element-category columns (counting, spatial, attribute, activity, object, location, animal/human, material), yet its own single weakest category is shape (66.7) — one of the four columns it loses, this one to minDALL-E (69.6). minDALL-E also wins "other" (74.6) and food (82.8), and VQ-Diffusion wins color (84.2) — a reminder that the overall ranking hides which model is actually best at which failure mode. Ground-truth COCO images never score a perfect 1.0 either — the paper reports real images beating synthesized ones in every category except material, color, and location, where the gap is small — which the authors attribute to a mix of noisy COCO crowd-worker captions and imperfect VQA rather than to the images themselves. Feeding in more entities also degrades every generator's score once the prompt names more than 5 objects/animals/foods (Figure 9), while the ground-truth COCO images stay flat — direct evidence, independent of any single model, that current text-to-image systems specifically struggle with compositionality rather than with any one category of content. The category columns are wildly uneven in size, though, which the table doesn't show: §4.2 gives the element occurrence counts as object 7,854, animal/human 3,501, attribute 3,399, activity 2,851, spatial 2,265, location 1,840, colour 1,743, counting 986, food 911, material 209, other 201, and shape just 69. So minDALL-E's 69.6-to-66.7 shape "win" rests on a slice roughly 114× smaller than the object column, with no confidence interval reported anywhere.

// does it hold up?

The eval read

TIFA's own headline correlation numbers, 0.493–0.597 Spearman depending on the VQA backbone, are a large improvement over CLIPScore's 0.332 — but they're a moderate-to-strong correlation, not near-perfect agreement, so a meaningful amount of daylight remains between TIFA's ranking and the human ranking even with the best backbone. And the correlation study it rests on is modest in scope by 2026 standards: 160 of TIFA v1.0's 4,081 prompts, 800 images from five 2022-era generators, each image rated twice by 20 graduate students at α = 0.67 ("substantial," the paper's own word, not "high"). The paper doesn't report a confidence interval or per-model breakdown of that correlation, so it's unclear whether TIFA's advantage over CLIPScore holds uniformly across the five generators or is driven by a subset of them.

The metric's accuracy is also structurally entangled with its VQA component's accuracy, and the paper's own Table 3 quantifies the gap: against 1,029 human-answered questions on 126 images, the recommended mPLUG backbone only gets 84.5% VQA accuracy itself — meaning roughly 1 in 6 of the VQA answers TIFA treats as its reading of the generated image is itself wrong. (The gold answers being scored against are a separate, much cleaner artifact: only 7 of 1,000 filtered question-answer pairs were judged unreasonable in the paper's own human check.) Figure 11 breaks down, per backbone, what fraction of a wrong TIFA answer is attributable to the VQA model rather than to the generated image: mPLUG is under 25% VQA-attributable, but VILT's blue (image-error) segment is visibly smaller — closer to 45% of VILT's errors trace back to the VQA model, not the generation. The paper never reports this split as an exact percentage per backbone or folds a VQA-error estimate into the published TIFA scores; a generator's TIFA score and its VQA backbone's own competence are conflated in the number a reader ultimately sees on a leaderboard.

The construction of the questions themselves also bounds what TIFA can detect, though less than it first appears. Relations between elements are extracted too — "spatial" is one of the 12 element categories (2,265 spatial elements in TIFA v1.0), and Figure 4's own "next to" element generates cross-entity questions like "is the dog next to the ocean?" So TIFA does check some interactions, not just isolated attributes. Conjunctions do get generated too, incidentally rather than by design — Appendix A's COCO example produces "is the surfer walking on the beach?", predicating both properties of one entity, and it discriminates (SD v1.1 answers no, v2.1 yes). The sharper limit is in the aggregation. TIFA's score is an unweighted mean over questions (§3.2), while the paper's own human-annotation guideline weights them: Appendix C.1 instructs raters that “objects are the most important elements — if an object is missing, then consider all related attributes, activity… missing.” So a missing dog costs the human rater every question about the dog, and costs TIFA one question among many. And TIFA is explicit that it evaluates faithfulness, not image quality — a photorealistic five-legged dog on a beach can score perfectly, since the annotation guideline behind Table 2's human study directs raters to score faithfulness alone ("here we only care about text-image match"). Finally, the question generator itself, code-davinci-002, was deprecated by OpenAI on March 23, 2023 — two days after this paper's first arXiv posting — consistent with why the paper separately gives its reasons for releasing a fine-tuned LLaMA-2-7B checkpoint (the generator "might change privately," plus the cost of repeated GPT-3 API calls, per §4.3 and Appendix D); the fixed TIFA v1.0 question set itself is unaffected (it was pre-generated), but no one can regenerate a new TIFA-style benchmark with the paper's original pipeline today.

baselines

Fair for 2023: BLEU-4, ROUGE-L, METEOR, and SPICE (all captioning-based, via a BLIP-2 FlanT5-XL captioner) plus CLIPScore (ViT-B/32) were the standard reference-free and captioning-based alternatives at the time. No comparison to a stronger CLIP backbone or to concurrent detector-based approaches (SOA, DALL-Eval) on the same correlation study.

contamination

Not really applicable in the usual train/test sense — TIFA scores generation, not a trained model's answers — but the VQA and QA components (UnifiedQA, mPLUG, etc.) are themselves pretrained models whose own training data overlap with COCO and other sources is not audited here.

what's missed

Image quality/aesthetics (explicitly out of scope by the annotation guideline); any weighting of questions by importance — TIFA averages them equally (§3.2) while the paper's own rater guideline calls objects "the most important elements" and cascades a missing object to all its attributes and activities (Appendix C.1); and the human-study's per-generator correlation breakdown. TIFA's own VQA-vs-generation error attribution (Figure 11) is shown but never turned into a per-backbone correction to the published leaderboard numbers.

would raise confidence

A larger and more recent human correlation study (more than 160 prompts, current-generation T2I models); a published confidence interval or per-generator breakdown on the ρ = 0.597 headline; and a version of Table 4 that reports VQA-error-adjusted TIFA scores alongside the raw ones.

// bottom line

TIFA's core idea — decompose a prompt into checkable, per-element questions rather than compressing it into one embedding — is the part that has aged well: at 448 citations and counting (including from later benchmarks well outside text-to-image, like the agent benchmark GAIA and the video-generation benchmark EvalCrafter, both of which cite it), the "ask, don't embed" framing clearly outlasted the specific numbers. What a 2026 reader shouldn't take at face value is the leaderboard itself: the seven benchmarked generators (AttnGAN through Stable Diffusion v2.1) and the code-davinci-002-generated question set are both frozen artifacts of a March 2023 snapshot, current text-to-image models are far past the ceiling this study measured, and the VQA backbones scored here (76.1–84.5% human agreement) are themselves several generations behind what's available now. Treat TIFA v1.0's leaderboard as a historical curve and its methodology, rather than its specific correlation number, as the paper's lasting contribution.

// takeaways

What to remember

  1. TIFA scores text-to-image faithfulness by having a VQA model answer GPT-3-generated, UnifiedQA-filtered questions about a generated image — accuracy on those questions is the score, no CLIP embedding or captioning model involved.
  2. Across five VQA backbones, TIFA's correlation with human faithfulness judgment (Spearman's ρ 0.493–0.597) beats every prior metric tested (BLEU-4, ROUGE-L, METEOR, SPICE, CLIPScore: 0.183–0.340) on every backbone — but the study behind that claim covers only 160 of TIFA v1.0's 4,081 prompts and five 2022-era generators.
  3. TIFA v1.0 — 4,081 prompts, 25,829 questions, 12 categories — remains a fixed, deterministic benchmark artifact; the recommended mPLUG VQA backbone is itself only 84.5% accurate against human answers, and the paper's own Figure 11 (under 25% VQA-attributable for mPLUG, higher for weaker backbones) is never turned into an exact per-backbone correction to the published TIFA scores.
