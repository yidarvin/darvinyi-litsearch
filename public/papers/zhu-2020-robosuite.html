robosuite: A Modular Simulation Framework and Benchmark for Robot Learning

A MuJoCo-based robot-manipulation framework — composable robots, grippers, controllers, and arenas — that ships nine standardized benchmark tasks and a Soft Actor-Critic baseline. It has been the substrate under a large slice of manipulation-learning research since 2020, but the page you're reading it from today is v3 — the paper's third arXiv revision, posted in 2025, after most of its ~700 citations had already accrued.

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

By 2020, data-driven robot learning — reinforcement learning and imitation learning alike — was producing exciting single-lab results that were hard to compare or reproduce. The paper's own framing is blunt about why: "the challenges of reproducibility and the limited accessibility of robot hardware have impaired research progress." Real robots are expensive, fragile, and unevenly distributed across labs, so two groups working on ostensibly the same manipulation problem were often running on different arms, different objects, and different success criteria, with no shared environment to hold everything else fixed.

Simulated platforms already existed for other domains — OpenAI Gym for classic control, CARLA for driving, AI2-THOR for indoor visual navigation, dm_control for continuous control more broadly — but robot manipulation specifically lacked an equivalent that combined (1) enough modularity to let a lab build a genuinely new task rather than being stuck with a fixed environment list, (2) a library of realistic, off-the-shelf robot controllers so a researcher isn't reimplementing operational-space control from scratch, and (3) a standardized set of benchmark tasks that multiple papers could actually report the same numbers against. robosuite grew directly out of the authors' own SURREAL project (an earlier distributed-RL robot-manipulation framework) and was folded into the broader Advancing Robot Intelligence through Simulated Environments (ARISE) Initiative, with the explicit goal of lowering the barrier to entry for both RL and imitation-learning work on manipulation.

// how it works

The framework, not (yet) the benchmark

robosuite is best understood as two layered things: a simulation framework (the modular API for composing robots, grippers, objects, and controllers into new tasks on top of the MuJoCo physics engine) and, sitting on top of it, a much smaller benchmark suite (nine fixed tasks the paper actually reports numbers on). Almost every large number in this paper — ten, nine, six, four — describes the framework's capability catalog, not the benchmark. Conflating "robosuite supports ten robots" with "robosuite benchmarks ten robots" is the single easiest way to misread this paper.

The framework's core abstraction (Figure 2) is an Environment that wraps a MuJoCo Simulation Model. A Task object composes a RobotModel, zero or more Object Models, and an Arena (the workspace fixtures) into a single MJCF scene that MuJoCo instantiates. At runtime, a Policy or a human I/O device (keyboard, 3D mouse, or MuJoCo's own GUI) sends action commands into the Environment; the robot's Controller converts those into joint torques for MuJoCo to execute; and Sensors (cameras, force-torque, proprioception) push observations, rewards, and metadata back out. This API surface is deliberately OpenAI-Gym-style, so existing RL code can plug in with minimal glue.

As of the v1.5 release this paper describes, the built-in catalog is: 10 robot models (Panda, Sawyer, IIWA, Jaco, Kinova3, UR5e, the bimanual Baxter, the 44-DoF humanoid GR1, the quadruped Spot, and the mobile-manipulator TIAGo), 9 gripper models (including an Inspire dexterous hand), 4 base models (including an Omron wheeled base), and 6 body-part controller types — IK POSE, OSC POSE, OSC POSITION, JOINT POSITION, JOINT VELOCITY, and JOINT TORQUE (Table 1 in the paper), several of which have fixed/variable-stiffness/variable-impedance action-space variants. A separately-installed extension package, robosuite-models, adds a further 8 robots, 8 grippers, and 3 bases on top of those built-in counts. Starting from v1.5, individual body-part controllers are orchestrated by a composite controller — either BASIC (each body part driven independently) or WHOLE BODY IK (a single inverse-kinematics solve over end-effector targets, then handed down to per-part JOINT POSITION controllers) — and the paper notes a third-party WHOLE BODY MINK IK implementation is also included as an example, not a first-party controller.

On top of that framework sits the actual benchmark: nine standardized single- and two-arm manipulation tasks (Section 3.1) — Block Lifting, Block Stacking, Pick-and-Place, Nut Assembly, Door Opening, Table Wiping, Two Arm Lifting, Two Arm Peg-in-Hole, and Two Arm Handover — each randomized at the start of every episode. Pick-and-Place and Nut Assembly each also have easier single-object variants, which the paper mentions but doesn't count as additional tasks. Figure 1 shows procedurally-varied instances of these environments across several of the supported robots.

// what they found

One algorithm, two robots, nine tasks

The paper's only reported experiment (Section 3.2) trains Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) — and only SAC; no other RL or imitation-learning algorithm is benchmarked in this paper — on all nine standardized tasks, using two robots (Panda, Sawyer) and, for an ablation on the two easiest tasks (Block Lifting and Door Opening), two controllers (OSC POSE vs. JOINT VELOCITY); the other seven tasks are reported with the OSC POSE controller only. Agents receive low-dimensional proprioceptive and object-specific state (not images), train for 500 epochs with a 500-step episode horizon, and results are averaged over 5 random seeds, with per-step reward normalized so the maximum possible per-episode return is 500. Training ran on 2 CPUs and 12 GB of VRAM with no GPU, "each taking about two days to complete."

By the paper's own qualitative read of these curves: "the SAC algorithm is able to solve three of the nine environments, including Block Lifting, Door Opening, and Two Arm Peg-in-Hole, while making slow progress in the other environments." The paper defines no numeric success threshold for "solve," and it never says how it determined that a task was solved — this is a qualitative read of which curves visibly separate from the rest, not a printed pass/fail table. Reading the curves by eye — these are visual estimates off an unlabelled chart, not printed values — Panda-OSC ends with the higher terminal return on five of the nine tasks — Block Lifting (~400 vs ~320), Block Stacking (~55 vs ~45), Door Opening (~430 vs ~325), Table Wiping (~55 vs ~45), and Two Arm Handover (~80 vs ~60) — while Sawyer-OSC ends higher on three: Pick-and-Place Can (~40 vs ~20), Nut Assembly Round (~90 vs ~80), and Two Arm Lifting, where Sawyer-OSC crosses above Panda-OSC by around epoch 200 and stays ahead through the end (~125 vs ~110). On Two Arm Peg-in-Hole, Sawyer-OSC also tracks visibly ahead of Panda-OSC for most of training before the two curves converge to a near-tie by epoch 500. Block Lifting and Door Opening show the clearest separation between OSC POSE and JOINT VELOCITY (both robots learn faster with OSC POSE, which the authors hypothesize "is credited to the accelerated exploration in task space"). The remaining six tasks — Block Stacking, Pick-and-Place, Nut Assembly Round, Table Wiping, Two Arm Lifting, Two Arm Handover — plateau well below the "solved" tasks for both robots.

From this, the paper makes one concrete recommendation for anyone using robosuite going forward: "we recommend using the nine environments with the Panda robot and the operational space controller... for standardized and fair comparisons." It also points to a separate benchmark repository where the SAC results and exact hyperparameters are released "for ease of replicability."

// does it hold up?

The eval read

robosuite is genuinely two different kinds of artifact wearing one title, and the evaluation section here reflects that tension. As a framework — the modular robot/gripper/base/controller/arena composition system — it needs no benchmark-style validation; its claim to fame is flexibility, and the sheer number of downstream papers building custom tasks on it — robomimic, LIBERO, BEHAVIOR, Voyager, and many others (LIBERO, BEHAVIOR, and Voyager are cited in this graph) — is the real evidence for that. As a benchmark — the thing the title also promises — its own empirical section is thin by design: one algorithm (SAC), two of the ten supported robots, five seeds, an OSC-POSE-vs-JOINT-VELOCITY controller ablation run on only two of the nine tasks (the other seven are reported with OSC POSE alone), and no comparison against any other published algorithm on the same tasks. That's a demonstration that the framework can run RL, not a benchmark study in the sense that, say, Meta-World or LIBERO run one.

baselines

SAC is the only algorithm reported. The paper explicitly frames its own robot demonstration utilities (keyboard/3D-mouse teleoperation, demonstration replay) as a first-class feature (Section 1), yet reports no imitation-learning baseline at all — an odd absence given how much of the framework is built around collecting and using human demonstrations.

contamination

Not applicable in the usual sense — this is a simulated-environment/framework paper, not a text-corpus benchmark, so there's no train/test leakage to check. The closer analogue is configuration leakage: because every setting (robot, gripper, controller, observation space) is swappable, a paper's reported "robosuite results" are only comparable to another paper's if both pin the same configuration — something the paper recommends (Panda + OSC POSE) but cannot enforce.

what's missed

The reported SAC numbers are visual curves with no printed table, and "solve" has no numeric criterion — a reader can't recompute the exact final return the paper is characterizing as "solved" versus "slow progress." There's also no real-robot experiment anywhere in the paper: the "sim-to-real transferability" claim in Section 2.3 is a design rationale (torque-based controllers matching real hardware) rather than a validated transfer result, and its only citation for actual sim-to-real transfer work is the authors' own prior paper [11], not a result reported here.

would raise confidence

A printed results table (not just training-curve figures) with final mean ± std per task/robot/controller; at least one non-SAC baseline (a PPO or imitation-learning run would test the framework's own demonstration-collection story); and, given the version gap below, an explicit statement of which robosuite release the reported numbers were generated on and whether they've been reproduced on the current one.

// the version question — and it's genuinely unusual here

The PDF at the arXiv link above is v3, submitted 2025-01-18 — more than four years after v1 (2020-09-25), and more than two years after an intermediate v2, posted 2022-11-15. This isn't a typo-fix revision history: v3's robot roster includes GR1 (a Fourier Intelligence humanoid), Spot (Boston Dynamics), and TIAGo (PAL Robotics) — none of which existed as robosuite integrations back in 2020 — and it cites a third-party controller (Zakka's "Mink," July 2024) that postdates the original submission by four years. The Abstract itself is written for the newest release: "This paper discusses the key system modules and the benchmark environments of our new release robosuite v1.5." With three versions on record, a reader who cites "robosuite (2020)" may be pointing at any of three substantially different texts — and the citation record bears that out. Bucketing Semantic Scholar's citing-paper years (year-level precision only, so the 2022 split below is estimated proportionally) against the three posting dates: roughly 18% of the 707 citations fall in the v1-only window (Sep 2020 – Nov 2022); roughly 37% fall in the v2 window (Nov 2022 – Jan 2025); and roughly 41% are dated 2025 or 2026, i.e. after v3 replaced it. No single version accounts for a majority of the citations, and v1 — the version most readers probably picture when they see "robosuite, 2020" — holds the smallest share of the three.

Section 1 itself scopes the benchmark section differently from how the Abstract scopes the whole paper: "We will also describe the benchmark tasks in the v1.0 robosuite release and benchmarking results of the most relevant state-of-the-art data-driven algorithms on these tasks." Read against the Abstract's "benchmark environments of our new release robosuite v1.5" quoted above, the two sentences directly disagree about which version the benchmark section is describing. Taken plainly, the Section 1 sentence says the SAC numbers in Figure 4 — the paper's only reported results — describe v1.0 behavior, not the v1.5 framework the rest of the manuscript was rewritten to describe. Nowhere in the current text is this reconciled: there's no changelog, no version table, and no statement of whether the v1.0 benchmark numbers still hold on v1.5. So a reader in 2026 gets a framework description current as of a 2025 revision, sitting next to benchmark results the paper itself says are from 2020-era software — and the paper does not say whether those two things still agree.

// takeaways

What to remember

  1. robosuite is a modular MuJoCo simulation framework first — 10 robots, 9 grippers, 4 bases, 6 controller modes, plus a separately-installed extension package adding 8/8/3 more — with a much smaller fixed benchmark of 9 standardized manipulation tasks layered on top. Don't read a framework capability count as a benchmark result, or vice versa.
  2. The paper's only empirical result is SAC on Panda and Sawyer, with an OSC-POSE-vs-JOINT-VELOCITY ablation on just two of the nine tasks; it "solves" three tasks (Block Lifting, Door Opening, Two Arm Peg-in-Hole) by a qualitative read of training curves, with no printed numeric table and no other algorithm compared.
  3. The arXiv page now serves v3 (2025-01-18), the third of three versions (v1 2020-09-25, v2 2022-11-15, v3 2025-01-18) — no single version accounts for a majority of the paper's 707 citations — and the paper's own Section 1 scopes its one results section to "the v1.0 robosuite release," directly contradicting the Abstract's framing of the same benchmark as "our new release robosuite v1.5."
