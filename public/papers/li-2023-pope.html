Evaluating Object Hallucination in Large Vision-Language Models

POPE turns object-hallucination scoring from CHAIR's prompt-sensitive caption parsing into a stable yes/no polling protocol — and shows three of the five evaluated LVLMs answer "Yes" to 95.37–100% of POPE's polling questions, regardless of the true answer.

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

Large vision-language models (LVLMs) — a vision encoder and an LLM stitched together and then visual-instruction-tuned — inherited a known VLPM failure alongside their new instruction-following abilities: object hallucination, generating objects in a description that simply aren't in the image. The paper's first move is to show this got worse, not better, with scale and instruction tuning. Using the CHAIR metric (Rohrbach et al., 2018) on MSCOCO captions from five contemporary LVLMs — mPLUG-Owl, LLaVA, MultiModal-GPT, MiniGPT-4, InstructBLIP — against four smaller VLPM baselines (OSCAR, VinVL, OFA, BLIP, numbers taken from Dai et al. 2023b rather than re-run), LLaVA's CHAIRS under one instruction reaches 32.7%, more than double OSCARBase's 13.0%.

But the paper's sharper finding is that CHAIR itself is a shaky ruler for LVLMs. Prompted with two semantically similar instructions — "Generate a short caption" (I1) vs. "Provide a brief description" (I2) — the same models' CHAIR scores swing hard: mPLUG-Owl's CHAIRI goes from 14.8 (I1) to 30.2 (I2), and the ranking between models can flip outright (under I1, LLaVA's CHAIRI of 10.5 beats MultiModal-GPT's 11.1; under I2 that reverses, 18.8 vs. 18.2). CHAIR also requires hand-crafted parsing rules to extract objects from free-form captions, rules not built for LVLMs' generation style. A metric this sensitive to prompt phrasing and parsing brittleness can't reliably separate "hallucinates more" from "was asked differently" — which is the gap POPE is built to close.

// how it works

The method

// version note

arXiv hosts three versions of this paper: v1/v2 (May 2023, the initial preprint) and v3 (26 Oct 2023, "Accepted to EMNLP 2023"), published on the ACL Anthology as 2023.emnlp-main.20. v1 and v3 are both 14 pages but share only 283 numeric tokens — 108 appear only in v1 and 193 appear only in v3 (283 of 584 distinct values total, 48%) — the evaluation tables were substantially re-run/expanded between the preprint and the camera-ready. Everything on this page is transcribed from v3, the version the authors' own "Accepted to EMNLP 2023" comment endorses as current.

Before designing a new evaluation, the authors first pin down why LVLMs hallucinate the objects they hallucinate. They plot hallucination counts against object frequency and against co-occurrence with a fixed object ("dining table") across MSCOCO, and define a top-k hit ratio HR@k to quantify it:

HRA@k = 1n Σᵢ Hit@k(i)Hallucinated(i) // fraction of each image's hallucinated objects that fall in the top-k most frequent (or co-occurring) MSCOCO objects

HRA@10 and HRC(dining table)@10 both land in the 0.42–0.66 range across the four LVLMs (mPLUG-Owl 0.5455 / 0.6608, LLaVA 0.4620 / 0.5628, MultiModal-GPT 0.4152 / 0.5742, MiniGPT-4 0.4610 / 0.5600), rising further at k=30. In plain terms: roughly half of what these models hallucinate is drawn from the handful of objects that dominate their training-instruction distribution.

That finding directly shapes POPE (Polling-based Object Probing Evaluation). Instead of parsing a free-form caption, POPE builds a set of triples ⟨image, questions, answers⟩ where each question is a template — "Is there a <object> in the image?" — and the model must answer "Yes" or "No". Ground-truth objects (answer "Yes") come from human annotations or automatic segmentation (SEEM); non-existent objects (answer "No") are drawn under three sampling strategies that are the paper's own experimental variable:

  • Random — sample negatives uniformly from all objects absent from the image.
  • Popular — sample from the top-k most frequent objects in the whole dataset that are absent from this image (k = l/2, l = questions per image).
  • Adversarial — rank objects by co-occurrence frequency with the image's ground-truth objects, and sample the top-k absent ones.

Positive:negative is fixed at 1:1 for label balance. On MSCOCO, the authors sample 500 validation images with >3 annotated objects and build l=6 questions each (3 yes, 3 no), for 3,000 polling questions per model per setting. Because the answer space is closed to "Yes"/"No", scoring needs no caption parser — Accuracy, Precision, Recall and F1 (the paper's chosen "major metric") drop out directly from the answers, plus a "Yes (%)" column that separately tracks how often a model just says yes.

// what they found

The results

On MSCOCO under the Random setting, InstructBLIP posts the highest Accuracy (88.73), Precision (85.08) and F1 (89.29) of the five models, with MiniGPT-4 second (77.83 / 75.38 / 78.86). Its Recall is 93.93 — but Recall is not where InstructBLIP leads: mPLUG-Owl, LLaVA and MultiModal-GPT all post higher raw Recall (99.53 / 99.80 / 100.00), and the "Yes (%)" column explains why — those three answer "Yes" to 95.37–99.97% of all questions regardless of the true answer, so their Recall is inflated by the same bias that tanks their Precision and F1 (68.06 / 68.65 / 66.68, versus InstructBLIP's 89.29). MultiModal-GPT is the extreme case, answering "Yes" 99.97% of the time under Random and a flat 100.00% under both Popular and Adversarial — a model that has essentially stopped looking at the image and is just emitting the same constant answer regardless of the question (POPE's positives:negatives are fixed at 1:1, so there is no majority class to fall back on — this is a fixed policy, not a base-rate shortcut).

Performance degrades from Random to Popular to Adversarial for most models — InstructBLIP's F1 falls 89.29 → 83.45 → 78.45, MiniGPT-4's 78.86 → 72.21 → 71.37 — consistent with the frequency/co-occurrence bias established above: harder negatives (popular or co-occurring objects) are exactly what these models are most prone to hallucinate. The two heavily "always-Yes" models don't follow this pattern as cleanly (MultiModal-GPT's F1 is flat at 66.67–66.68 across all three settings; mPLUG-Owl's actually ticks up 0.03 points from Popular to Adversarial), because a model that says "Yes" almost regardless of input has little room left to degrade.

POPE's stability claim is checked directly: evaluating LLaVA with four differently-worded POPE prompts vs. four differently-worded CHAIR instructions, POPE's F1 averages 67.43 with std 0.78 — a coefficient of variation of 1.16% — while CHAIRI averages 13.88 with std 3.22, a coefficient of variation of 23.2%, roughly 20x more relative spread. Extending POPE past human annotation, the authors rebuild it from SEEM segmentation masks on MSCOCO, A-OKVQA and GQA; the setting ordering (Random > Popular > Adversarial) holds throughout, and the model ranking (InstructBLIP > MiniGPT-4 > LLaVA) "mostly follow[s] the same trend," in the paper's own words — with one exception under Adversarial, where SEEM's finer-grained masks flip it: MiniGPT-4's F1 drops from 71.37 (human-annotated) to 62.70 (SEEM-based), which falls below LLaVA's 66.38 in that same block, even as MiniGPT-4 still leads LLaVA on Accuracy there (61.93 vs. 49.77). Finally, POPE correlates with downstream usefulness: on both A-OKVQA and GQA, InstructBLIP tops both POPE and VQA scores, and — in both datasets — MiniGPT-4 beats LLaVA on POPE F1 but loses to it on VQA score (A-OKVQA: MiniGPT-4 POPE 72.47 vs. LLaVA 66.64, but VQA 38.69 vs. 50.51), showing hallucination rate and task usefulness aren't the same axis.

// does it hold up?

The eval read

POPE is a genuine methodological upgrade over CHAIR — closed-form answers really do remove parsing brittleness, and the prompt-stability comparison (std 0.78 vs. 3.22) backs that up with a real number, not just a qualitative claim. But three parts of the design deserve a closer look.

The paper already names the yes-bias behind its own F1 numbers — but stops short of naming the one number that would make it unmissable. MultiModal-GPT under the Popular setting answers "Yes" to literally 100.00% of questions — a model producing zero information about image content — and its row reads Accuracy 50.00, Precision 50.00, Recall 100.00, F1 66.67. In the same paragraph that introduces this table, the authors write that "LLaVA, MultiModal-GPT and mPLUG-Owl are extremely prone to answer 'Yes' (near 99%)... these three LVLMs are over confident, leading to lower accuracy on questions with the answer 'No'" — so the paper does connect the near-100% Yes rate to unreliable scores in the very paragraph reporting them. What it doesn't spell out is the exact arithmetic: F1 = 2PR/(P+R) with P=50 (the chance-level precision 1:1 label balance guarantees any input-independent strategy) and R=100 is mathematically guaranteed to equal 66.67 — the ceiling any signal-free, constant-"Yes" policy can reach on this metric. F1 remains the paper's declared "major metric," and the text never names 66.67 as that specific artefact rather than a genuine, if middling, score.

Popular and Adversarial sampling are built from the exact bias Section 4 already measured, not an independent probe. The paper is explicit about this — "by devising different sampling strategies, we can validate whether LVLMs are prone to hallucinate specific objects, e.g., frequently appearing or co-occurring objects discussed in Section 4" — so the causal story (models degrade on harder negatives because they over-hallucinate frequent/co-occurring objects) and the evaluation design that reveals it (negatives chosen by frequency/co-occurrence) are drawn from the same underlying signal. That's a defensible and disclosed choice for demonstrating the hypothesis, but it means "Adversarial" measures susceptibility to this specific, already-identified bias rather than an unrelated held-out failure mode — the three settings are better read as a dial on one known confound than as three independent difficulty axes.

Two appendix results cut against claims the paper makes in its own body. The prompt-stability finding — POPE’s F1 moving 67.43 ± 0.78 across six templates against CHAIR’s 13.88 ± 3.22 — is measured on LLaVA only (Table 4), and LLaVA answers “Yes” to at least 95.37% of POPE’s questions in every setting, so every one of its six POPE scores sits within roughly a point of 66.67, the F1 a constant-“Yes” policy earns under 1:1 balance. Stability measured against a floor is not the same as stability. And Table 12’s claim that captioning quality tracks POPE holds on BLEU but inverts on the other two metrics: MiniGPT-4 scores lower on POPE than InstructBLIP (78.86 against 89.29) yet higher on both METEOR (25.6 against 22.6) and ROUGE-L (44.7 against 42.3). A third appendix number is worth knowing for scale: BLIP, a small supervised VQA model, posts the highest A-OKVQA random F1 in the paper at 90.87, above InstructBLIP’s 87.20.

The one true validation of what "Yes/No" answers actually mean is run on only 2 of the 5 models. Section 5.3's consistency check — comparing POPE's "No" verdicts against whether that object ever appears in the model's own captions — uses only InstructBLIP and MiniGPT-4, "given their relatively balanced yes/no distributions." For InstructBLIP, 0 of 1,303 objects given a "No" verdict were later mentioned in a caption; for MiniGPT-4, 5 of 1,445. That's a real check, but it's run precisely on the two models where it's least likely to be confounded by yes-bias — LLaVA, MultiModal-GPT and mPLUG-Owl, the models with 95.37–100% Yes rates, are the ones whose POPE scores this check does not validate.

baselines

CHAIR comparison is fair (same models, same captions); the four VLPM baselines in Table 1 (OSCAR/VinVL/OFA/BLIP) are borrowed from Dai et al. (2023b) rather than re-run under this paper's own instructions, so the VLPM-vs-LVLM gap in Table 1 isn't a fully controlled comparison.

contamination

Flagged by the authors themselves, not by us: InstructBLIP's A-OKVQA POPE/VQA numbers in Table 6 come with the caveat "since InstructBLIP has been trained on A-OKVQA, the result should be considered with caution."

what's missed

The paper explicitly scopes itself to coarse object existence — "we focus on coarse-grained object hallucinations... and leave fine-grained object hallucinations such as the number, attributes, and positions of the object for future work" — so hallucinated counts, attributes and relations are out of scope by the authors' own design, not an unstated gap.

would raise confidence

Running the Section 5.3 consistency check on all five models rather than just the two least yes-biased ones, and naming 66.67 explicitly as the F1 ceiling any constant-"Yes" policy reaches under 1:1 label balance — rather than leaving that specific arithmetic for a reader to derive from the Accuracy and Yes(%) columns.

// bottom line

POPE's core contribution — closed-form yes/no polling in place of caption parsing — is real and empirically demonstrated to be more stable than CHAIR. The paper is also unusually candid about its own edges: it names the InstructBLIP/A-OKVQA leakage itself, scopes its own object-existence-only claim, flags its own "near 99%" yes-bias and the resulting over-confidence in the same paragraph that reports F1, and even notes in its Limitations section that POPE's own "matching-based method" for reading Yes/No off free-form model output "may lead to inaccurate evaluation results" — the same class of brittleness it set out to remove from CHAIR, just smaller in degree. The one gap that candor doesn't close: F1, the paper's declared "major metric," can sit at exactly 66.67 for a model that has produced no signal at all — a constant "Yes" under 1:1 label balance lands exactly there — and the text never names that value as the specific ceiling it is, leaving a reader to derive the arithmetic themselves from the Accuracy and Yes(%) columns.

// takeaways

What to remember

  1. POPE replaces CHAIR's prompt-sensitive caption parsing with closed-form yes/no polling over sampled ground-truth and non-existent objects: across four differently-worded rephrasings, POPE's F1 has a coefficient of variation of 1.16% (std 0.78 on a 67.43 mean) versus CHAIRI's 23.2% (std 3.22 on a 13.88 mean) — about 20x less relative prompt-induced spread.
  2. Object hallucination in 2023-era LVLMs is heavily frequency-driven — roughly half of what models hallucinate falls in the top-10 most frequent or co-occurring MSCOCO objects (HRA@10/HRC@10 ≈ 0.42–0.66) — and three of five evaluated LVLMs (mPLUG-Owl, LLaVA, MultiModal-GPT) answer "Yes" to 95.37–100% of POPE's polling questions, landing their accuracy within a few points of the 50% a coin flip would average under 1:1 label balance (as low as 49.77 for LLaVA in the SEEM-based Adversarial setting — below, not above, chance).
  3. POPE is a released, scoreable, extensible probe (human-annotation or SEEM-segmentation-based; its code and data are published at github.com/RUCAIBox/POPE, a repository the paper itself doesn't name) — but its results prose foregrounds F1 over the Accuracy column that already flags a degenerate always-"Yes" row, and its one internal consistency check validates only the two models least affected by yes-bias.
