DailyDialog: A Manually Labelled Multi-turn Dialogue Dataset

13,118 human-written, multi-turn daily-life dialogues (avg. 7.9 turns) scraped from English-learner practice sites, manually double-labeled with dialog-act (Inform / Questions / Directives / Commissive) and Ekman Big-Six-plus-Other emotion tags — a compact, single-session alternative to noisy social-media dialogue corpora.

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

By 2017 the multi-turn dialogue field had two families of training data, and both were the wrong shape for the task. Domain-specific spoken corpora — Trains (Ringger et al., 1996), DBox (Petukhova et al., 2014) — were carefully annotated but small and locked to one task, so models trained on them don't generalize to open-domain chit-chat. The alternative, open-domain corpora, traded annotation quality for scale in ways that hurt just as much: Switchboard and OpenSubtitles pack 150+ and 1,000+ speaker turns into a single nominal "conversation," so a random window of that transcript rarely stays on one topic; Twitter Dialog Corpus and the Chinese Weibo dataset are built from post-reply pairs on social networks, which are noisy, informal, and structurally different from a real back-and-forth exchange (often involving more than two parties, with no guarantee the "reply" is even coherent with the "post").

The authors' diagnosis is that daily human conversation serves two purposes — exchanging information and building social rapport — and neither existing dataset lets a model learn either one cleanly. Information exchange shows up as a dialog-act flow (a question gets an answer, a suggestion gets accepted or declined), which is exactly the kind of structure that gets lost in 150-turn Switchboard transcripts or single-shot Twitter replies. Social rapport shows up as emotion, which nobody had manually labeled at scale for open-domain chit-chat. DailyDialog's answer is a dataset that is human-written (not scraped social-media noise), topic-coherent (each conversation stays in one physical/social context, e.g. a shop, a classroom), reasonably short (avg. ~8 turns rather than hundreds), and manually annotated on both the intention and emotion axes so the two phenomena the paper cares about are directly measurable rather than assumed.

// how it works

The method

The raw text is crawled from websites that help English learners practice daily dialog (hence the name) — not from a social network or a fictional script. After crawling, the authors de-duplicate the raw data, filter out any dialogue with more than two parties, and run automatic misspelling correction with the autocorrect Python package. The result is 13,118 multi-turn dialogues, each ending after a "reasonable" number of speaker turns rather than running on indefinitely.

A: I'm worried about something.

B: What's that?

A: Well, I have to drive to school for a meeting this morning, and I'm going to end up getting stuck in rush-hour traffic.

B: That's annoying, but nothing to worry about. Just breathe deeply when you feel yourself getting upset.

A: Ok, I'll try that.

B: Is there anything else bothering you?

A: Just one more thing. A school called me this morning to see if I could teach a few classes this weekend and I don't know what to do.

B: Do you have any other plans this weekend?

A: I'm supposed to work on a paper that'd due on Monday.

B: Try not to take on more than you can handle.

A: You're right. I probably should just work on my paper. Thanks!

Two label sets are then applied by hand. Dialog act (the paper's "intention" axis) follows the criteria of Amanova et al. (2016), chosen because it aligns with the ISO 24617-2 standard and with existing annotated corpora like Trains and DBox: every utterance gets one of four labels — Inform (statements and questions that provide information), Questions (seeking information), Directives (request, instruct, suggest, accept/reject an offer), or Commissive (accept/reject a request, suggestion, or offer). Emotion follows Ekman's (1992) "Big Six" theory — Anger, Disgust, Fear, Happiness, Sadness, Surprise — plus an added Other category for everything that doesn't fit, giving seven emotion classes total. Three annotators with dialogue/communication-theory training first label 100 dialogues together to calibrate and resolve disagreements, then independently annotate the full corpus; the reported inter-annotator agreement is 78.9%, with residual disagreements resolved by majority rule or re-annotation.

// what they found

The results

The corpus-characteristics analysis (Section 3) is arguably the paper's main deliverable. Conversations cluster into ten topic categories, dominated by Relationship (33.33%), Ordinary Life (28.26%), and Work (14.49%). At the dialog-act level, Inform utterances are the plurality at 46,532 (45.2%), followed by Questions at 29,428 (28.6%), Directives at 17,295 (16.8%), and Commissive at 9,724 (9.4%). The authors surface two recurring multi-turn flow patterns beyond the basic Questions→Inform and Directives→Commissive bi-turn exchanges: a speaker flipping from information-provider to information-seeker within a single turn, found in 2,398 dialogues (18.3%); and a Directives→Directives→Commissives-like negotiation pattern (one person proposes, the other counter-proposes), found in 1,203 dialogues (9.2%).

Emotion labels are heavily skewed toward the "no clear emotion" bucket: of all utterances, 83.10% fall in Other. Among the 16.90% of utterances that do carry one of the six primary Ekman emotions, Happiness is overwhelming at 12,885 utterances (74.02% of emotion-bearing utterances, 12.51% of the total), with Surprise (1,823, 10.47%/1.77%), Sadness (1,150, 6.61%/1.12%), Anger (1,022, 5.87%/0.99%), Disgust (353, 2.03%/0.34%), and Fear (74, 1.00%/0.17%) trailing far behind. The authors also report that 3,675 dialogues (28.0%) end on a "happy" note, and 113 dialogues (0.8%) shift from a negative starting emotion to a positive ending one.

Section 4 then benchmarks five families of existing dialogue approaches on a random 11,118/1,000/1,000 train/validation/test conversation split (vocabulary 25,000, 300-d Word2Vec-initialized embeddings, 1-layer 512-unit GRU encoder/decoder, batch size 128, Adam at learning rate 0.0002). Retrieval-based methods are scored by BLEU against the (unseen-in-training) gold response:

Reranking by intention/emotion label barely changes BLEU (and if anything drops it slightly relative to plain feature-based retrieval), but does raise "equivalence" — the percentage of retrieved responses whose label matches the gold response's label — from 46.3%/73.7% (intention/emotion) for the plain feature-based method to 47.3%/72.3% for +Intention and 46.7%/74.3% for +Intention&Emotion. On the generation side, comparing vanilla, attention, and hierarchical (HRED) Seq2Seq, with and without label conditioning ("L+") and with and without OpenSubtitles pretraining ("Pre+"): the best BLEU-4 in the whole table is 0.009, reached three separate ways — plain HRED (no labels), L+Attn-Seq2Seq, and L+HRED alike — versus 0.006 for vanilla Seq2Seq and for Attn-Seq2Seq without labels. Label conditioning lifts BLEU-1/BLEU-2 for all three architectures (Attn-Seq2Seq's BLEU-1 goes from 0.335 to 0.464 with labels), but the gain doesn't carry through to BLEU-3/BLEU-4: for HRED, adding labels actually drops BLEU-3 (0.019 → 0.016) while BLEU-4 stays flat at 0.009, and Seq2Seq's BLEU-4 is likewise unchanged at 0.006 with or without labels — only Attn-Seq2Seq's BLEU-4 rises with labels (0.006 → 0.009). Meanwhile OpenSubtitles pretraining lowers perplexity (Pre+Seq2Seq: PPL 35.01 vs. 55.94 unpretrained) but hurts BLEU (Pre+HRED collapses to BLEU-1 0.153, BLEU-4 0.000) — the authors attribute this to a domain mismatch between OpenSubtitles' movie-line register and DailyDialog's daily-life dialogues.

// does it hold up?

The eval read

As a benchmark that nine years of subsequent dialogue and emotion-recognition work has built on, DailyDialog's biggest liability is baked into its own Table 3: 83.10% of utterances carry no primary Ekman emotion ("Other"). That imbalance is the dataset's own reported statistic, not an artifact of downstream misuse, but it means any classifier can score deceptively high overall accuracy by defaulting to "no emotion" — which is exactly why the emotion-recognition-in-conversation literature that grew up around this dataset largely had to adopt a convention (excluding "Other"/"neutral" from the reported F1, or reporting macro- rather than micro-averaged F1) that this 1992-vintage-theory-based, seven-class labeling scheme doesn't itself enforce. The paper reports one aggregate inter-annotator-agreement number, 78.9%, with no breakdown by label axis (intention vs. emotion) or by class, and no chance-corrected statistic like Cohen's or Fleiss' kappa — given that 83% majority-class rate on the emotion axis, a large share of that raw 78.9% agreement is plausibly just annotators agreeing on "Other" by default rather than substantively agreeing on hard emotional judgment calls.

The train/validation/test split (11,118/1,000/1,000 conversations) is drawn randomly at the conversation level from data the authors describe as crawled from "various websites which serve for English learner to practice English dialog." That source is a limited pool of templated, pedagogically-written scenarios (ordering at a restaurant, checking into a hotel, asking directions), and the paper doesn't report any check for near-duplicate or template-overlapping conversations across the split — a real risk when the underlying "daily conversation" content is itself curated to teach a fixed, recurring set of everyday-English scenarios rather than sampled from unconstrained human speech. Because the dialogues are written by ESL-site content creators rather than genuine free-form crowdsourced chit-chat (contrast with PersonaChat's Turker-to-Turker conversations), the "reflects our daily communication way" framing is somewhat optimistic: it reflects a stylized, textbook version of daily communication, which is cleaner and more formal than real speech but also less spontaneous.

On the evaluation-methodology side, the paper's own generation and retrieval experiments lean entirely on automatic metrics — BLEU, perplexity, and the intention/emotion "equivalence" percentage — with no human evaluation of any generated or retrieved response, despite the paper explicitly citing Liu et al. (2016)'s "How NOT to evaluate your dialogue system" on BLEU's known weakness for open-domain generation, and despite noting themselves that the equivalence metric is "not a very strong evaluation metric." That's an unusually self-aware admission to leave unaddressed: the paper flags that its own headline generation numbers (BLEU-4 in the 0.000–0.009 range) may not track anything a human reader would call response quality, but doesn't run the small human study that would tell us whether, say, L+Attn-Seq2Seq's small BLEU-4 edge over vanilla Seq2Seq corresponds to any real coherence gain.

baselines

Reasonable for late 2016/2017 — vanilla and attention Seq2Seq, HRED, and feature/embedding retrieval were the standard toolkit; no baseline is a strawman, but none are validated by a human judge.

contamination

Random conversation-level split with no cross-split near-duplicate check on a corpus drawn from a narrow, templated ESL-practice source; by the 2020s the corpus itself is a common pretraining/fine-tuning target, so zero-shot LLM numbers on it should be read skeptically.

what's missed

The 83.10% "Other" emotion majority class isn't corrected for anywhere in the paper's own reported numbers, and the single 78.9% agreement figure hides how much of that is majority-class agreement rather than real inter-rater consistency on hard cases.

would raise confidence

A chance-corrected agreement statistic per label axis, a near-duplicate audit across the train/val/test split, and even a small human pairwise-preference study to check whether the BLEU differences between generation models correspond to any perceptible quality gap.

// bottom line

DailyDialog's lasting contribution isn't the 2017 Seq2Seq/HRED benchmarking numbers — those are dated and, by the authors' own admission, weakly validated — it's the corpus itself: a clean, single-session, dual-labeled (dialog-act + emotion) alternative to noisy social-media dialogue data that later multi-session and emotion-recognition work (including MSC's own Table 2 comparison) still treats as the standard short-conversation reference point, imbalance and all.

// takeaways

What to remember

  1. DailyDialog is 13,118 human-written, single-session, multi-turn daily-life dialogues (avg. 7.9 turns, 14.6 tokens/utterance) crawled from English-learner practice sites — a compact, topic-coherent alternative to sprawling Switchboard/OpenSubtitles transcripts or noisy Twitter/Weibo post-reply pairs.
  2. Every utterance is manually double-labeled: a four-class dialog act (Inform 45.2%, Questions 28.6%, Directives 16.8%, Commissive 9.4%) and a seven-class Ekman-Big-Six-plus-Other emotion tag, at 78.9% reported inter-annotator agreement.
  3. Emotion labels are dominated by "Other" (83.10% of all utterances) and, among emotion-bearing utterances, by Happiness (74.02%) — an imbalance later emotion-recognition-in-conversation work routinely corrects for by excluding the majority class from reported F1.
  4. 2017-era retrieval and generation baselines top out at BLEU-4 0.194 (feature-based retrieval) and 0.009 (a three-way tie among plain HRED, L+Attn-Seq2Seq, and L+HRED) with no accompanying human evaluation — the paper itself flags BLEU and its own "equivalence" metric as weak signals, a caveat worth carrying into any later paper that cites DailyDialog numbers uncritically.
