Baby Intuitions Benchmark (BIB): Discerning the goals, preferences, and actions of others

A five-family, violation-of-expectation video benchmark built directly out of infant-cognition experiments, designed so a machine's "surprise" score is in principle checkable against how long babies actually look. Three passively-trained neural baselines clear the obstacle and efficiency tasks but sit at or below chance on preference and multi-agent binding, and collapse on the hardest instrumental-action sub-task.

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

By their first birthday, human infants already reason about other agents: they expect agents to pursue object-based goals rather than locations, to have preferences specific to them as individuals, to be blocked by obstacles, to take instrumental detours toward higher-order goals, and to move efficiently. Decades of developmental-psychology work established this using the violation-of-expectation (VoE) paradigm — show an infant a repeated pattern, then measure whether they look longer at an outcome that breaks it. Computational work on the analogous problem for machines — inverse reinforcement learning and Bayesian models that treat other agents as noisy rational planners (Baker et al., 2009, 2011, 2017; Ullman et al., 2009), and neural approaches such as Rabinowitz et al.'s (2018) ToMnet that learn a representation of an agent's mental state from observed behavior — had, in the authors' telling, never been evaluated on a benchmark built to test the generalizability that matters. Baker et al.'s evaluations offered fewer than 100 sample episodes, too few to train a data-hungry learned model. Rabinowitz et al.'s ToMnet drew training and test episodes from the same distribution, so a high score couldn't distinguish abstract reasoning from narrow pattern-matching. And none of these evaluations were built from paradigms that had actually been run with infants, so a model's score could never be read against a human one.

BIB's answer is to import the VoE paradigm itself, rather than just its spirit: procedurally generate a large volume of both background-training episodes and held-out evaluation episodes, adopt a single canonical train/evaluate split designed to force flexible recombination of separately-learned pieces of knowledge (rather than many minor leave-out splits), and adapt stimuli directly from published infant experiments so BIB's outcomes are, in principle, comparable to infant looking-time data. The paper is explicit that BIB is not the only benchmark pursuing this: AGENT (Shu et al., 2021) was developed contemporaneously, is also infant-inspired and VoE-based, and has already been validated against adult behavioral ratings. The two are framed as complementary — AGENT overlaps with BIB on object-based goals and efficiency and evaluates across many leave-out splits per scenario; BIB adds four competencies AGENT does not test (multi-agent preference binding, inaccessible goals, instrumental action, and telling a rational agent from an irrational one) under one harder canonical split.

// how it works

The method

Every BIB evaluation episode is an "episode" in the VoE sense: an eight-trial familiarization phase that establishes a pattern, followed by a test phase presenting a paired expected and unexpected outcome video. Stimuli are procedurally generated in a 10×10 grid world populated by simple geometric shapes standing in for agents, objects, tools, keys, locks, and barriers, rendered both in 2D and — via AI2-THOR (Kolve et al., 2019) — as a matched 3D variant for testing perceptual difficulty. Five task families each target one question about agent reasoning:

  • Preference for a goal object, not a location (Figure 2, left) — an agent that always moves to the same object across familiarization should, at test, follow that object to a new location rather than move to the old location's new occupant.
  • Binding that preference to a specific agent — familiarize on one agent's choice, then test whether a model expects a new agent to share it (it should not, or only weakly).
  • Obstacles and inaccessible goals (Figure 4) — a model should expect an agent to approach its nonpreferred object only once its preferred object is blocked, not when it remains reachable.
  • Instrumental action toward a higher-order goal (Figure 5) — the agent must retrieve a key and unlock a green barrier before its actual goal is reachable; the model should expect the detour to the key only when the barrier is actually blocking the goal, across three variants (no barrier, an inconsequential barrier, and a blocking barrier).
  • Rational vs. irrational efficiency (Figure 2, right) — a rational agent should be expected to take a new efficient path around a moved obstacle, while an agent that was already irrational during familiarization should generate no such expectation.

Each evaluation task family contributes 1,000 episodes (2,000 videos: one expected/unexpected pair per episode), provided in two evaluation-set variants — one reusing the same objects and agents seen in training, the other substituting entirely new ones to probe generalization. Because a VoE episode is a passively observed pre-rendered video with no reward signal and no opportunity to actively sample the environment, the authors deliberately exclude standard imitation-learning, inverse-RL, and RL baselines — approaches that require exactly that kind of privileged access — and say so explicitly rather than testing them and reporting a weak result.

To give models something to learn from before evaluation, BIB provides four background-training task families — Single-Object (10,000 episodes), No-Navigation Preference (10,000), Single-Object Multi-Agent (4,000), and Agent-Blocked Instrumental Action (4,000) — each structurally simpler than its corresponding evaluation task (e.g. objects sit close to the agent's start, familiarization and test trials share a distribution). A model has to combine what it separately learns from these pieces to succeed on the evaluation tasks, which recombine them in ways never directly presented during training.

Three baselines, all trained purely by passive observation, were tested: BC-MLP and BC-RNN, behavior-cloning models that predict an agent's next action (a 2D displacement) from a state encoder pretrained with Augmented Temporal Contrast (Stooke et al., 2021) plus either an MLP or a bidirectional-LSTM context encoder over the familiarization trials; and VIDEO-RNN, a ToMnet-inspired (Rabinowitz et al., 2018) video model in which a CNN + bi-LSTM produces a per-agent "characteristic" embedding from the eight familiarization trials, tiled spatially and concatenated to a test frame, then decoded by a U-Net (Ronneberger et al., 2015) that predicts the next frame under pixel-space MSE loss. A fourth baseline, an offline-RL model (Siegel et al., 2020's BM+MPO with a hand-engineered distance-to-goal reward), performed similarly to the BC models and is reported only in the appendix.

Scoring follows the VoE logic directly: a trial's "expectedness" is the model's prediction error — pixel MSE for the video model, action MSE for the BC models — at its single most-surprising step, rather than averaged across the trial, because the authors found averaging consistently produced weaker discrimination between expected and unexpected outcomes. An episode counts as correct if the model's error is higher on the unexpected video than on the paired expected video.

On Preference, all three models land at or below chance (BC-MLP 26.3% — worse than chance — BC-RNN 48.3%, VIDEO-RNN 47.6%): rather than tracking the demonstrated preference, the two RNN-based models default to predicting the agent will approach whichever object is spatially closer in about 70% of trials, and BC-MLP instead encodes a preference for a goal location, contrary to what the background training should have taught it. The same closer-object heuristic drives chance-level performance on Multi-agent binding (48.7 / 48.2 / 50.3): the models fail to bind a preference to the specific agent that displayed it. On Inaccessible goal, all three clear chance (76.9 / 81.6 / 74.0), but the video model still frequently predicts the agent will go to the now-blocked object (Figure 8b) rather than switching to the reachable one.

The efficiency tasks look strong in aggregate (efficient-action averages of 88.8 / 82.5 / 83.1) but that average is carried entirely by near-ceiling path-control (94.0–99.2) and time-control (99.1–99.9) scores; on the irrational-agent sub-task, which asks whether a model can tell a rational agent from an irrational one, the two RNN-based models fall to 56.5% and 50.1% — essentially chance — over-generalizing "efficient path" as an expectation even for an agent that was never rational to begin with. BC-MLP holds up somewhat better here (73.8%). The instrumental-action tasks span the widest range of any family, from near-ceiling to below chance: near-ceiling with no barrier present (98.8 / 98.8 / 99.7), still reasonably strong with an inconsequential barrier (55.2 / 78.2 / 77.0, BC-MLP notably weaker), but crashing on the blocking-barrier condition — the one case that actually requires representing the key-and-lock detour as instrumental — to 47.1%, 56.8%, and 62.9%, with BC-MLP falling below chance entirely. The authors attribute this partly to a structural mismatch with the corresponding background-training task, where the agent (not the object) is the one confined by the barrier.

Two further evaluations appear in the appendix. Swapping in new objects/agents for the video model degrades scores only slightly while preserving the same pattern (appendix Table 3: preference 47.4, multi-agent 50.0, inaccessible goal 61.7, efficient-action average 72.7, instrumental-action average 69.6). Testing on the matched 3D renders is, in the paper's own words, "generally worse overall" on the tasks that have a 3D variant, though not uniformly: efficient-action average drops sharply (83.1 → 62.9) and inaccessible goal falls further (74.0 → 40.0), while multi-agent binding is essentially flat (50.3 → 50.0) and preference actually rises slightly (47.6 → 49.2); the 3D variant was not built for the instrumental-action tasks at all, so no 3D instrumental numbers are reported. A fourth baseline, offline RL (BM+MPO), is reported separately in appendix Table 8 and performs similarly to the BC models overall, so the main text does not analyze it further.

// does it hold up?

The eval read

BIB is unusual among the benchmarks this survey covers in that it isn't an LLM-prompting eval at all: it's a developmental-psychology instrument repurposed for machine learning, scored by pairwise VoE prediction error on models trained from scratch on tens of thousands of synthetic episodes. That changes what "does it hold up" means here, but the paper still has real gaps worth naming precisely.

First, no baseline in the paper is shown to do well across all five task families, and the authors are candid about why: standard imitation-learning, inverse-RL, and RL methods are excluded by design because VoE gives no active rollout or observable reward, and when the paper considers whether AGENT's own strong hand-engineered Bayesian inverse-planning baseline (BIPaCK) could be adapted to BIB, it concludes that doing so "is not straightforward... as it requires features that are not currently provided" (Section 6). So at publication, BIB has no baseline — Bayesian, neural, or otherwise — demonstrated to approach a ceiling; the three tested models are all descendants of the same ToMnet-style characteristic-encoder idea (Rabinowitz et al., 2018): BC-MLP and BC-RNN differ only in whether an MLP or a bidirectional LSTM encodes the familiarization context, with both then passing the result through the same policy MLP to predict the next action, while VIDEO-RNN instead decodes its characteristic embedding with a U-Net to predict the next frame.

Second, BIB's entire premise is that a VoE score is checkable against how infants actually look at these events — the paper's abstract calls this out as the reason to prefer BIB's paradigm over a generic accuracy metric. But this paper reports zero new infant behavioral data: the General Discussion states plainly that "the comparison between human and machine performance will be bolstered by future behavioral studies with infants." That is a reasonable thing to defer to follow-up work, and the authors are explicit that it hasn't been done rather than implying otherwise — but it does mean the benchmark's foundational comparability claim is, within this paper, asserted rather than demonstrated.

Third, the authors themselves flag a real structural confound rather than hiding it: several of the worst failures (Preference, Instrumental: blocking barrier) are attributed partly to background-training tasks whose surface layout doesn't match the corresponding evaluation task — e.g. background training confines the agent with the green barrier (Figure 6c) while the evaluation task confines the object (Figure 5c). Because no control condition isolates this layout mismatch from a genuine failure to generalize abstractly, a reader can't fully separate "the model can't reason about instrumental action" from "the model never saw training data with this surface structure." Finally, Table 1's numbers read as single runs per model per task — no error bars, standard deviations, or repeated-seed variance appear anywhere across the main and appendix result tables I checked (Tables 1, 2, 3, 6, and 8), which matters most for the near-chance cells (Preference, Multi-agent, Irrational Agent) where a few points either way changes the story; the paper's 1,000-pair sample size keeps the pure sampling noise on any single number comparatively small (roughly ±1.5 points at that N), but that isn't the same as reporting variance across training runs.

baselines

Three passively-trained neural models (BC-MLP, BC-RNN, VIDEO-RNN), all built on a ToMnet-style characteristic encoder; active-sampling IL/IRL/RL baselines are excluded by explicit design choice, and the paper's own attempt to reason about a Bayesian inverse-planning baseline concludes it isn't straightforward to build for BIB — so no tested or sketched baseline is shown to do well everywhere.

contamination

Not applicable in the pretraining-leakage sense — stimuli are procedurally generated synthetic video from a published generator. The live risk instead is a background-training/evaluation layout mismatch the authors themselves identify as a likely driver of some failures.

what's missed

No new infant looking-time validation appears in this paper, despite that comparison being BIB's central motivating premise; the authors flag this explicitly as future work. No run-to-run/seed variance is reported for any baseline score.

would raise confidence

An actual infant VoE replication on BIB's own generated stimuli; a stronger or partially-adapted inverse-planning baseline to establish a real ceiling; repeated-seed variance on the near-chance cells; and either layout-matched background training or an ablation that isolates the training/evaluation surface-statistics confound from genuine reasoning failure.

// bottom line

BIB is a carefully constructed, large-scale VoE stress test, and the failures it surfaces in three passively-trained baselines — chance-or-below on preference and multi-agent binding, a collapse on the blocking-barrier instrumental task — are real and precisely characterized. What the paper does not yet do is validate its own comparability-to-infants premise or show that any baseline can approach BIB's ceiling; both are named as open by the authors, not concealed, but a reader citing BIB's numbers as evidence of a durable capability gap should know neither has happened yet.

// takeaways

What to remember

  1. BIB translates five infant-cognition VoE paradigms — goal-object preference, agent-specific preference binding, obstacle/inaccessible goals, instrumental action, and rational-vs-irrational efficiency — into a large, procedurally generated 2D/3D grid-world video benchmark with a single canonical background-training/evaluation split.
  2. Three passively-trained baselines (BC-MLP, BC-RNN, a ToMnet-style video model) clear chance on the obstacle and efficiency tasks but sit at or below chance on preference (26.3–48.3%) and multi-agent binding (48.2–50.3%), and collapse on the blocking-barrier instrumental-action sub-task (47.1–62.9%) — the two RNN models default to whichever object is spatially closer on preference (~70% of trials there) and on multi-agent binding, BC-MLP instead encodes a goal location rather than a goal object, and on the blocking-barrier sub-task all three default to heading straight for the goal object instead of representing the key-and-lock detour as instrumental.
  3. BIB is explicitly positioned as complementary to the contemporaneous AGENT benchmark (Shu et al., 2021): AGENT covers fewer competencies but has a strong hand-engineered Bayesian baseline and many leave-out splits; BIB covers more competencies under one harder canonical split but, as of this paper, has no baseline shown to succeed on it and no infant validation data of its own.
