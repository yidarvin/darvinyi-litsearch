Towards Building Multilingual Language Model for Medicine

This paper ships three artifacts: MMedC, a new 25.5-billion-token, six-language medical training corpus; MMedBench, a rationale-annotated multilingual medical QA benchmark; and MMedLM, MMedLM 2, and MMed-Llama 3, produced by continuing pretraining InternLM, InternLM 2, and Llama 3 on MMedC — closing a little over 40% of the gap to GPT-4 on MMedBench and beating GPT-3.5 on the nine-task average across four English medical benchmarks, all with an 8B open model.

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

Progress on medical language models has almost entirely been an English-language story. GPT-4 and Med-PaLM 2 are graded on the USMLE-style MedQA, on MedMCQA, PubMedQA, and the medical subset of MMLU; open-source medical models such as MedAlpaca, ChatDoctor, PMC-LLaMA, and MEDITRON are trained and evaluated the same way. Meanwhile, general-purpose multilingual LLMs such as BLOOM and InternLM 2 are trained on broad multilingual web corpora, but medical text is a thin slice of that mixture — and the authors hit the practical consequence directly when they try to compare un-fine-tuned open-source models outside English: "the response from these open-source LLMs is relatively poor, making it difficult to draw effective comparisons." No shared corpus or benchmark existed to measure, let alone close, that gap.

The individual pieces of a non-English medical benchmark already existed, just scattered: MedQA's Chinese half, the Spanish HEAD-QA, the French FrenchMedMCQA, the Russian RuMedDaNet, and a Japanese medical-licensing QA set, each built and evaluated in isolation against different local models. What was missing was a single training corpus and a single benchmark that put all six languages — English, Chinese, Japanese, French, Russian, Spanish — on the same axis, so a model's medical competence in one language could actually be compared against its competence in another, and continued domain-adaptive training could be measured consistently across all of them at once.

// how it works

The method

MMedC: a 25.5-billion-token medical corpus

MMedC pools four kinds of source material per language: (i) an automatic filter over CulturaX — a 6.3-trillion-token cleaned multilingual CommonCrawl — built from 200 hand-picked medical seed terms per language; (ii) medical textbooks, OCR'd and cleaned; (iii) open-source medical websites; and (iv) several existing small-scale medical corpora. The result is 25.5 billion tokens across English, Chinese, Japanese, French, Russian, and Spanish. English is the largest share at 42% (10.56B tokens) and Russian the smallest at 7% (1.69B tokens) — but even that smallest slice is nearly 2 billion tokens, i.e. a substantial monolingual medical corpus in its own right.

MMedBench: aggregating five monolingual exams into one multilingual one

MMedBench is built by pooling five existing multi-choice medical QA sources — MedQA (English and Simplified Chinese), a Japanese medical-licensing QA set, FrenchMedMCQA, RuMedDaNet, and HEAD-QA (Spanish) — into 53,566 QA pairs, split 45,048 for training and 8,518 for testing (an 8:1:1 split where no official one already existed). GPT-4 is then prompted, given the question, options, and correct answer, to write a rationale in a fixed "You're a {language} doctor. Analyze the reasons behind choosing this particular option in 100 words..." format. Because that rationale is machine-generated, its quality has to be checked: three annotators manually review a 1,200-sample subset (200 test questions per language) against two criteria — the explanation must be consistent with the stated correct answer, and it must actually articulate the logic behind the answer rather than merely restate it. 94.7% pass, leaving 1,136 human-verified samples that carry rationale-similarity scoring at test time; the training split's 45,048 rationales are used as GPT-4 generated them, without this check. A separate GPT-4 pass sorts every test question into one of 21 medical topics (Internal Medicine and Biochemistry dominate, at 13.5% and 11.09% of the test set respectively), a categorization the paper says was "rigorously verified by at least two clinicians."

MMedLM: continuing pretraining on MMedC

The paper then continues auto-regressive pretraining on MMedC starting from three general backbones — InternLM, InternLM 2, and Llama 3 (8B) — to produce MMedLM, MMedLM 2, and MMed-Llama 3. The paper's evaluation overall spans three regimes — zero-shot, LoRA-based parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT), and full fine-tuning — but the open-source models, MMedLM/MMedLM 2/MMed-Llama 3 included, are reported only under PEFT and full fine-tuning: the paper found open-source zero-shot responses too poor to support a meaningful comparison, so it fine-tunes them instead, using MMedBench's own 45,048-pair training split. Every fine-tuned model is trained on both a multiple-choice-only instruction and a choice-plus-rationale instruction, so it can be scored on both accuracy and rationale quality. Rationale quality is scored three ways — BLEU, ROUGE, BERTScore (reported as BLEU-1/ROUGE-1 in the main table) — plus, for a six-model subset, human and GPT-4 relative rankings.

// what they found

The results

Under full fine-tuning on MMedBench's own 8,518-question test set, continuing pretraining on MMedC lifts every backbone: MMed-Llama 3 reaches 67.75% average accuracy against 62.79% for a Llama-3 baseline fine-tuned the identical way without MMedC pretraining (+4.96 points), and MMedLM 2 reaches 67.30% against InternLM 2's 58.59% (+8.71 points). Zero-shot GPT-4 scores 74.27% average accuracy across the six languages (79.43% with the paper's strongest configuration, 5-shot chain-of-thought). MMed-Llama 3 — at 8B parameters, and open-weights rather than closed behind an API — closes roughly 40% of the gap between the fine-tuned Llama-3 baseline and GPT-4 (11.48 points down to 6.52 points, i.e. 4.96/11.48 = 43.2% — or 39.6% if GPT-4’s average is recomputed from its own printed per-language cells; see the critique).

The headline average obscures how uneven the six languages are. In the full-fine-tuning block, French is the lowest-scoring language for 11 of the 14 models reported (BLOOMZ through MMed-Llama 3); Japanese is lower than French only for Mistral, BioMistral, and Llama 3. MMed-Llama 3's own per-language spread runs from 55.63% (French) to 79.25% (Chinese) — a 23.6-point range within a single model, considerably wider than the +4.96-point gain the "Avg." column credits to MMedC pretraining.

On the rationale side (Table 2, full fine-tuning), MMedLM 2 scores the best average BLEU-1/ROUGE-1 at 48.81/45.29, ahead of InternLM 2's 46.52/42.86 (+2.29 BLEU-1); MMed-Llama 3 scores 47.21/43.29 against Llama 3's 46.76/42.84 (+0.45 BLEU-1) — a roughly five-times-smaller rationale-quality gain for the Llama-3 lineage than for the InternLM 2 lineage, even though the accuracy gains run the other way (MMed-Llama 3's +4.96 accuracy points vs. MMedLM 2's +8.71).

An ablation on MMed-Llama 3 (Table 4) isolates each ingredient. Adding rationale-supervised fine-tuning on top of multiple-choice-only fine-tuning — both without any MMedC pretraining — raises accuracy from 58.72% to 62.79% (+4.07 points). Adding English-only continued pretraining on top of that (MMed-Llama3en) actually lowers accuracy to 61.39%, which the authors attribute to overfitting on English; extending that pretraining to the full six-language MMedC (MMed-Llama3ocr) raises it to 64.40%. Adding the automatically filtered "US-Data" on top of the human-verified "HQ-Data" (textbooks and websites) raises it further to 67.75% (+3.35 points), with an additional +0.29 BLEU-1 (46.93 → 47.22) and +0.16 ROUGE-1 (43.13 → 43.29) rationale-quality gain.

On four public English benchmarks — MedQA, MedMCQA, PubMedQA, and the six sub-tasks of MMLU-Medical, nine tasks in total — MMed-Llama 3 reaches a 72.59% average, ahead of GPT-3.5's 67.69% but well behind GPT-4 (85.36%) and Med-PaLM 2 (86.66%). The paper's claimed "4.5%, 4.3% and 2.2% performance gain[s]... respectively on MedQA, MedMCQA and PubMedQA" are each a gain over a different comparator — the best other open-source score in that specific column, not a single fixed baseline: Llama 3 on MedQA (65.4 vs. 60.9), MEDITRON on MedMCQA (63.5 vs. 59.2), and PMC-LLaMA on PubMedQA (80.1 vs. 77.9).

// does it hold up?

The eval read

MMedBench's real contribution is a genuinely useful piece of evaluation infrastructure — a shared, six-language axis that lets a lab see exactly where a "multilingual medical" claim actually breaks down (French, repeatedly). But three aspects of its own construction deserve scrutiny before its accuracy numbers get cited as a universal multilingual-medical score.

First, the closed-source ceiling is measured, but not fairly comparable to the open-source floor. GPT-4 and GPT-3.5 are never fine-tuned, since neither can be tuned via API — GPT-4 is reported both zero-shot and under a 5-shot chain-of-thought prompt (the paper's strongest closed-source configuration, 79.43%) — while every open-source model in the PEFT and full-fine-tuning blocks is fine-tuned directly on MMedBench's own 45,048-pair training split before being scored on its 8,518-pair test split. That asymmetry is not avoidable given a closed API — but it does mean the "67.75 vs. 74.27" gap to GPT-4 compares a model trained on the test distribution's own training half against one that has never seen it. The paper is candid about a related point: "as the training data for these closed-source models are confidential, it is difficult to judge whether they are really 'zero-shot'" — a caveat that applies just as much to whether GPT-4's own pretraining already contains some of MedQA's, HEAD-QA's, or FrenchMedMCQA's original public test questions.

Second, no contamination check appears anywhere in the paper — not in the main text, the discussion, or the methodology section — for any of the five source benchmarks MMedBench aggregates: MedQA (2020), HEAD-QA (2019), RuMedDaNet (2022), FrenchMedMCQA (2023), and the Japanese licensing-exam dataset (2023). All five are public, and several substantially predate GPT-4 and Llama 3's training cutoffs. A model's performance on public medical exams is a live contamination concern in exactly this literature — the paper itself cites Nori et al.'s work on GPT-4 and USMLE-style prompting — yet MMedBench neither tests for nor discusses the possibility that some of its own test questions already sat inside a baseline's pretraining corpus.

Third, the rationale-quality pipeline has a structural circularity worth naming precisely. GPT-4 generates every reference rationale used to score every model's rationale similarity (BLEU/ROUGE/BERTScore), and a separate GPT-4 call is also one of the raters in the human-vs-automatic correlation study (Figure 4) — the same study that finds GPT-4's ranking has the highest correlation with human judgment (τ=0.660) and, on that basis, recommends BERTScore (τ=0.538, the best "absolute" metric) for future work specifically because GPT-4-as-judge is "not easy to scale." None of this makes the reported numbers wrong, but a model whose outputs anchor the reference text is not an independent evaluator of that same model family's outputs — and neither GPT-4 nor GPT-4 (5-shot, CoT), the two configurations reported for accuracy, appear at all in the rationale-quality table, so the model that wrote every reference rationale is never itself scored against it.

The six language columns are not the same task, which undercuts the per-language reading. Supplementary Table 4 and the dataset descriptions show the columns differ in shape, not just in difficulty. In the test split, 48.39% of French questions have multiple correct options; English, Chinese, Spanish and Russian are at 0% and Japanese near 20%. Under exact-match scoring that alone could account for French being the weakest column almost everywhere — it is a harder format, not necessarily a harder language. Russian runs the other way: RuMedDaNet is, in the paper's own words, processed "into a binary-choice question format," so its floor is 50%, which is why the weakest models spike there (GPT-3.5 scores 32.48 on French against 66.36 on Russian). And the test splits are wildly uneven — 199 Japanese items against 3,426 Chinese, a 17× range — while the headline Avg. is an unweighted macro-mean, so those 199 Japanese questions carry a full sixth of it. None of this is hidden; it is all in the supplement. But the paper's own framing invites cross-language comparison that the construction does not really support.

The most-quoted number in the paper does not reconcile with its own row. Table 1 prints zero-shot GPT-4's average as 74.27, but the mean of that row's six printed per-language cells (78.00, 75.07, 72.91, 56.59, 83.62, 85.67) is 75.31. Every other row in the table reconciles to within 0.02, so this is not rounding. It matters because 74.27 is the figure the paper leans on ("in proximity to GPT-4's accuracy") and the reference point for the gap MMed-Llama 3 closes: on the printed average the model closes 43.2% of it, on the recomputed one 39.6%. The conclusion survives either way, but the two figures cannot both be right.

baselines

GPT-4/GPT-3.5/Gemini are never fine-tuned (no API fine-tuning available) — GPT-4 is reported both zero-shot and under a 5-shot chain-of-thought prompt; every open-source row is fine-tuned directly on MMedBench's own training split before testing, so the reported gap to closed models is not an apples-to-apples comparison in either direction.

contamination

Not discussed anywhere in the paper. MMedBench pools five public, pre-existing benchmarks (MedQA 2020, HEAD-QA 2019, RuMedDaNet 2022, FrenchMedMCQA 2023, a 2023 Japanese licensing set) that predate GPT-4/Llama 3's training cutoffs, and none of the five is checked for pretraining overlap.

what's missed

The six-language "Avg." hides a per-language spread as wide as 23.6 points within MMed-Llama 3 alone (79.25% Chinese vs. 55.63% French); French is the lowest-scoring language in 11 of 14 full-fine-tuning rows. The 45,048 training-split rationales are used exactly as GPT-4 generated them — only the 1,136-sample test-set rationale split is human-verified.

would raise confidence

A contamination audit against the five source test sets; an independent (non-GPT-4) reference rationale, or at least a human-written gold subset to sanity-check the GPT-4-generated references; an empirical — not merely prompted — check of GPT-4-as-judge position bias, since the ranking prompt tells GPT-4 to "avoid any position biases" rather than measuring whether it actually does.

// bottom line

MMedBench is a real, useful step — a shared six-language axis for medical LLM evaluation — and its ablation study is unusually clean about attributing gains to specific ingredients (rationale supervision, multilingual pretraining, human-verified vs. auto-filtered data). But its accuracy numbers rest on an untested contamination assumption, its rationale-quality numbers rest on a single model (GPT-4) supplying and partly judging its own reference text, and its "multilingual" framing needs the per-language breakdown, not the average, to be read honestly.

// takeaways

What to remember

  1. Three separate artifacts, one paper: MMedC (25.5B tokens, 6 languages) is the training corpus; MMedBench (53,566 QA pairs — 45,048 train / 8,518 test — with GPT-4-generated, partially human-verified rationales) is the benchmark; MMedLM / MMedLM 2 / MMed-Llama 3 are the models trained on MMedC. Keep any given number attached to the right one.
  2. Continued pretraining on MMedC helps consistently under full fine-tuning: MMed-Llama 3 beats a same-recipe Llama-3 baseline 67.75% vs. 62.79% on MMedBench, and MMedLM 2 beats InternLM 2 67.30% vs. 58.59%.
  3. The benchmark's multilingual average masks a large per-language spread — French is the hardest language for 11 of 14 evaluated models, and MMed-Llama 3's own best-to-worst-language gap (23.6 points) dwarfs the +4.96-point average gain the paper credits to MMedC.
  4. Only 1,136 of MMedBench's rationale annotations (a 200-per-language test subset) are human-verified, at a 94.7% pass rate; the 45,048 training rationales are used exactly as GPT-4 generated them.
  5. No contamination analysis is reported for any of the five public source benchmarks MMedBench aggregates, several of which predate the LLMs being tested on them.
