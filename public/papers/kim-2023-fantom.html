FANToM: A Benchmark for Stress-testing Machine Theory of Mind in Interactions

A conversational theory-of-mind benchmark built on real information asymmetry: characters step away from a multiparty chat and rejoin it, and six converging question formats about the same fact catch a model that answers one format right while failing another that demands identical reasoning — "illusory" theory of mind.

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

Theory of mind (ToM) — tracking what another agent thinks, knows, or believes — is usually tested on language models with short, hand-written or templated narratives (ToMi's Sally-Anne-style stories, Social IQa, and similar). The authors argue narratives have two structural weaknesses. First, a narrative is a condensed report: whatever a human curator chose to include or omit becomes a signal in itself, so a sentence like "Carlos did not see this, so he does not know where the apple is" hands the model an explicit hint about the very mental state it's supposed to infer — a form of reporting bias models can exploit rather than reason through. Second, narratives are static and non-interactive, unlike the real social interactions (conversations) in which ToM actually matters for communication.

The paper also raises a validity gap in how ToM is measured at all, invoking two criteria from psychology (Quesque and Rossetti, 2020): non-merging (the evaluation must force a real distinction between what the respondent believes and what the other party believes, otherwise a model could get credit for the right answer just by projecting its own belief) and mentalizing (correct answers shouldn't be explainable by some easier, lower-level shortcut such as high word overlap between an answer and a salient span of the input). A benchmark that a model can pass by pattern-matching salient text rather than tracking a distinct mental state doesn't actually validate ToM — and existing narrative benchmarks don't systematically control for either criterion.

Finally, prior work typically asks about a mental state in only one question format. That leaves no way to tell genuine, coherent ToM reasoning apart from what the authors call illusory ToM: a model that answers one phrasing of a question correctly (say, a multiple-choice belief question) while failing a differently-formatted question that requires exactly the same underlying inference (say, listing who does and doesn't know the fact). FANToM is built to close all three gaps at once: conversational grounding, information-asymmetry-based non-merging, and multiple converging question formats over the same fact.

// how it works

The method

FANToM's conversations are entirely LLM-generated small talk (topics like pets, risk-taking, personal growth; each topic has several subtopics), produced by prompting InstructGPT davinci-003 to have two or three named characters converse, then to discuss further subtopics as the conversation continues. As it unfolds, characters are scripted to leave ("Hey guys, I'll go grab a coffee," drawn from a predefined list of 64 leaving reasons) and later rejoin ("I'm back, what are you guys discussing now?"), with up to five characters present at once. Whatever gets said while a character is away becomes inaccessible information for that character once they return — the deliberate information asymmetry the whole benchmark is built around. To identify exactly what that inaccessible content is, the authors prompt GPT-4 (gpt-4-0314) with the preceding and current conversation segments, separated by an explicit "[PersonX joined the conversation]" marker, and ask it what was said before the join that wasn't repeated after.

From each piece of inaccessible information, GPT-4 generates three non-yes/no FactQs (e.g., "What is the breed of Linda's dog?"), each with two candidate answers: a Full Fact A grounded in the full preceding conversation the absent character missed, and a Limited Fact A grounded only in what that character actually witnessed (or a statement that no information was given, if none was). The pipeline then rephrases each FactQ and its two answers into six distinct theory-of-mind question types about a specific character PersonX's belief or knowledge of that fact: (1) BeliefQ[Dist.], a free-form belief question scored by comparing a SentenceBERT-embedding distance between the response and the two relabeled answers — "Omniscient-view Belief" (from Full Fact A) vs. "PersonX-centric Belief" (from Limited Fact A) — backed by a token-F1 check so nonsensical text can't win purely on embedding distance; (2) BeliefQ[Choice], the same question as a forced choice between those two options; (3) AnswerabilityQ[List], "list all characters who know the correct answer"; (4) InfoAccessQ[List], "list all characters who know this information" (given the Full Fact A directly, so it isolates the second of the two reasoning steps AnswerabilityQ requires); and the yes/no counterparts (5) AnswerabilityQ[Y/N] and (6) InfoAccessQ[Y/N], asked per character. All six types probe the same underlying question — "who is aware of this information?" — just reformatted, which is what makes cross-format inconsistency diagnostic.

Every conversation and question set went through Amazon Mechanical Turk validation: 32 qualified annotators (three per conversation) flagged incoherent or unsafe conversations — 10 of 256 conversations got some incoherence votes (none reached a majority), and all 10 were refined; none were flagged unsafe — and workers separately verified the BeliefQ[Choice] answer options, with ~8.6% of question sets discarded as erroneous. The result is 256 conversations and roughly 10K questions: 1,415 each of BeliefQ[Dist.] and BeliefQ[Choice], 703 each of FactQ, AnswerabilityQ[List], and InfoAccessQ[List], and 2,689 each of AnswerabilityQ[Y/N] and InfoAccessQ[Y/N] (the Y/N formats iterate over every character present, so they dominate the count). Two input conditions are tested: a short context (just the segment around the leave/rejoin, averaging 13.8 turns and 21.9 words/turn — versus 4.9 turns and 4.2 words/turn for the comparable ToMi narratives) and a full context spanning the entire multi-subtopic conversation.

Scoring differs by format: accuracy for BeliefQ[Dist.] (distance + token F1), BeliefQ[Choice], AnswerabilityQ[List], and InfoAccessQ[List] (list-type answers get no partial credit — every character must be correctly included or excluded); weighted F1 for the Y/N formats. Two aggregate metrics sit on top: an All score per question family (e.g., "All AnswerabilityQs[List+Y/N]") requiring a model to get both the list-type and yes/no version right for a character, and the benchmark's strictest metric, ALL*, which requires a model to answer all six ToM question types correctly for the same underlying information piece — the direct operationalization of "illusory ToM." Since humans were only asked BeliefQ[Choice], AnswerabilityQ[List], and InfoAccessQ[List] (asking binary yes/no versions of already-answered list questions would be redundant for a human), the paper also reports ALL, which is ALL* minus BeliefQ[Dist.], to make a fair human/model comparison possible.

// what they found

The results

Across thirteen instruction-tuned models — GPT-4 (0613 June and October snapshots, and 0314), ChatGPT (0613), InstructGPT (davinci-003, curie-001), Flan-T5-XL/XXL, Flan-UL2, Falcon Instruct (7B, 40B), Mistral Instruct 7B, Zephyr 7B, and Llama-2 Chat 70B — every model scores far below the human baseline (11 graduate CS students, 32 question sets), and the paper's headline claim is that models trail humans "by more than 70% on average" once both individual questions and the full ALL/ALL* aggregates are counted. On the short-conversation input, humans reach 87.5% on the All Question Types (ALL) score, 93.8% on BeliefQ[Choice], and 90.6% on both AnswerabilityQ[List] and InfoAccessQ[List]. GPT-4 0613 (June), the strongest model, reaches 73.3% on BeliefQ[Choice] and 37.8%/36.4% on the two List questions — but only 12.3% on ALL. GPT-4 0613 (October) does even worse on the strict aggregate at 4.1% ALL despite 68.4% on BeliefQ[Choice]. Falcon Instruct 40B and ChatGPT 0613 land at essentially 0.0% and 0.1% ALL respectively, despite scoring 54.3% and 53.5% on BeliefQ[Choice] alone — the gap between a single question format and the full-consistency score is the paper's central "illusory ToM" evidence.

Chain-of-thought prompting ("let's think step by step," following Kojima et al., 2022) and fine-tuning both help, but neither closes the gap. With CoT, GPT-4 0613 (June)'s ALL rises from 12.3% to 26.6% (its ALL* rises from 8.2% to 18.4%), and its All-AnswerabilityQs[List+Y/N] / All-InfoAccessQs[List+Y/N] scores reach 40.2% / 57.7% — still well short of humans' 90.6% on both. Fine-tuning Flan-T5-XL directly on FANToM (topic-held-out split) pushes it to 53.7% ALL, 55.9% on All-AnswerabilityQs, and 54.4% on All-InfoAccessQs — closer to, but still below, human performance on the coherence metrics, even though the paper separately notes fine-tuning can push individual question-type accuracy above the human level.

A second finding cuts against the idea that better fact recall means better ToM. Token-F1 on plain FactQ (a proxy for basic comprehension: can the model find the relevant fact at all?) doesn't track accuracy on BeliefQ[Dist.] (can it correctly withhold that fact from a character who shouldn't know it? Figure 3 plots token-F1 for FactQ against accuracy, not token-F1, for BeliefQ[Dist.]). InstructGPT davinci-003 scores 60.9 FactQ token-F1 but only 16.5 BeliefQ[Dist.] accuracy, while Llama-2 Chat and Mistral Instruct 7B score lower on FactQ token-F1 (52.7 and 56.6) yet higher on BeliefQ[Dist.] accuracy (17.8 and 26.2) — models good at finding the fact aren't necessarily good at knowing whose fact it isn't.

Two further breakdowns sharpen the picture. On the binary Y/N formats given the short conversation, models consistently do worse on AnswerabilityQ[Y/N] (which needs an extra reasoning step) than on InfoAccessQ[Y/N] — e.g. GPT-4 0613 (October) scores 75.7 vs. 91.5, and GPT-4 0613 (June) 85.9 vs. 90.3 — though that ordering doesn't reliably hold for the harder List-type versions. And on BeliefQ specifically, models do better on second-order beliefs than first-order ones (mirroring a pattern Le et al., 2019 also observed on ToMi): GPT-4 0613 (June) reaches 66.7 on second-order vs. 63.8 on first-order. Within second-order beliefs, models generally score higher on cyclic beliefs (A's belief about B's belief about A) than acyclic ones that track a third character (A's belief about B's belief about C) — e.g. Mistral Instruct 35.5 cyclic vs. 25.1 acyclic — but GPT-4 0613 (June) is the counterexample: it's actually better on acyclic (67.1) than cyclic (66.3) without CoT, and CoT flips it back in line with the general pattern — cyclic rises to 69.1 while acyclic falls to 66.0.

// does it hold up?

The eval read

FANToM's core idea is genuinely sharp: instead of one question format per fact, ask six differently-shaped questions that all reduce to "who is aware of this?" and score a model only if it's coherent across all of them. That's a real advance over single-format ToM benchmarks (ToMi, Social IQa) that can't distinguish a model with actual belief-tracking from one that got lucky on a particular phrasing. But a few things temper how literally the headline near-zero ALL* numbers should be read.

baselines

All thirteen models are evaluated zero/few-shot off the shelf; only Flan-T5-XL is fine-tuned, and only text-curie-001 is fine-tuned as a secondary check (per Appendix B). There is no head-to-head test of a dedicated ToM-tracking method — the paper cites Sclar et al. (2023)'s plug-and-play multi-character belief tracker in related work but never runs it on FANToM itself, so the benchmark's real ceiling for a ToM-aware (not just larger or fine-tuned) system is untested.

contamination

Classic pretraining-leakage contamination is unlikely since every conversation, fact, and belief label is freshly synthesized. The bigger risk is a self-generation loop: the conversations come from InstructGPT davinci-003 and the fact/belief/answerability labels are all authored by GPT-4 (gpt-4-0314). Because the entire benchmark's linguistic style and content distribution is anchored to the OpenAI model family being evaluated, GPT-4 and ChatGPT may have a stylistic home-field advantage over Falcon, Mistral, and Llama-2 that has nothing to do with theory of mind — a possibility the paper doesn't test (e.g., by having a disjoint model family generate half the benchmark).

what's missed

Human/MTurk validation only covers conversation coherence/safety and the correctness of BeliefQ[Choice] options (~8.6% discarded) — the FactQ ground-truth answers (Full/Limited Fact A) and the BeliefQ[Dist.] question and options, all generated by GPT-4 (§3.2, App. A.2–A.3), are not described as independently human-verified. (The AnswerabilityQ/InfoAccessQ list-type gold labels are not GPT-4-authored — they fall out mechanically from the scripted leave/rejoin structure, i.e. which characters were actually present when the information was shared, so that piece of ground truth is deterministic rather than model-generated.) Any systematic GPT-4 error in the Fact-A or BeliefQ generation step would silently become "ground truth" that every other model — including GPT-4 itself — is scored against. Separately, because ALL* is a strict six-way conjunction, nearly every non-fine-tuned model collapses to a narrow 0.0-0.4 band on it — the metric is excellent for demonstrating illusory ToM exists, but too compressed near zero to rank models against each other; that discrimination only comes from the per-type breakdowns (Figures 2-3, Table 2).

would raise confidence

Independent human verification of the FactQ/InfoAccess gold answers (not just BeliefQ[Choice] and coherence); a version of the pipeline built with a disjoint generator model family to rule out in-family scoring bias; a larger and more demographically varied human baseline that also covers BeliefQ[Dist.] and the Y/N formats (currently humans answer neither); and variance/confidence intervals on the near-floor ALL* numbers given how few points separate 0.0 from 0.4.

// bottom line

FANToM's illusory-ToM diagnostic — the same fact, six question shapes, one strict conjunctive score — is a real methodological contribution that static narrative ToM benchmarks can't offer, and the FactQ-vs-BeliefQ[Dist.] result (models that find facts well don't reliably withhold them from characters who shouldn't have them) is convincing on its own terms. But because the benchmark's conversations and gold labels are generated (not independently verified) by the exact model family topping the leaderboard, read the near-zero ALL*/ALL scores as strong evidence that current LLMs are inconsistent across question formats about the same belief, rather than as a calibrated absolute measure of how much theory of mind any given model has.

// takeaways

What to remember

  1. FANToM moves theory-of-mind evaluation from static narratives into 256 LLM-generated multiparty conversations with real information asymmetry — characters leave and rejoin, and whatever was said in their absence becomes inaccessible information, directly operationalizing psychology's "non-merging" criterion for a valid ToM test.
  2. Its signature move is asking six differently-formatted questions (BeliefQ[Dist./Choice], AnswerabilityQ[List/Y-N], InfoAccessQ[List/Y-N]) about the same underlying fact and requiring all six correct via a strict ALL*/ALL aggregate — GPT-4 0613 (June) hits 73.3% on BeliefQ[Choice] alone but only 12.3% ALL, against humans' 87.5% ALL, catching "illusory" ToM that single-format benchmarks miss.
  3. Chain-of-thought and fine-tuning each help (CoT lifts GPT-4 0613 June's ALL to 26.6%; fine-tuned Flan-T5-XL reaches 53.7% ALL and can beat humans on individual question types) but neither closes the coherence gap — and because the benchmark's conversations and gold labels are themselves GPT-4/InstructGPT-generated and only partially human-verified, the near-zero ALL* floor is best read as evidence of cross-format inconsistency, not a precisely calibrated ToM score.
