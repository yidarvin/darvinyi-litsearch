WILDS: A Benchmark of in-the-Wild Distribution Shifts

Ten real-world datasets — hospital-to-hospital tumor patches, camera-trap wildlife photos, molecules split by chemical scaffold, satellite imagery split by time and country — built to measure whether models survive the distribution shifts that actually occur in deployment, not the synthetic corruptions or cartoon-vs-photo splits standard robustness benchmarks use instead.

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

By 2020 the ML robustness literature had a well-stocked toolbox of distribution-shift benchmarks, and almost none of them involved a distribution shift that actually happens on its own. ImageNet-C corrupts images with synthetic noise and blur. The Backgrounds Challenge and Waterbirds swap image backgrounds. Colored MNIST recolors digits. PACS and DeepFashion Remixed generalize across curated splits — photos vs. cartoons, one object scale vs. another. These are useful, controlled testbeds, but as the authors put it, "robustness to these synthetic shifts need not transfer to the kinds of shifts that arise in real-world deployments" — and prior work had already shown exactly that kind of transfer failure. Meanwhile the shifts that actually break deployed models look nothing like a corruption filter: a tumor classifier trained on slides from a handful of hospitals meeting a new hospital's staining protocol; a wildlife-monitoring model meeting camera traps it has never seen; a poverty-mapping model meeting a country it wasn't trained on.

WILDS's fix is to stop simulating the shift and instead curate datasets where a real, naturally occurring distribution shift already exists and already degrades performance — built together with the domain experts (in medicine, ecology, genomics, remote sensing) who have to deal with these shifts to get their own work done. Every dataset had to clear three bars: (1) a real ID–OOD performance drop, not just a distribution difference that happens not to matter; (2) real-world relevance — splits and metrics chosen with domain experts, not invented for convenience; and (3) potential leverage — the shift has to be solvable in principle, with domain annotations and multiple training domains available so a method actually has something to learn from.

The paper organizes distribution shift into two regimes (plus a hybrid of both). In domain generalization, training and test domains are disjoint — e.g. molecules with an unseen chemical scaffold in OGB-MolPCBA, or a hospital never seen in training in Camelyon17. In subpopulation shift, the test domains are the same domains seen in training, just in different, sometimes much smaller proportions — e.g. a demographic identity group in CivilComments that is a small slice of the training data but the group a fair model has to do well on regardless.

// how it works

The method

WILDS is a curation exercise as much as a benchmark: ten existing real-world datasets, each substantially reworked (relabeled, resplit, or reprocessed) to make its native distribution shift precise, standardized, and usable in a common ML pipeline. Each example is a triple (x, y, d) — input, label, and domain — and every dataset partitions its domains cleanly across the three shift regimes above (five domain-generalization datasets, one pure subpopulation-shift dataset, and four hybrids that combine both). Figure 1 is the paper's own picture of the distinction: in domain generalization, disjoint scaffold groups (P_sc1 … P_sc44930 for training, P_sc44931 … P_sc90124 for test) mean the model has literally never seen a molecule from the test scaffolds; in subpopulation shift, the same regions (Americas, Africa) appear on both sides, but the model is graded on its worst region, not its average.

The ten datasets span six input modalities (photos, tissue/cell microscopy images, molecular graphs, satellite imagery, text, and source code) and range from 6,515 examples (GlobalWheat-wilds) to 539,502 (Amazon-wilds); domain counts range from 5 hospitals (Camelyon17) to 120,084 molecular scaffolds (OGB-MolPCBA). Figure 2 is the paper's full roster.

The other methodological contribution is less visible but does real work: how do you measure an "ID vs. OOD gap" fairly when every dataset's domains are structured differently? The paper works through five distinct comparison protocols and uses whichever is feasible per dataset — test-to-test (train and test on data from the OOD distribution itself, holding evaluation data fixed — never actually used here, since it needs prohibitively much OOD training data); mixed-to-test (train on a mixture of ID and OOD data, evaluate on OOD only — used for RxRx1, GlobalWheat, FMoW, PovertyMap); train-to-train (train and evaluate on held-out ID data — used for iWildCam2020, Camelyon17, Py150, where many interchangeable domains make ID and OOD roughly comparable in difficulty); average (report mean instead of worst-case performance — used for the subpopulation datasets CivilComments and Amazon); and random split (shuffle everything i.i.d. — used for OGB-MolPCBA, to stay consistent with the Open Graph Benchmark's own protocol). Every baseline is trained with standard architectures (ResNet/DenseNet for images, DistilBERT for text, a Graph Isomorphism Network for molecules, Faster-RCNN for wheat-head detection) and selected using an out-of-distribution validation set, deliberately mimicking how a practitioner without OOD test labels would actually tune a model.

To make this reproducible rather than just reported, the paper ships an installable Python package (wilds) that automates dataset download, exposes the same get_dataset / get_train_loader / Grouper interface across all ten datasets, bundles the default models and hyperparameters used for every baseline number in the paper, and standardizes evaluation — plus a public leaderboard, so a new training algorithm can be benchmarked against ERM and the paper's own baselines without re-implementing any of the ten pipelines.

// what they found

The results

Standard training (empirical risk minimization, ERM) shows a real, substantial ID-OOD gap on every one of the ten datasets. Table 1 reports it dataset by dataset, each using its own metric and its own ID-comparison protocol (see above):

Among the nine datasets scored on a percentage-point metric (excluding PovertyMap's Pearson-r), CivilComments-wilds has the largest ID-OOD gap (36.2 points: 92.2% average accuracy vs. 56.0% worst-group accuracy) and OGB-MolPCBA the smallest (7.2 points of average precision). Crucially, the paper doesn't stop at showing the gap — on the datasets where a mixed-to-test comparison was feasible, training on a mixture of ID and OOD data lets the same model reach high accuracy on both, which is the paper's evidence that the OOD test sets aren't intrinsically harder examples; the shift itself, not example difficulty, is producing the drop.

The second, more consequential result is what happens when you throw an actual distribution-shift method at these gaps instead of plain ERM. The paper tests three representative baselines against every applicable dataset: CORAL (penalizes feature-distribution differences across domains, built for unsupervised domain adaptation), IRM (penalizes domain-dependent optimal classifiers, built for domain generalization), and Group DRO (minimizes worst-domain loss, built for subpopulation shift) — each applied across all applicable Wilds datasets regardless of the shift type it was originally designed for. Sweeping all ten datasets against ERM's OOD number: on eight of them (Camelyon17, RxRx1, OGB-MolPCBA, GlobalWheat, FMoW, PovertyMap, Amazon, Py150), none of the three baselines beats ERM — several make things markedly worse (IRM drops RxRx1 from 29.9 to 8.2). On iWildCam2020, Table 2's single OOD column shows CORAL nominally ahead (32.8 vs. ERM's 31.0), but the paper's own fuller appendix breakdown (Table 4, which reports ID and OOD Macro F1 and accuracy across validation and test) shows CORAL trailing ERM on training-ID performance (43.5 vs. 47.0 test-ID Macro F1) and validation, and the authors' own verdict on that fuller table is explicit: "these did not improve upon the ERM baseline." So the OOD-only edge doesn't hold up as an improvement even by the paper's own account. CivilComments-wilds is the only dataset where the paper's own evaluation concludes a baseline actually improves over ERM — CORAL 65.6%, IRM 66.3%, Group DRO 70.0% vs. ERM's 56.0% — though even Group DRO's 70.0% still trails the in-distribution average accuracy of 92.2% by a wide margin. The authors attribute part of that CivilComments result to their implementation's heuristic upsampling of the minority domain rather than the algorithms' core mechanism (Appendix E.6).

// does it hold up?

The eval read

WILDS is unusually candid about its own evaluation's soft spots, which makes it easier to trust and easier to critique precisely. Three things stand out.

First, the headline "OOD performance is consistently and substantially lower" claim leans on five different ID-comparison protocols, and only two of them (test-to-test, mixed-to-test) actually control for the evaluation distribution. Train-to-train, average, and random-split comparisons — used for six of the ten datasets — don't isolate the shift from baseline difficulty differences between the ID and OOD distributions; the paper is explicit about this trade-off (Section 5.2) but a reader skimming just Table 1 could easily miss that "ID" means something structurally different from row to row.

Second, Camelyon17-wilds is the dataset the paper itself flags as fragile: its own guidance to method developers warns that "practical limitations mean that for some datasets, we have data from only a limited number of domains (e.g., one OOD test hospital in Camelyon17-wilds)," and Figure 18 shows exactly what that limitation costs — across 10 random seeds with identical hyperparameters, OOD validation accuracy clusters tightly (0.81–0.89) while OOD test accuracy on the single held-out hospital swings from 0.62 to 0.82, a standard deviation of 6.4%, the largest of any percentage-based metric in Table 1. That's not a training-instability story so much as a one-hospital-sample-size story: a paper reporting a single random seed's Camelyon17 number could be reporting anywhere in a 20-point-wide band by luck alone.

Third, the paper is unusually forthright about negative results and non-results, which is a real mark of rigor rather than a gap: Appendix F documents five additional datasets (stop-and-frisk stop records, ENCODE transcription-factor binding, BDD100K driving scenes, an Amazon category/time variant, and Yelp) that the authors considered and excluded from WILDS specifically because the distribution shift didn't produce a meaningful performance drop. The stop-and-frisk case is the most interesting of these: despite large precision disparities across race and borough, retraining specifically on the worst-performing subgroups (Black pedestrians, Bronx stops) didn't close the gap — evidence the authors interpret as the disparity being a data-noise/policy artifact rather than a distribution-shift problem the model could fix by seeing more in-domain data. Separately, Section 7.1 reports that excluding CivilComments and Amazon (whose OOD evaluation is on a smaller subpopulation and is naturally noisier), average ID and OOD standard deviations in Table 1 are comparable (2.0% vs. 2.6%) — a direct, self-administered check against the "underspecification" story (that many models fit ID data equally well but diverge wildly OOD) that other papers use to explain distribution-shift failures.

What this doesn't settle: the negative result for CORAL/IRM/Group DRO is a fair test of those specific algorithms on these specific datasets, but each was designed for a different shift regime than most of what it's tested against here (CORAL for domain adaptation, not generalization; Group DRO for subpopulation shift, not the domain-generalization datasets it's also run on) — the authors say as much themselves as a reason for "optimism" about future methods, not a claim that the distribution-shift problem is unsolvable. Three baselines is also a thin evidentiary base for a "current methods don't help" conclusion, however real the negative pattern is across nine of ten datasets.

baselines

CORAL, IRM, and Group DRO are each applied outside their original shift regime on most datasets (CORAL for domain adaptation, Group DRO for subpopulation shift, both also run on pure domain-generalization datasets) — the authors flag this themselves as a reason the negative result may not generalize to purpose-built future methods.

contamination

Not an LLM-era text/answer leakage concern; the analogous risk here is domain leakage across the hospital/camera-trap/scaffold/user partitions, which are explicit and disjoint by construction. The paper does report several near-duplicate controls at split-construction time: Amazon-wilds removes reviews with identical text across users (Appendix E.9.4); Py150-wilds quarantines every CodeSearchNet repository — already used to pretrain the baseline model — into the training set (Appendix E.10.4); iWildCam2020-wilds places "all images from the same sequence (i.e., all images taken in the same burst)... together in the same split" (Appendix E.1.1); and FMoW-wilds' split rule makes "sure that the data splits all contain disjoint locations" (Appendix E.7.4). Two gaps stand out, and one the paper concedes outright — though the ID-split-from-training-domains shape recurs beyond them, e.g. GlobalWheat-wilds draws both its Validation (ID) and Test (ID) sets from "the same 18 training acquisition sessions in Europe" (Appendix E.5.1). Camelyon17-wilds' ID validation set is drawn from the same slides as training — "Training: 302,436 patches taken from 30 WSIs, with 10 WSIs from each of the 3 hospitals in the training set" against "Validation (ID): 33,560 patches taken from the same 30 WSIs from the training hospitals" (Appendix E.2.1), with patches extracted "in a standard manner, similar to Veeling et al. (2018)" (Appendix E.2.4) and no dedup or near-duplicate check reported across that train/ID-val boundary — and that ID number is exactly the baseline the headline gap is measured against (93.2% ID vs. 70.3% OOD, a 22.9-point gap). The authors name this exact hazard elsewhere in the same appendix — "We do not provide a Test (ID) set, as there is no practical setting in which we would have labels on a uniformly randomly sampled set of patches from a WSI, but no labels on the other patches from the same WSI" — and drop the Test (ID) split over it, while keeping the Validation (ID) split that supplies Table 1's baseline. PovertyMap-wilds is more direct about it: "Yeh et al. (2020) split the ID train/val/test while making sure that the spatial extent of the images between each split never overlap, while we simply take uniformly random splits of the ID data. This means that between our ID train/val/test splits, we may have images that have share some overlapping spatial extent" (Appendix E.8.4) — the authors declining the stricter control their own source dataset used, and saying so.

what's missed

Table 1 and Table 2 do print Camelyon17's 6.4-point OOD std dev in parentheses next to the mean, so it's not hidden — but a reader skimming the headline 70.3% can still miss what that parenthetical implies: per the paper's own Figure 18, ten identically-configured seeds swing from 0.62 to 0.82 OOD test accuracy, a 20-point range from a single held-out hospital that a 3+-replicate mean-and-std-dev pair compresses into one line.

would raise confidence

More than one held-out test hospital for Camelyon17; testing domain-generalization-specific methods (not repurposed subpopulation/domain-adaptation methods) against the domain-generalization datasets; and significance testing on the Table 2 baseline-vs-ERM comparisons rather than eyeballing overlapping std devs.

// bottom line

WILDS's real contribution isn't a new algorithm or a single headline accuracy number — it's an evidence base, built from genuinely real (not synthetic) distribution shifts, showing that off-the-shelf domain-generalization and subpopulation-shift methods mostly don't close the ID-OOD gap and occasionally widen it. That negative result is the paper's teeth, and the authors' own honesty about where the evaluation is thin — one hospital for Camelyon17, baselines run outside their intended regime, five candidate datasets excluded because the shift didn't matter there — is exactly what makes the reported gaps on the other nine datasets easier to trust than a paper that only showed its wins.

// takeaways

What to remember

  1. WILDS packages 10 curated, real-world distribution-shift datasets — tumor patches, camera-trap photos, molecular scaffolds, wheat-field imagery, toxic comments, satellite imagery, product reviews, and source code — spanning domain generalization, subpopulation shift, and hybrids of both, plus an installable package and public leaderboard that standardize how ID and OOD performance are measured.
  2. Standard ERM training shows a real ID-OOD gap on every dataset, from 7.2 points (OGB-MolPCBA) to 36.2 points (CivilComments-wilds) on their respective percentage-based metrics — and mixed-to-test training shows the gap is caused by the shift itself, not by the OOD examples being intrinsically harder.
  3. Off-the-shelf domain-generalization and subpopulation-shift methods (CORAL, IRM, Group DRO) mostly fail to close that gap and sometimes widen it — CivilComments-wilds is the only dataset where the paper's own evaluation concludes any of the three actually improves over ERM (a nominal edge for CORAL on iWildCam2020's OOD column doesn't hold up once the paper's fuller appendix table is checked).
