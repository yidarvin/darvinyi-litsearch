Know Me, Respond to Me: Benchmarking LLMs for Dynamic User Profiling and Personalized Responses at Scale

PersonaMem gives each simulated user a persona whose preferences change over time, buries the evidence across up to 60 sessions and 1M tokens of chat history, and asks 15 LLMs to pick the response that fits the user's current situation. Frontier models land around 50% accuracy on a 4-way multiple choice — and fall below the 25% guessing floor on the hardest type of all — proactively suggesting something new that fits the user's current preferences.

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

Most long-context "memory" benchmarks ask a narrower question than the one that actually matters for a personalized assistant: can the model retrieve a fact it was told once, from somewhere inside a very long transcript? LOCOMO and LongMemEval, two of the three benchmarks the paper compares itself against directly (Table 2), are built this way — third- or first-person question answering over long-term dialogue, with essentially no modeling of a user's preferences changing over the course of the interaction (the paper's own comparison table records "No update" for LOCOMO and at most a single update for LongMemEval, versus up to three for PersonaMem). The third, PrefEval, is closer in spirit — it already tests preference-following rather than plain fact retrieval — but its preferences are likewise static ("No update" in the same table); PersonaMem's distinct contribution is the preferences-that-evolve setting none of the three cover.

Real usage is messier: a user who said “I am a big fan of Italian food, especially pizza” in 2020 may say “I recently found out I'm allergic to gluten, so I've started exploring Mediterranean cuisine” in 2023, and a chatbot that resurfaces the 2020 preference in 2024 is not just wrong on a trivia question — it is failing at personalization itself. The authors frame this as three distinct sub-abilities a chatbot needs: (1) internalize a user's traits and preferences from history, (2) track how those traits evolve over time, and (3) actually use the current state of the profile to generate a response in a new situation, not just recite it back. PersonaMem is built to isolate and score each of these three abilities separately rather than folding them into one generic "long-context QA" number.

// how it works

The method

Everything in PersonaMem is synthetic — personas, histories, conversations, and questions are all machine-generated, not collected from real users or crowdworkers. The pipeline starts from short (one-to-three-sentence) personas sampled from PersonaHub (Ge et al., 2024), then uses GPT-4o to expand each into a five-step curation process (Figure 2): (1) construct a fuller demographic + biographical persona; (2) generate a chronological "general personal history" (career, life events) spanning up to ten years; (3) generate a separate "topic-specific personal history" for each of 15 conversation topics (food, travel, therapy, legal advice, dating, finance, etc.), each with an initial preference, and — for some topics — a later preference update plus the in-narrative reason for the change; (4) expand each topic-specific history segment into a full multi-turn conversation session between the user and a chatbot, with GPT-4o first citing the source event internally and then a self-reflection pass checking for missed events; and (5) topologically sort and concatenate sessions from different topics by their timestamps (preserving causal order within a topic) into one long interaction history. Because sessions only need to be generated once and can then be re-ordered into multiple valid concatenations, the pipeline is deliberately cheap and scalable — the paper reports about $2 per persona-per-topic, independent of the eventual context length.

The benchmark ships in three sizes by re-concatenating different numbers of sessions per persona: 10 sessions (~32k tokens), 20 sessions (~128k tokens), and 60 sessions (~1M tokens), with 15–30 turns per session. In total there are 20 personas and over 180 interaction histories, spanning 15 conversation topics and yielding roughly 6,000 in-situ query–response pairs across 7 query types (Table 1): recall user-shared facts, suggest new ideas, acknowledge the latest preference, track the full preference evolution, revisit the reasons behind a preference update, provide preference-aligned recommendations, and generalize a reason to a new scenario.

Evaluation is cast as 4-way multiple choice: given the interaction history up to a point in time plus a first-person "in-situ" user query, the model must pick the response option that fits the user's current state, out of one correct option and three distractors built from outdated or irrelevant facts/preferences. The main (discriminative) setting shows all four options and asks for a choice plus a brief explanation; a secondary generative setting instead scores each candidate response by the length-normalized joint log-probability the model assigns it, without ever showing the options — but this requires full-vocabulary logits, so it is only run on open-weight models (LLaMA-3.1-70B/8B, DeepSeek-R1-Distill-LLaMA-8B) at the smallest, 10-session/32k-token size. The authors also tried to guard against shortcut answers: they filtered out questions an LLM could already answer correctly (2 of 3 attempts) with zero conversation context, and removed cases where the correct option was trivially identifiable (e.g. conspicuously longer, or lexically overlapping with the question).

To sanity-check quality, the authors ran a small human validation study (§3): three of the paper's own authors — not external crowdworkers — rated 90 randomly sampled query–response pairs (18 topics × 5 questions) on appropriateness, relevance, correctness, and whether the labeled "correct" response was in fact better than all three distractors. Reported agreement was 97.8% appropriate (Gwet's AC1 = 0.928), 95.6% relevant (AC1 = 0.899), 97.8% correct (AC1 = 0.877), and 90.0% best-response (AC1 = 0.560, the weakest of the four, which the authors attribute to that dimension being inherently more subjective).

// what they found

The results

The main evaluation runs 15 LLMs zero-shot on the 128k-token (20-session) version of PersonaMem: GPT-4.1, GPT-4.5, o1, o3-mini, o4-mini, GPT-4o, GPT-4o-mini, Gemini-2.0-Flash, Gemini-2.0-Flash-Lite, Gemini-1.5-Flash, DeepSeek-R1-671B, Llama-4-Maverick, Llama-3.1-405B, Claude-3.7-Sonnet, and Claude-3.5-Haiku. Overall accuracy on the 4-way choice (random guess = 25%) tops out at 52% for GPT-4.5, GPT-4.1, and Gemini-1.5-Flash; o1 reaches 50%; Llama-4-Maverick scores 43%; and the two weakest models, Claude-3.5-Haiku and Claude-3.7-Sonnet, score 30% and 26% respectively — barely above chance. Notably, none of the four reasoning models tested (o1, o3-mini, o4-mini, DeepSeek-R1-671B) beats the strongest non-reasoning models; DeepSeek-R1-671B in fact ties GPT-4o at 45% overall.

Breaking the same 128k-token evaluation down by query type (Figure 3) shows the difficulty is very unevenly distributed. Averaged across all 15 models, "Revisit Reasons Behind Preference Updates" is the easiest type at 72% average accuracy, followed by "Tracking Full Preference Evolution" at 62%. The hardest by a wide margin is "Suggest New Ideas" — proposing something the user hasn't already mentioned — at just 18% average, actually below the 25% random-guess floor. That isn't an artifact of a few weak models dragging an average down: reading the row across, 12 of the 15 models score below 25%, o1 lands exactly on it, and only GPT-4.5 (27%) and Claude-3.7-Sonnet (28%) clear it — and Claude-3.7-Sonnet is simultaneously last on overall accuracy at 26%, so the model that handles the hardest query type best is the one that handles the benchmark worst. "Generalize Reasons to New Scenarios" averages 36%, and "Provide Preference-Aligned Recommendations" and "Acknowledge Latest User Preference" tie as the next-hardest at 43% each (an exact tie at the figure's printed precision). In other words, models are relatively good at recalling what already happened but struggle to generate something new that is consistent with a user's current, evolved state — exactly the gap the benchmark was designed to expose.

Only 4 of the 15 models — Llama-4-Maverick, Gemini-2.0-Flash, Gemini-2.0-Flash-Lite, and Gemini-1.5-Flash — were pushed to the full 60-session/1M-token version, since the rest don't support that context length. Their overall accuracy drops from the 128k setting: Gemini-1.5-Flash falls from 52% to 45%, Gemini-2.0-Flash-Lite from 48% to 38%, Gemini-2.0-Flash from 49% to 37%, and Llama-4-Maverick from 43% to 28% — consistent with the paper's broader claim that longer, more crowded histories make the task harder. Slicing by how many sessions have elapsed since the relevant preference was last mentioned (Figure 4), the paper reports a "lost-in-the-middle" pattern: performance tends to be higher when the relevant information sits in the earliest or most recent sessions of the history than when it's buried in the middle, echoing prior long-context findings (Liu et al., 2024).

Adding external memory helps: for GPT-4o and GPT-4o-mini at the 32k-token scale (§4.4), both a simple RAG setup (top-5 BGE-M3-retrieved messages per question) and Mem0 (an LLM-maintained fact memory, top-5 facts retrieved per question) improve accuracy over the vanilla model on most of the 7 query types, with the largest gains on "Recall User-Shared Facts" and "Generalize Reasons to New Scenarios," and the smallest gains on "Revisit Reasons Behind Preference Updates." The paper reports RAG consistently outperforming Mem0 across most question types — while also noting Mem0 is far more expensive at inference time: in a separate latency study (Appendix E, GPT-4o-mini, 32k context, 589 queries), RAG averaged 0.61 seconds per query versus roughly 150 seconds per query for Mem0. A follow-up experiment (Appendix C, Figure 9) also reports RAG's effect at the larger 128k-token scale for the same two models, with average accuracy gains of +15 points for GPT-4o and +8 points for GPT-4o-mini over their vanilla baselines — but Mem0 is never re-tested beyond 32k, so the RAG-vs-Mem0 comparison itself is only established at the smallest context length.

In the generative setting (§4.5, open-weight models only, 10-session/32k-token version), the paper reports the same qualitative difficulty ordering across query types as the discriminative setting: recalling facts and tracking preference changes are comparatively easy, generating new suggestions and generalizing to new scenarios remain the hardest. Comparing LLaMA-3.1-8B under both settings, the generative version scores higher than the same model's discriminative (multiple-choice) version on 4 of the 7 query types and on the unweighted 7-type average — the authors read this as evidence the model can sometimes produce a reasonable personalized response without needing to discriminate between four explicit candidates, though the comparison inverts on the remaining 3 types (Provide Preference-Aligned Recommendations, Acknowledge Latest User Preference, Generalize Reasons to New Scenarios), where the discriminative/MCQ score is actually higher.

A manual error analysis on 100 GPT-4o failures (Appendix F) attributes 48% of errors to "Lack of Personalization" (the model picks a generically reasonable answer over the more personalized one), 24% to failing to recognize a preference update (picking an outdated option), 14% to format errors (not selecting a valid option at all), 12% to hallucinating a preference the user never stated, and 2% to other causes.

// does it hold up?

The eval read

PersonaMem is entirely synthetic end to end — personas from PersonaHub, personal histories, conversation sessions, and even the multiple-choice distractors are all generated by GPT-4o, with no real user data and no crowdsourced writing anywhere in the pipeline. That is a defensible privacy-motivated design choice the authors state explicitly (Appendix A.1), but it also means every "user" utterance and every distractor inherits GPT-4o's generation style, which raises the standard synthetic-benchmark question of whether the benchmark is really testing personalization ability or partly testing familiarity with GPT-4o's own phrasing conventions. The paper's own results argue against a strong version of that worry — GPT-4o itself lands at a middling 45% overall, behind GPT-4.5, GPT-4.1, and Gemini-1.5-Flash at 52% — but a systematic model-family effect on the easier query types can't be ruled out from what's reported.

The quality-control step is thin relative to the benchmark's size: only 90 of roughly 6,000 query–response pairs (1.5%) were human-validated, and the "human" evaluators were three of the paper's own co-authors rather than independent or blinded annotators (the paper itself flags this: "we recognize that the authors may not be fully impartial annotators," Appendix B footnote). The weakest of the four agreement dimensions — whether the labeled correct answer actually beats all three distractors — came in at 90.0% with only "moderate" inter-rater agreement (Gwet's AC1 = 0.560), which is also the dimension most directly load-bearing for the benchmark's validity as a multiple-choice task; a construction bug that occasionally produces a beatable distractor would show up here, and this is exactly the dimension with the least reliable evidence.

All main-track evaluation is zero-shot, single-pass, discriminative multiple choice against synthetic distractors the same pipeline generated — there's no comparison to a fine-tuned or specialized personalization baseline, and no LLM-as-judge cross-check on the open-ended generative variant (per Appendix A.4, the paper explains that open-ended evaluation is computationally expensive due to the need for an LLM-as-judge on each question–answer pair, a reasonable cost trade-off but one that means the generative setting's validity rests entirely on log-probability scoring against a fixed candidate set the model never sees). The retrieval-augmented comparison is narrower than it first looks: RAG-vs-vanilla is reported at both 32k (Figure 5) and 128k (Appendix C, Figure 9) for GPT-4o and GPT-4o-mini, but Mem0 is only ever tested at the smallest 32k-token size, so the RAG-vs-Mem0 head-to-head holds only there — and neither RAG nor Mem0 is tested at all at the 1M-token scale, where the paper's steepest accuracy drops occur.

What the headline "~50% overall accuracy" number obscures is how unevenly that averages out: it's driven up by near-ceiling performance on recall-style questions (72% average on "Revisit Reasons") and dragged down by near-floor performance on generation-style questions (18% average on "Suggest New Ideas"). The paper doesn't state how the "Overall Accuracy" row is computed, but it is not a simple mean of the 7 per-type numbers shown in Figure 3 — e.g. Gemini-1.5-Flash's 7 per-type scores average to roughly 54%, while its printed Overall Accuracy is 52% — and the query types are far from evenly represented in the dataset to begin with (per Appendix D.1, "Acknowledge Latest User Preferences" alone makes up about 30% of all queries). A reader skimming only the topline 50%-ish numbers would badly undersell how close to random the models are on the benchmark's most demanding, generative query types.

baselines

15 contemporary LLMs (GPT-4.1/4.5/4o/4o-mini, o1/o3-mini/o4-mini, Gemini-1.5/2.0, DeepSeek-R1-671B, Llama-4-Maverick/3.1-405B, Claude-3.5/3.7) plus a 25% random-guess floor; no fine-tuned or specialized personalization baseline is included, so all comparisons are among off-the-shelf zero-shot models.

contamination

Low risk from pretraining-data leakage since every persona, history, and conversation is freshly synthesized (not scraped or reused from an existing corpus); the more relevant risk is a generator-model bias, since GPT-4o both wrote the benchmark and is one of the models scored on it, though GPT-4o does not top the leaderboard.

what's missed

The pooled "overall accuracy" headline hides a >50-point spread between the easiest query type (72% avg) and the hardest (18% avg); RAG/Mem0 memory-augmentation results cover only 2 of 15 models throughout, and the RAG-vs-Mem0 comparison itself only at the smallest 32k-token size — neither is tested at all at 1M tokens.

would raise confidence

Independent/external human validation at a larger sample than 90 of ~6,000 pairs; a non-GPT-4o data-generation pipeline (or an ablation showing generator-model choice doesn't bias which models score well); Mem0 (or comparable retrieval) results beyond 32k tokens, and any retrieval-augmented result at all at the 1M-token scale, where the core accuracy claims live.

// bottom line

PersonaMem is a well-motivated and genuinely useful stress test — it's the first of its peer group to combine multi-session, multi-topic, first-person, timelined preference evolution at up to 1M tokens — but its entirely-synthetic, single-generator construction and thin (author-only, 1.5%-sampled) human validation mean the ~50% headline number should be read as suggestive rather than a fully audited ground truth, and the sharp per-query-type spread underneath it is the more informative signal than the topline average.

// takeaways

What to remember

  1. PersonaMem tests whether LLMs can track a user's evolving preferences and act on the current state, not just retrieve a static fact — 20 personas, 180+ interaction histories, up to 60 sessions/1M tokens, ~6,000 in-situ multiple-choice queries across 7 query types and 15 topics.
  2. Frontier models (GPT-4.5, GPT-4.1, Gemini-1.5-Flash) top out around 52% overall accuracy on a 4-way choice (25% = random); reasoning models (o1, o3-mini, o4-mini, DeepSeek-R1) show no advantage over non-reasoning models on this task.
  3. The gap between skills is stark: models average 72% on recalling why a preference changed but only 18% — below the 25% random-guess floor — on proactively suggesting something new that fits the user's current preferences, and accuracy degrades further as context grows from 128k to 1M tokens.
  4. The entire benchmark — personas, conversations, and distractors — is synthesized by GPT-4o from PersonaHub seed personas, with no real user or crowdworker data; quality was checked by 3 of the paper's own authors on only 90 of ~6,000 pairs, not by independent annotators.
