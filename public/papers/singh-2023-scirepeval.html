SciRepEval: A Multi-Format Benchmark for Scientific Document Representations

SciRepEval packages 24 realistic tasks — classification, regression, proximity ranking, and ad-hoc search — into one scientific-document-embedding benchmark, absorbs SciDocs as a subset, and shows that a single shared embedding per paper caps generalization, while training a separate embedding per task format (control codes or adapters) closes much of that gap.

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

By late 2022, SciDocs — introduced alongside SPECTER (Cohan et al., 2020) — was the standard yardstick for scientific document embeddings, and SciNCL had already become its state of the art. But SciDocs is thin. It has two classification tasks, one recommendation task, and four nearest-neighbor tasks (predicting citations, co-citations, co-views, and co-reads), and this paper's own correlation analysis (Appendix G) finds that across the models it tests, those four nearest-neighbor tasks correlate with each other at over 0.99 — a model that does well on one does well on essentially all four, so together they carry barely more evaluative signal than a single task. The recommendation task fares worse: its test set holds only 1,000 clickthrough events, and propensity weighting means an even smaller number of examples end up deciding the score, so SciRepEval drops it outright rather than keep it. SciDocs also can't be used to study training itself — "the tasks in SciDocs were constructed to be used only for evaluation, and have few-enough samples that training on SciDocs is impractical," so a benchmark built only from it can check an embedding trained elsewhere but can't drive research into how multi-task training changes generalization.

General-purpose text-embedding benchmarks like MTEB (Muennighoff et al., 2022) cover more task shapes, but aren't tuned to scientific text — and the gap this paper documents between domain-specific and general embeddings turns out to be real but modest: the strongest scientific-domain vanilla baselines it tests, SciNCL (68.8 average) and the new SPECTER2 Base (69.1), only edge out the best general-purpose baseline it tries, OpenAI's Ada-002 (67.8), by about a point. SciRepEval's answer to both gaps is to keep SciDocs's six inherited tasks as a subset while adding 18 more spanning four formats — classification, regression, proximity, and ad-hoc search — with enough of them large enough to train on that the paper can also ask whether multi-task training, and specifically task-format-specialized training, actually helps.

// how it works

The method

SciRepEval assembles 24 tasks across four formats meant to mirror how paper embeddings actually get used: classification (topic and disease-area labeling), regression (predicting citation counts, publication year, peer-review scores, author h-index, tweet mentions), proximity (nearest-neighbor style ranking against a query paper — citations, co-citations, author disambiguation, reviewer matching, RELISH document recommendation), and ad-hoc search (ranking candidates against a short text query — a new 500K-clickthrough Semantic Scholar Search dataset, plus NFCorpus and TREC-CoVID). Eight of the largest tasks — each with 200K+ training instances, unevenly spread across the four formats (two classification, two regression, three proximity, one search) — are designated in-train; the other 16 are held out purely for evaluation. Of the 24 tasks, 8 are introduced for the first time (six of the eight in-train tasks, plus Peer Review Score and h-Index of Authors among the out-of-train set); the rest are pulled in from prior work largely unchanged — SciDocs's four nearest-neighbor tasks and two classification tasks, S2AND for author disambiguation, RELISH, NFCorpus, TREC-CoVID, and a Paper-Reviewer Matching set pooled from three earlier reviewer-assignment studies.

The paper's second contribution is a training recipe for that benchmark. The standard approach (SPECTER, SciNCL) fine-tunes a transformer on citation triplets to produce one embedding per document, then reuses that single embedding for every downstream task. SciRepEval's authors hypothesize that this single-vector bottleneck is exactly what limits cross-format generalization — an embedding space that gives a clean linear classifier need not also place true nearest neighbors close together (Figure 1) — so they pre-fine-tune a SPECTER2 Base model (built like SPECTER but on roughly 10x more citation-triplet data spanning 23 fields of study, versus the two domains that dominate SPECTER's original training set) jointly on all 8 in-train tasks, using a task-appropriate loss per format: cross-entropy for classification, mean-squared error for regression, and a triplet-margin loss for proximity and search, comparing a query embedding's distance to a positive versus a negative candidate:

Ltriplet = max{ d(QE, P+E) − d(QE, P−E) + ϵ, 0 } // d = Euclidean distance; ϵ = margin hyperparameter (set to 1)

To get a distinct embedding per task format out of that one shared model, the paper compares two mechanisms (Figure 1): prepending a learned control-code token — [CLF], [RGN], [PRX], or [QRY] — to the input and reading off that token's final-layer representation ("MTL CTRL"), versus attaching a separate lightweight adapter module per format on top of a frozen base encoder, either as independently-trained Adapters, Adapter Fusion (a two-step process that adds fusion layers atop pre-trained per-format adapters), or BERT PALs (which adds attention layers and trains the whole network). A "MTL CLS" baseline — the same multi-task pre-fine-tuning, but producing one shared embedding rather than one per format — isolates whether format-specialization itself is doing the work, versus multi-task training alone.

// what they found

The results

Among the vanilla (not further trained) transformer baselines, every general-purpose embedding model beats SciBERT (58.0 average) outright — E5-base-v2 (67.2), MPNet (67.7), Instructor-base (64.8), and Ada-002 (67.8) all land in the high-60s — but the domain-specific SciNCL (68.8) and the new SPECTER2 Base (69.1, trained on 10x more citation data across 23 fields of study) edge past the best general-purpose baseline tested — plain SPECTER (67.5) does not, landing below both Ada-002 and MPNet. Multi-task pre-fine-tuning on the 8 in-train tasks helps only once it stops sharing one embedding across formats: MTL CLS reaches 69.0 average — on par with (if anything a hair behind) SPECTER2 Base's 69.1, despite being trained directly on in-domain SciRepEval data — while every format-specialized variant does better: MTL CTRL reaches 70.4, Adapters and Adapter Fusion both reach 70.9 (tied, and per the paper's ANOVA + Tukey HSD test at α = 0.05, each underlined as not significantly different from the best), and an ensemble averaging the MTL CTRL and Adapter embeddings reaches the paper's best result, 71.2 average (62.9 in-train / 74.1 out-of-train) — though that same significance test underlines Adapters and Fusion (both 70.9) as statistically tied with it on Out-of-Train and Average; on In-Train, by contrast, the ensemble's 62.9 is the only underlined cell, so no rival is marked as statistically tied with it there. Table 3's cross-task analysis shows the mechanism directly: sampling one in-train and one out-of-train task per format and scoring each against all four control codes, the code trained on a task's own format wins for classification, regression, and search embeddings in both splits (out-of-train proximity is the one case where the query-format code edges it out numerically, 45.2 vs. 45.1, though the paper still calls out the proximity code as matching its diagonal-pattern claim).

Beyond SciRepEval itself, the format-partitioned SPECTER2 models set a new result on Medić & Šnajder's separate MDCR citation-recommendation benchmark, where the paper's own baseline (BM25) had previously beaten neural models outright:

// does it hold up?

The eval read

SciRepEval's internal comparisons are careful: five seeded runs per trained model, reported with standard deviations, scored against an ANOVA + Tukey HSD test at α = 0.05 that explicitly marks which results aren't statistically distinguishable from the best (the underlines in Table 2). That rigor makes the headline margin easy to see for what it is — the best system, Adapters + MTL CTRL at 71.2 average, is only 0.3 points ahead of two variants (Adapters, Fusion, both 70.9) that the paper's own test says are statistically tied with it. It also exposes some looseness between the paper's prose and its own tables. The results section states that every multi-representation approach beats the single-embedding MTL CLS baseline "by 1.4 to 2 points" — but re-deriving the deltas from Table 2's own Average column gives PALs only +0.9 (below that range) and the full ensemble +2.2 (above it). Appendix E has a similar slip: it says the ensemble model is "equivalent or better than BM25 on all the scientific domains" apart from Geology and History, but Table 10's own per-field MAP and Recall@5 numbers show the ensemble ahead of BM25 on both metrics in both of those supposedly-exceptional fields (Geology: 34.1 vs. 33.1 MAP, 28.4 vs. 28.0 R@5; History: 40.3 vs. 38.1 MAP, 34.7 vs. 32.9 R@5) — neither table cell backs up the stated exception. A third slip sits in the same discussion: the results section says “the adapter variants are better than MTL CTRL overall, and result in an improvement of up to 0.8 points on the out-of-train tasks with the fusion adapters performing the best.” Table 2 contradicts both halves. PALs is an adapter variant by the table’s own caption, and it trails MTL CTRL on all three columns (61.8/72.6/69.9 against 62.4/73.1/70.4). And Fusion does not perform best — it ties plain Adapters at every reported digit (73.9 out-of-train, 70.9 average) while costing 22M parameters per format against Adapters’ 1M (Table 4), which makes it the worse of the two on the paper’s own efficiency argument.

The one leakage risk the paper names directly is the MDCR overlap (23% of candidate papers, not links, already in SPECTER2's pre-training data) — a genuinely useful disclosure, left unfactored out of the headline number. It does not extend the same scrutiny to SciRepEval's own split: several out-of-train test sets (NFCorpus and TREC-CoVID draw from PubMed/CORD-19, RELISH from PubMed) are pooled from the same broad scientific-literature ecosystem that feeds the in-train Search and citation datasets, and the paper does not report a document-level overlap audit between its own in-train and out-of-train pools the way it does for MDCR.

baselines

Fair among the models tested — SciBERT, SPECTER, SciNCL, ASPIRE (proximity-only, Appendix C), plus four late-2022 general-purpose embedders (E5-v2, MPNet, Instructor, Ada-002) — and internally validated with 5 seeded runs and a significance test most benchmark papers skip. But the sweep was only refreshed as far as the paper's own camera-ready snapshot — E5-v2, Instructor, and Ada-002 (all released Dec. 2022) postdate the Nov. 2022 v1 preprint, so the baselines aren't frozen at submission — and nothing here has been re-run against embedding models released since.

contamination

The MDCR ~23%-paper-overlap caveat is disclosed but not corrected for. No equivalent overlap check is reported between SciRepEval's own in-train corpora (Search, citation triplets) and its out-of-train test pools drawn from the same scientific-literature ecosystem (PubMed, CORD-19).

what's missed

The "1.4 to 2 points" and "equivalent or better...apart from Geology and History" claims don't match their own tables (see above). Figure 2's correlation heatmap shows five of the six SciDocs-derived tasks (the four nearest-neighbor tasks plus SciDocs MeSH Diseases) still correlate near 1.0 with each other inside the larger suite — SciDocs MAG sits apart — so the 24-task "average" isn't an average of 24 independent signals. Task-partitioning is validated only against random partitions and pairwise-format combinations, not a systematic search.

would raise confidence

A document-overlap audit between in-train and out-of-train pools; re-running the baseline sweep against post-2022 embedding models; and the downstream validation the authors themselves propose as future work — an online A/B test checking whether higher SciRepEval scores translate into better real search or recommendation outcomes.

// bottom line

SciRepEval is a genuine fix to SciDocs's narrowness, and the multi-format training result is real and reproduces across two additional base models (Appendix D): learning an embedding per task format beats learning one shared multi-task embedding, consistently. But the paper's own numbers don't always back its own prose, and its most consequential claim — that a better SciRepEval score means a better embedding in practice — is exactly the one the authors say they haven't tested yet.

// takeaways

What to remember

  1. A single embedding per document is the bottleneck, not the lack of multi-task training: simple multi-task learning across all 8 in-train tasks (MTL CLS, 69.0 average) lands on par with — if anything a hair behind — not multi-tasking at all (SPECTER2 Base, 69.1) — but swapping in a control-code or adapter embedding per task format lifts every variant above MTL CLS, to 69.9–71.2, and the best result combines a control-code model with an adapter model (Adapters + MTL CTRL, 71.2).
  2. SciRepEval keeps SciDocs's six tasks as a subset — dropping only its noisy 1,000-example recommendation task — and adds 18 more across regression, search, and realistic proximity settings like author disambiguation and reviewer matching, for 24 tasks total (8 new), 8 of them large enough to train on.
  3. The paper is candid about its own biggest open questions: it flags the ~23% document overlap between the MDCR benchmark and SPECTER2's pre-training data as an unfactored transductive advantage, and lists confirming its benchmark gains in a live, A/B-tested application as future work rather than something it has shown.
