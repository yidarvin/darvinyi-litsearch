MTEB: Massive Text Embedding Benchmark

Eight embedding tasks, 58 datasets, 112 languages, one harness: MTEB replaces the field's habit of judging a new embedding model on whichever single benchmark flatters it — usually semantic textual similarity — with a fixed evaluation spanning classification, clustering, pair classification, reranking, retrieval, STS, summarization and bitext mining. Across close to 5,000 experiments on 33 models, from Glove to OpenAI's Embeddings API to a 7.1B-parameter SGPT, the headline finding is negative: no single model wins every task, and the benchmark's own "Avg" column quietly weights that verdict toward whichever tasks happen to have the most curated datasets.

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

By 2022 there were dozens of text embedding models — Glove-style word vectors, BERT variants, contrastively fine-tuned SimCSE and SBERT, T5-based ST5 and GTR, decoder-based SGPT, OpenAI's paid Embeddings API — but no shared way to say which one was actually good. The authors point to a specific narrowness: SimCSE and SBERT are evaluated solely on semantic textual similarity (STS) and classification, leaving open whether a model that wins on STS transfers to clustering or reranking at all. That's not a hypothetical gap — the paper cites prior evidence that STS performance poorly correlates with other real-world use cases. Earlier attempts at breadth existed but each covered only part of the space: SentEval (2018) aggregates STS-style datasets but predates transformer embeddings and has no retrieval or clustering tasks; USEB is mostly reranking and skips retrieval and classification; BEIR became the standard for zero-shot retrieval but is retrieval-only. On top of the missing task coverage, each of these tools implements its own evaluation pipeline — different preprocessing, different hyperparameters — so a reported improvement might just be a favorable eval harness rather than a better embedding. The practical consequence the authors describe is "blind" reuse: a model tuned and validated on STS gets deployed for search or clustering with no evidence it transfers.

// how it works

The method

MTEB doesn't fine-tune anything — it plugs a frozen embedding model into eight fixed evaluation protocols, one per task type, and reports each protocol's own standard metric. Classification embeds a train and test split, then trains a logistic regression probe (max 100 iterations) on the train embeddings and scores it on the test set (main metric: accuracy). Clustering runs mini-batch k-means (k = number of gold labels) over the embedded texts and scores the assignment with V-measure, which is invariant to label permutation. Pair Classification embeds two texts, computes their cosine similarity, and picks the best binary threshold, reporting the resulting average precision. Reranking embeds a query and a mixed list of relevant/irrelevant references, ranks the references by cosine similarity to the query, and averages MAP across queries. Retrieval reuses BEIR's protocol directly — embed queries and corpus, rank by cosine similarity, score nDCG@10. STS and Summarization both score the Spearman correlation between cosine similarity and a human-labeled ground truth (for summarization, each machine-written summary is matched to its closest human reference). Bitext Mining finds the closest cross-lingual sentence pair by cosine similarity and scores F1. Because every task shares this "embed once, evaluate with a fixed and simple downstream procedure" contract, a score difference between two models is attributable to the embeddings themselves rather than to incompatible eval code — that consistency is the paper's actual methodological contribution, not any single dataset.

The 58 datasets split into 12 Classification, 11 Clustering, 3 Pair Classification, 4 Reranking, 15 Retrieval, 10 STS and 1 Summarization dataset in the English-only subset (56 datasets), plus 2 further Bitext Mining datasets (BUCC, Tatoeba) that exist only in the multilingual tables. Ten of the 58 datasets are multilingual, spanning all 112 languages, but multilinguality is uneven across tasks — bitext mining, classification and STS get multilingual coverage; retrieval and clustering stay English-only. The benchmark evaluates 33 models in total: 4 self-supervised (Glove, Komninos, BERT, unsupervised SimCSE) and 29 supervised/contrastively-tuned models spanning encoder architectures (SimCSE-sup, coCondenser, Contriever, SPECTER, LaBSE, MiniLM, MPNet), a decoder-based family (SGPT, in nli- and msmarco-tuned variants, up to a 7.1B-parameter BLOOM backbone, plus OpenAI's cpt-text-based Ada Similarity API, benchmarked through the Embeddings endpoint), T5-encoder families (GTR and ST5, Base through XXL) and one non-transformer baseline (LASER2).

The results

The abstract's claim — "no particular text embedding method dominates across all tasks" — holds up cell by cell in Table 1. Five different models win the seven English task-type columns: ST5-XXL tops Classification (73.42 accuracy), Clustering (43.71 V-measure, edging out MPNet's 43.69), STS (82.63 Spearman) and the overall dataset-weighted Avg (59.51); GTR-XL tops Pair Classification (86.13 AP, ahead of GTR-XXL's 86.12 and ST5-XL's 86.06 by a hair); MPNet tops Reranking (59.36 MAP); SGPT-5.8B-msmarco tops Retrieval (50.25 nDCG@10); and multilingual MPNet tops Summarization (31.57 Spearman). The model with the best average score, ST5-XXL, is not the best model on four of the seven tasks. The paper frames this as a "bifurcation": models contrastively fine-tuned on NLI for STS (SimCSE, ST5, SGPT-nli) do markedly worse at retrieval, which is an asymmetric query-vs-document task, while models tuned on MS MARCO for retrieval (GTR, SGPT-msmarco) underperform at STS, a symmetric similarity task. On QuoraRetrieval — flagged by the authors as an unusually symmetric retrieval dataset — SGPT-5.8B-nli actually beats SGPT-5.8B-msmarco, the reverse of the pattern everywhere else, which supports that the split is about task symmetry rather than one model family being categorically better.

Scaling behavior (Figure 2) differs by fine-tuning recipe: the paper's own framing is that GTR and ST5 performance "strongly correlates" with model size, and that correlation isn't perfectly monotone — 6 of the 12 GTR/ST5 task curves dip somewhere along the scale, with ST5 Pair Classification actually ending lower than it starts (85.17 at Base vs 85.06 at XXL); the smallest SGPT variant lags similarly-sized GTR/ST5 models on five of the six panels shown, edging past ST5-Base only on Retrieval (37.04 vs 33.63 nDCG@10) — the authors attribute the general SGPT gap to its bias-only fine-tuning (under 0.1% of parameters trained), which only catches up once the number of tunable bias parameters grows with scale. Efficiency (embedding speed on STS15, one A100 80GB) resolves the 33 models into three practical clusters: word embeddings (Glove) for maximum speed at reasonable quality; a slow, high-performance cluster (GTR-XXL, ST5-XXL, SGPT-5.8B) for maximum score, with SGPT-5.8B's added cost of 16.4 kB embeddings (versus 1.2 kB for Glove/Komninos) to store; and a fine-tuned mid-tier (MPNet, MiniLM) that gets most of the top cluster's quality at far higher throughput. On multilinguality, LaBSE dominates bitext mining across a wide language spread while LASER2 is high-variance; multilingual MPNet is the strongest all-round multilingual classifier/STS model, narrowly ahead of the faster multilingual MiniLM; and SGPT-BLOOM-7.1B leads specifically on languages BLOOM was heavily pretrained on (Chinese, French, Portuguese, Hindi) without being much ahead of the far cheaper MPNet overall.

// does it hold up?

The eval read

The most consequential design choice in MTEB is one most readers never notice: the single headline "Avg." number in Table 1 is a plain mean over all 56 English-subset dataset scores, not an equal blend of the 7 task types. Re-deriving it row by row confirms this exactly — e.g. ST5-XXL's printed 59.51 matches (73.42·12 + 43.71·11 + 85.06·3 + 56.43·4 + 42.24·15 + 82.63·10 + 30.08·1)/56 to the third decimal, not any unweighted average of the 7 task columns. That means Retrieval (15 datasets) and Classification (12) together supply nearly half the average's weight, while Summarization supplies 1/56th. The authors name this themselves as "Limitation 2: Task imbalance," which is honest, but naming a bias in an appendix doesn't remove it from the number every downstream paper quotes as "MTEB score." The paper's own correlation study makes the stakes concrete: Figure 6b (task correlation, not reproduced from every angle here) shows Summarization correlates weakly or negatively with the other six tasks (Class. 3, Clust. −4, PairClass. 8, Rerank. −8, Retr. −16, STS 0), meaning a model's summarization ability is close to independent information — yet the aggregate treats every one of the 56 datasets as interchangeable evidence about "how good is this embedding."

Contamination gets a similarly candid but unresolved treatment. The authors observe that MPNet and MiniLM "perform far better than bigger models" on SciDocsRR reranking, and attribute it directly to "parts of SciDocsRR being included in their training data" — then state plainly: "we ignore overlap of MTEB datasets with model training datasets in MTEB scores. As long as enough datasets are averaged, we believe these effects to be insignificant." That is an assumption, not a measurement, and the SciDocsRR case is itself evidence the assumption can fail for a specific dataset/model pair even if it washes out in a 56-dataset average. Baseline coverage, by contrast, is a real strength for a 2022 paper: 33 models spanning a decade of embedding technique (Glove, 2014-era word vectors) up through a commercial API (OpenAI Ada Similarity) and a 7.1B-parameter decoder (SGPT-BLOOM), evaluated identically. But breadth of models isn't breadth of fair comparison — SGPT-nli and SGPT-msmarco are the same architecture with different fine-tuning corpora, and the STS/Retrieval "bifurcation" the paper documents is largely a byproduct of what each model was tuned to score well on, not a difference in underlying embedding quality. A single Avg number cannot separate "generally better embeddings" from "fine-tuned for the task being measured."

baselines

Broad and appropriately current for 2022 — 33 models from Glove to a commercial API to a 7.1B-parameter decoder, all scored identically. But the comparison conflates architecture with fine-tuning objective: SGPT-nli vs. SGPT-msmarco is the same backbone with a different training corpus, so the "no model wins everywhere" finding partly reflects that models were tuned for different tasks, not that embedding quality is fundamentally multi-dimensional.

contamination

Explicitly not checked. The paper states it "ignores overlap of MTEB datasets with model training datasets," then in the same section attributes MPNet/MiniLM's outsized SciDocsRR reranking lead to exactly that overlap — an admitted blind spot the authors caught once by inspection, not one they audited systematically across all 56 datasets.

what's missed

The Avg. column is a 56-dataset mean, not a 7-task blend, so Retrieval/Classification/Clustering (38 of 56 datasets) dominate it while Summarization (1 dataset, near-zero correlation with the rest per Figure 4) barely moves it. Retrieval and Clustering are also English-only despite the 112-language headline, and the paper's own Limitations section separately flags no long-document, code, or multimodal embedding coverage.

would raise confidence

A task-type-normalized aggregate (median or equal-weighted mean across the 7 tasks, not the 56 datasets) instead of — or alongside — the current Avg.; a dedicated per-dataset contamination audit given the one documented SciDocsRR instance; and multilingual Retrieval and Clustering datasets to match the coverage that bitext mining, classification and STS already have — the only three of MTEB's eight task types with multilingual data (pair classification, reranking and summarization are English-only too).

// bottom line

MTEB earns its place as the reference design for embedding evaluation: it replaced STS-only vibes-checking with a fixed, reproducible 8-task harness, and its central finding — no model wins every task — survives scaling curves, an efficiency frontier, and a multilingual breakdown, not just one table. Its most durable weakness is one the authors name but don't fix: "MTEB score" is a mean over 56 unevenly distributed datasets rather than an equal blend of the 7 task types those datasets span, and train/test contamination is assumed away rather than measured, with the paper's own SciDocsRR example showing that assumption can be wrong for a specific dataset.

// takeaways

What to remember

  1. MTEB unifies 8 embedding task types (Classification, Clustering, Pair Classification, Reranking, Retrieval, STS, Summarization, Bitext Mining — 58 datasets, 112 languages) into one harness where every task shares a fixed, simple downstream protocol, so a score difference reflects the embeddings rather than incompatible eval code.
  2. Benchmarking 33 models shows no architecture or fine-tuning recipe wins every task: ST5-XXL leads Classification, Clustering, STS and the overall Avg, GTR-XL leads Pair Classification, MPNet leads Reranking, SGPT-5.8B-msmarco leads Retrieval, and multilingual MPNet leads Summarization — direct evidence for "the field has yet to converge on a universal text embedding method."
  3. The benchmark's single "Avg." number is a mean over 56 unevenly distributed datasets (15 Retrieval, 1 Summarization) rather than an equal blend of the 7 task types — a limitation the authors name but don't correct — so a paper claiming a new "MTEB SOTA" can really mean "best at whichever tasks have the most datasets," and MTEB's contamination handling is an unverified assumption, not a measurement.
