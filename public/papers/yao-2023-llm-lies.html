LLM Lies: Hallucinations are not Bugs, but Features as Adversarial Examples

Vicuna-7B and LLaMA2-7B-chat can be steered, by gradient-guided token replacement, to state a specific fabrication chosen in advance — either from a lightly edited real question or from a string of tokens that mean nothing to a human. The authors prove an idealized attention block can be pushed to any output at all, and argue this makes hallucination look less like a training-data artifact and more like another view of an adversarial example.

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

By late 2023 the dominant explanations for LLM hallucination were all about training data: models memorize and overfit their corpus, or they exploit a corpus-frequency heuristic — fabricating an answer by pattern-matching to whatever text looked similar during pretraining. Cited work in this vein includes SelfCheckGPT's black-box detection framing, McKenna et al.'s study of hallucination sources in inference tasks, and Feldman et al.'s tagged-context-prompt mitigation. Under that account, hallucination is a data problem: fix the training distribution, reduce the corpus noise, and the fabrications should shrink.

This paper argues that account is incomplete, and does it by construction rather than by argument: it shows that a prompt built from random tokens with no relationship whatsoever to the training distribution can still make Vicuna-7B produce a specific, coherent, pre-chosen fabrication. If hallucination can be triggered entirely outside the training distribution, then "the model is regurgitating something data-adjacent" cannot be the whole story. The paper's own framing: these phenomena "consistently reveal that hallucinations may be another view of adversarial examples, as a fundamental characteristic of LLMs" — an analogy to Goodfellow et al.'s adversarial examples in discriminative models, and more specifically to Ilyas et al.'s 2019 argument that adversarial examples are "not bugs, they are features" of the model, not artifacts of the training set. That paper's title is the direct model for this one's.

// how it works

The method

The paper has a theoretical half and an empirical half, and they answer different questions. The theoretical half (Theorem 1) asks: can a transformer's attention mechanism be forced to produce an arbitrary output at all? The authors analyze a single attention block operating in the continuous embedding space. Given an input sequence embedded as e1,…,el, they show that for any target value Vt = WVet at any output position, there exists a continuous perturbation Δ = {δx1,…,δxl} of the input embeddings — specifically δxj = et − exj — that makes the attention block's output exactly equal that target value, because the attention weights Aij always sum to 1 over j. The proof is a direct algebraic substitution, not an approximation result, but it is stated for one attention block with unconstrained continuous perturbation of the entire input — a much more permissive setting than an attacker actually has (a discrete vocabulary, and in practice only a handful of token positions to change).

The empirical half turns that theorem into something a real attacker could use. Since the token space is discrete, the authors propose a gradient-based token replacement strategy: for each position i in the prompt, compute a first-order estimate of how much replacing token xi with a candidate τ from the vocabulary would increase the log-likelihood of the target (hallucinatory) response ỹ, keep the top-k candidates per position, sample a batch of B single-token-swap candidates, and greedily pick the swap that maximizes log p(ỹ|x̃). This is the same gradient-guided-swap idea used in prior universal-adversarial-trigger and HotFlip-style attacks on discriminative and generative NLP models.

Ci = Topk ( [eadv − exi]T ∇exi log p(ỹ|x) ) // candidate replacement tokens at position i, ranked by first-order effect on the target log-likelihood

Two attack modes reuse this same swap procedure from opposite starting points. The weak semantic attack starts from a real, semantically coherent question and constrains itself to replacing only a few tokens (bounding |x̃ − x| ≤ ε), so the prompt still reads as a mangled but recognizable version of the original question. The OoD (out-of-distribution) attack starts from l tokens sampled at random and applies no semantic constraint at all — the final prompt is meaningless to a human reader. Both are optimized to make the target model output the same pre-chosen hallucinatory sentence ỹ. To generate targets, the authors build a small hallucination dataset from Wikipedia-derived common-sense QA pairs: they take a question x with a verified correct answer f(x), then fabricate ỹ by swapping the subject, predicate, or object (e.g. "Joe Biden … 2020" becomes the target "Donald Trump … 2020").

// what they found

The results

All experiments attack only two open-source 2023-era 7B chat models: Vicuna-7B and LLaMA2-7B-chat. (GPT-3.5 and PaLM are named only as context in the abstract's opening sentence, PaLM again in the introduction, and GPT-4 appears only as a cited reference — none of them is ever attacked or evaluated in this paper.) Success is judged by human review: a human checks whether the model's response to the attacked prompt matches the pre-defined target hallucination.

Attack success rate (Table 1, human-judged, full pre-defined sentence match). Against Vicuna-7B, the weak semantic attack succeeds 92.31% of the time and the OoD attack 80.77% of the time. Against LLaMA2-7B-chat, the same attacks succeed 53.85% and 30.77% of the time respectively — noticeably lower, but still far from zero for a model responding to strings that carry no semantic content at all.

A separate, smaller-granularity experiment probes something different: not whether a full hallucinatory sentence can be elicited, but whether continuous perturbation of a randomly-initialized prompt's embeddings can force Vicuna-7B to output an arbitrary single token anywhere in its ~32k-token vocabulary. Across the whole token table, 99.86% of tokens were successfully forced as output, with only 0.14% failing (mostly rare tokens such as stray symbols and uncommon Unicode glyphs). This is direct empirical support for Theorem 1's claim about single-token control, and it is a distinct measurement from Table 1's sentence-level attack success rate — the two percentages should not be read as the same quantity at different precision.

An ablation on LLaMA2-7B-chat (Table 4) varies the length of the random OoD initialization: at 10 tokens the attack succeeds 23.08% of the time, at 20 tokens 30.77% (matching Table 1's OoD/LLaMA2 cell, which uses the same 20-token default from the paper's hyperparameter appendix), and at 30 tokens 65.38% — a jump the paper quantifies as "34.6%" (65.38 − 30.77 = 34.61, which rounds to that figure). Longer OoD prompts simply give the optimizer more token positions to search over.

Finally, the authors test a lightweight defense: since the raw, un-attacked prompt tends to produce a low-entropy first-token distribution (the top token dominates), while both attack types produce noticeably higher-entropy first-token distributions, they propose refusing to answer whenever the first token's prediction entropy exceeds a threshold. At a threshold of 1.6, all raw prompts are still answered normally, while 46.1% of OoD-attacked prompts and 61.5% of weak-semantic-attacked prompts get refused.

// does it hold up?

The eval read

The central question the paper invites but does not fully resolve: does an optimizer-found prompt tell us anything about hallucination as it occurs naturally? A gradient-guided search with a top-k candidate pool, a sample budget of B=1024, and up to 1,000 iterations is a large search over a large space — and it is a general property of high-capacity models that almost any specific output can be reached by some sufficiently adversarial input, adversarial examples in vision being the canonical case. Showing that such a search can find a trigger for a chosen fabrication demonstrates that the model's decision surface is not robust to worst-case input search; it does not by itself demonstrate that hallucinations occurring in ordinary use share a cause with this adversarially-found trigger. The paper's own hedge — hallucination "may be another view of adversarial examples" — is more careful than its title, and the page follows the hedge, not the title.

Theorem 1 is also narrower than the paper's rhetorical use of it suggests. It proves that a single attention block, given unconstrained continuous perturbation of every position's embedding, can be forced to output any value — an algebraic fact about softmax attention weights summing to 1, not a statement about a full stacked, layer-normed, residual transformer under a discrete, budget-constrained token search. The gap between the theorem's idealized regime and the paper's own practical numbers is visible in the data itself: if hallucination-by-perturbation were simply "not a bug, but a characteristic" in the strong sense the title implies, one might expect discrete attacks to succeed close to universally; instead LLaMA2-7B-chat resists the OoD attack 69.23% of the time (100 − 30.77%). The theorem explains why perturbation-driven hallucination is possible in principle; it does not explain the specific, well-below-100% success rates the discrete attack actually achieves.

The proposed entropy-threshold defense is evaluated only against the paper's own two attacks, on the same two models the attacks were tuned against — there is no adaptive-attacker experiment (e.g. an attacker who penalizes low output-entropy inside the optimization objective, which the paper's own token-replacement scoring function could accommodate directly). "Simple yet effective" defenses evaluated only on their own attacker's static-threshold assumption are a known pattern in the adversarial-robustness literature for not holding up once the attacker adapts; the paper does not test that case.

The paper's own plot is not fully consistent with the prose it accompanies, either. Reading Figure 4(b) at its own marked threshold (the dash-dot line at 1.5) puts the OoD-attack curve at roughly 30% recall (≈70% refused) and the weak-semantic curve at roughly 54% recall (≈46% refused) — the reverse ordering from the text quoted above, which reports 46.1% of OoD prompts and 61.5% of weak-semantic prompts refused. The page states the prose numbers because they are what the paper prints as text, but a reader relying on the figure alone would come away with the opposite ranking of which attack the defense catches more often.

Model coverage is thin: the entire empirical claim rests on two 7B open-weight, 2023-vintage chat models. The paper does not test any proprietary model (despite naming GPT-3.5 and PaLM in its own framing and citing the GPT-4 report as background), any larger open model, or any post-2023 alignment/safety-tuned model — all of which could plausibly have very different first-token entropy profiles, undermining the generality the "fundamental characteristic of LLMs" framing implies.

baselines

No baseline attack method is compared against — the paper reports its own gradient-based token replacement against no other trigger-search method (e.g. a universal-adversarial-triggers-style search — a reference to that work sat in the v1 bibliography but was dropped by v3), so there is no sense of whether 92.31%/80.77%/53.85%/30.77% is a strong or weak result relative to prior discrete-token attacks.

contamination

Not applicable in the usual sense — the "dataset" is a small set of fabricated QA targets the authors construct themselves from Wikipedia facts, not a held-out benchmark that could leak into training. The paper never states its size (the reported rates are each consistent with a denominator as small as 26, though nothing in the text confirms that count).

what's missed

The gap between "an idealized attention block can be forced to any output" (proven) and "hallucination is not a bug but a characteristic" (claimed) is never closed quantitatively; the discrete attack's well-below-100% success rates are themselves evidence against the strongest reading of that claim.

would raise confidence

An adaptive attack against the entropy defense; results on at least one larger or safety-tuned model; a baseline discrete-attack method for comparison; and a check of whether the entropy signal that catches these gradient-optimized triggers also catches hallucinations that occur without any adversarial search.

// bottom line

This is a genuine attack-and-defense paper wearing an analysis paper's title. The experiments convincingly show that two specific 7B chat models can be driven, via gradient-guided token search, to state a chosen fabrication — from either a mangled real question or from meaningless random tokens — and that a cheap entropy check catches a majority but not all of those triggers at a threshold that leaves ordinary prompts untouched. What it does not show is that this optimizer-found vulnerability explains why models hallucinate when nobody is attacking them; the paper's own careful wording ("may be another view") says less than its title does, and the page should be read accordingly.

// takeaways

What to remember

  1. Gradient-guided token replacement can drive Vicuna-7B to a pre-chosen fabrication 92.31% of the time from a lightly edited real question and 80.77% of the time from meaningless random tokens; LLaMA2-7B-chat resists more (53.85% / 30.77%) but is not immune.
  2. Theorem 1 proves an idealized, single attention block can be perturbed in continuous embedding space to output any value — algebraically true, but a stronger regime than the discrete, budget-constrained attack the paper actually runs, and the paper's own well-below-100% success rates reflect that gap.
  3. The paper's own claim is hedged — hallucination "may be another view of adversarial examples" — which is weaker than its title and is the reading the eval-rigor section holds it to; a simple entropy-threshold defense catches 46.1%–61.5% of the attacked prompts tested here but was never checked against an adaptive attacker.
