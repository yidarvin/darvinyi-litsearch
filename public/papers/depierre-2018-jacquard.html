Jacquard: A Large Scale Dataset for Robotic Grasp Detection

A pyBullet-and-Blender pipeline drops ShapeNet CAD models into simulated scenes to generate 1.1 million labeled grasp rectangles across 11k objects — 46–137× the Cornell Grasping Dataset depending on which axis you count — plus a new evaluation criterion, simulated grasp trial (SGT), that scores a predicted grasp by physically replaying it rather than matching a rectangle.

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

Training a CNN to predict where a parallel-plate gripper should grasp an object needs a large set of labeled grasp locations, and in 2018 every practical way to get those labels had a real cost. Human labeling — the Cornell Grasping Dataset — is accurate but small: 240 objects, 8,019 hand-drawn grasp rectangles, and (the paper's own numbers disagree with each other by a little here — more on that below) somewhere around 885–1,035 RGB-D images, because a person has to draw every rectangle by hand and can be biased toward grasps that are easy for a human hand rather than a parallel-plate gripper. Physical-trial datasets scale further but cost robot time: Levine et al. logged 800k grasp attempts using 14 arms running for two months, and Pinto & Gupta collected 50k trials with a single Baxter — both still need a person to reposition the object between attempts, and both log only one grasp annotation per image even though a single object usually supports many valid grasps. Synthetic alternatives already existed too: Dex-Net 2.0 renders 6.7M depth-only images, but its Grasp Quality CNN only ranks externally-generated candidates rather than predicting a grasp location end-to-end; Johns et al. simulated attempts on 1,000 objects but never released the data.

On top of the data-scale problem, the standard way to judge a predicted grasp — inherited from Cornell — is purely geometric: a prediction counts as correct if its orientation is within 30° of a ground-truth rectangle and their intersection-over-union exceeds 25%. The authors argue, and show with examples, that this criterion produces "visually" wrong verdicts in both directions — geometrically matching rectangles a human would call bad grasps, and geometrically off rectangles that would actually work.

// how it works

The method

Jacquard is generated, not collected. CAD models come from ShapeNetSem, a semantically-annotated subset of ShapeNet; each one is rescaled so its longest side falls between 8 and 90 cm, given a size-scaled mass (80 g at 8 cm, 900 g at 90 cm), dropped from a random pose onto a randomly-textured plane inside the pyBullet physics engine, and left to settle. Up to five scenes are captured per object this way, for different views. Every grasp — whether a training label or a network's prediction — is described as a five-dimensional rectangle:

g = { x, y, h, w, θ } // center (x,y), size (h,w), orientation θ relative to the image's horizontal axis

The settled scene feeds two independent modules. Rendering (Blender, Cycles renderer) produces an RGB image, a "true" depth map, and a binary object mask — and separately renders two more RGB images under a projected structured-light pattern, which are run through Hirschmuller's semi-global-matching stereo algorithm to synthesize a noisy depth map. That's a deliberate step: instead of adding Gaussian noise to the perfect depth image (the shortcut Johns et al. used), Jacquard tries to reproduce what an actual stereo or structured-light depth sensor would output, to narrow the simulation-to-real domain gap on the depth channel specifically. Annotation (also pyBullet) samples thousands of candidate grasp rectangles per scene, weighted toward image regions with dense aligned edges rather than uniformly at random (a heuristic chosen for efficiency — the paper reports that other reasonable heuristics converged to a similar final grasp distribution, just at the cost of more random trials); every candidate is tested via rigid-body simulation with a 2 cm gripper jaw, and every candidate that succeeds is retested across the full family of jaw widths, {1, 2, 3, 4, 6} cm (10 cm max opening), so one successful location can carry up to five separate jaw-size annotations. A final pass discards near-duplicate successes so each grasp is only recorded once.

The result released to the community: more than 50k images across 11k objects and 1.1 million unique successful grasp positions — with RGB, true depth, stereo-computed depth and object masks for every image.

The paper's second contribution is a new way to judge a prediction, not just more data to train on: simulated grasp trial (SGT). Instead of comparing a predicted rectangle to a ground-truth rectangle geometrically, SGT rebuilds the exact scene in the simulator and has the simulated robot physically attempt the predicted grasp — the prediction is "correct" only if the object is actually lifted, moved, and dropped at the target location, which is the same operational definition already used to generate every training label. The motivation is the rectangle metric's own visible failure modes, shown below.

SGT isn't just a metric reported in a table — the authors release a public web interface alongside the dataset so other researchers can submit a predicted grasp and get back its SGT outcome, without having to reimplement the simulation pipeline themselves.

// what they found

The results

Table I positions Jacquard against the public datasets the paper compares itself to. Read against Cornell specifically, the ratio depends on which axis you pick: 11k vs. 240 objects is ≈46×; 54k vs. Cornell's own table-reported 1,035 images is ≈52× (or ≈61× against the 885 images the paper's own related-work section quotes for Cornell — the two numbers disagree with each other, see the critique below); and 1.1M vs. 8,019 grasps is ≈137×. The paper's text calls Jacquard "more than 50 times bigger" than Cornell without specifying an axis — true for images and grasps, not quite true for object count.

The cross-dataset experiment (Table II) trains the same off-the-shelf AlexNet — ImageNet-pretrained convolutional weights, fully-connected layers trained from scratch, depth normalized into the color range with the blue channel duplicated to accept RGB-D input, 100k SGD iterations, learning rate 0.0005 dropped to 0.00005 after 75k iterations — separately on Cornell and on a 15k-image / 3k-object / 316k-grasp subset of Jacquard, with 5-fold cross-validation in both directions, and cross-tests each. Both metrics are reported where they apply; SGT is only computable when the test set is Jacquard-sourced. The paper doesn't say why the Cornell-tested cells have no SGT column — that's our reading, not a claim it makes explicitly — but it follows from how SGT is defined (Sec. Reading the table's six cells in full (not just the ones favorable to Jacquard): trained-on-Jacquard beats trained-on-Cornell on two of the three columns — the Jacquard-tested rectangle column (74.21% vs. 54.28%, a 19.93-point gap the paper rounds to "20 points") and the SGT column (72.42% vs. 42.76%, a 29.66-point gap rounded to "30 points") — but loses the one column where the model is tested inside its rival's own domain: trained-on-Cornell scores 86.88% on Cornell-test, ahead of trained-on-Jacquard's 81.92% there. The paper reads this as evidence that a network trained on Jacquard's larger, more diverse pool of objects and grasps generalizes better to an unseen (Cornell) domain than a network trained on Cornell generalizes to Jacquard's greater diversity — not as an unqualified win on every axis.

A separate, independent check uses an actual robot: a parallel-plate gripper on a Fanuc M-20iA arm attempting grasps on 28 real objects (15 everyday items, 13 industrial components). AlexNet trained on a 2k-object Jacquard subset reached a 78.43% real-world success rate; the same network trained on Cornell reached 60.46%, with most of its failures attributed to bad rectangle localization. The paper notes this 2k-subset network's real-robot rate is "even 6 points higher" (78.43 − 72.42 = 6.01, rounds to 6) than "the grasp accuracy displayed by Alexnet when it was trained and tested on the subset of 3k objects of Jacquard (see Table II) using the SGT criterion" — that 72.42% is Table II's SGT cell, from a different network, trained on the 3k-object/15k-image/316k-grasp subset used in the cross-dataset experiment, tested on a different population (the simulated Jacquard test split, not the 28 real objects) under a different criterion (SGT replay, not a physical grasp attempt). So the "6 points" figure crosses training subset, test population, and evaluation criterion all at once — it is suggestive that the real robot didn't badly underperform the simulated numbers, not a controlled comparison of the same model against itself.

// does it hold up?

The eval read

The strongest thing this paper has going for it is that it doesn't stop at the simulator: Section V-C's real-robot test is a genuine out-of-loop check, and its ranking (Jacquard-trained 78.43% > Cornell-trained 60.46%) agrees directionally with the simulated SGT ranking (72.42% > 42.76%). That matters because SGT itself has an obvious circularity risk worth naming precisely: it judges a predicted grasp by replaying it in the same pyBullet pipeline that generated Jacquard's own training labels, on Jacquard-sourced test scenes only (the Cornell-tested cells in Table II have no SGT column at all — our reading is that this is why, since there's no simulation state to rebuild from a real photograph, though the paper itself never states the omission or its cause). A model that overfits to whatever pyBullet's contact solver rewards, rather than to real physics, would look good under SGT without that meaning much. The real-robot experiment is the paper's actual answer to that concern, and it's a reasonable one — but it's a small one: 28 objects total, no stated number of grasp attempts per object or in total, and no confidence interval or significance test on the 78.43%-vs-60.46% gap, in contrast to the ± standard deviations reported for every simulated Table II cell (from 5-fold cross-validation). We don't know how noisy that headline real-world number is.

The cross-dataset comparison (Table II) also bundles two variables the paper doesn't try to separate. The Jacquard subset used for training carries 316k grasp annotations across 3k objects — about 39× Cornell's 8,019 grasps across 240 objects — so the 20-and-30-point generalization gaps are consistent with "synthetic scale and diversity helps" (the paper's stated hypothesis) but equally consistent with "more training data of any kind helps," since no experiment holds dataset size fixed while swapping real for synthetic. A run trained on a Cornell-sized subset of Jacquard would isolate the domain-realism question from the scale question; the paper doesn't run one. Separately, the paper never states whether its 5-fold cross-validation splits are done at the object level or the image level — a distinction the Cornell-dataset literature this paper is directly answering (Lenz et al., Redmon & Angelova) treats as consequential, since an image-level split can leak other views of the same physical object into the training fold and inflate same-domain accuracy.

On the dataset's own headline framing: the "more than 50 times bigger" claim in Section II is true for images (≈52–61×, depending on which of the paper's own two Cornell image counts you use) and understated for grasps (≈137×), but not quite true for object count (≈46×) — a minor point, but the paper's Table I and its own related-work prose disagree with each other on how many images Cornell has (1,035 in the table vs. 885 in the text), which is worth knowing before quoting either number elsewhere.

Read from 2026, this is squarely pre-transformer, pre-6-DoF, single-object robotics: AlexNet as the backbone, a top-down camera, one object per scene, and a 2D oriented-rectangle grasp representation that only really makes sense for a parallel-plate gripper approaching from directly above. GraspNet-1Billion (2020), which cites this paper, replaced almost every one of these choices — real captured RGB-D sensor images instead of rendered CAD scenes, cluttered multi-object scenes instead of a single object on a plane, full 6-DoF grasp poses instead of 2D rectangles, and an analytic force-closure scoring function that can grade any predicted grasp without a simulator replay, instead of SGT. Benchmarks and simulators built since (ManiSkill2, LIBERO, RLBench) have moved further still, to full manipulation policies rather than single-shot grasp detection. Jacquard's real contribution to that lineage is less the specific dataset than the pattern it established — automatize labeled data at scale via physics simulation, and evaluate by physically replaying the interaction rather than only matching geometry — which the field kept and generalized well past 2D rectangles.

baselines

Only Cornell is used experimentally (Table II); Dex-Net 2.0 and Levine et al. appear only descriptively in Table I, not as trained-and-tested comparisons, so we don't know how a Jacquard-trained model stacks up against models trained on those other large-scale sources.

contamination

Not an LLM-style leakage question here, but an analogous one: the paper doesn't state whether its 5-fold splits are object-wise or image-wise, so possible same-object leakage between folds is unverifiable from the text.

what's missed

No ablation holding training-set size fixed while varying real-vs-synthetic source, so scale and domain-realism are confounded in every headline number; no trial count or variance reported for the 28-object real-robot test; and the paper's own headline "6 points higher" real-robot comparison spans two different networks (2k-object vs. 3k-object training subsets), two different test populations, and two different criteria at once.

would raise confidence

A size-matched Jacquard-subset-vs-Cornell ablation; a stated object-wise-vs-image-wise fold protocol; per-trial real-robot data (N attempts, confidence interval) instead of two bare percentages.

// bottom line

Jacquard does what it sets out to do — trade hand-labeling for physics simulation to get two orders of magnitude more labeled grasps, and back its geometric metric with a physically-grounded one — and the real-robot numbers, thin as they are, back up the claim that the resulting models transfer. What it doesn't do is separate why that happens (more data? more diverse data? less human labeling bias?) or say much about how noisy its one physical-world number is. Treat the dataset and the SGT idea as the durable contributions; treat "50× bigger" and "6 points higher on the real robot" as headline framings that are directionally true but softer than they read at first pass.

// takeaways

What to remember

  1. Jacquard automatizes grasp-dataset generation with pyBullet physics and Blender rendering on ShapeNetSem CAD models, producing 1.1M labeled grasps across 11k objects and 54k images — roughly 46× Cornell by object count, 52–61× by image count, and 137× by grasp count, depending on which axis you read.
  2. Its second contribution, simulated grasp trial (SGT), judges a predicted grasp by physically replaying it in the simulator rather than matching a rectangle geometrically — but SGT only runs on Jacquard-sourced test scenes, and is validated only loosely against a small, statistics-free real-robot experiment (28 objects, 78.43% vs. 60.46% success).
  3. The cross-dataset AlexNet experiment shows a Jacquard-trained model beats a Cornell-trained one on 2 of 3 comparable columns but loses when tested inside Cornell's own domain — and the comparison never isolates data scale from data realism, a gap the field's next big step, GraspNet-1Billion, addressed with real sensor captures, cluttered scenes, and 6-DoF poses instead of 2D rectangles.
