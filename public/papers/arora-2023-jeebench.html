Have LLMs Advanced Enough? A Challenging Problem Solving Benchmark For Large Language Models

JEEBench pulls 515 problems from eight years of India's IIT JEE-Advanced entrance exam — pre-engineering Physics, Chemistry and Math that demands long-horizon reasoning, not fact lookup. GPT-4 with chain-of-thought and self-consistency reaches only 38.9% overall, and prompting it with the exam's own negative-marking scheme makes its score go down, not up.

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

By mid-2023, GPT-4-class models were being praised for "impressive performance on standard reasoning and STEM benchmarks" — GSM8K, MATH, MMLU, ScienceQA — but the authors don't take that on faith. They run their own diagnostic: sample 50 questions each from the MATH test set and from the high-school Physics, Chemistry and Mathematics sections of MMLU, and evaluate GPT-4 zero-shot on all of them alongside 50 JEEBench questions. GPT-4 solves more than 80% of the MMLU subsets, and roughly 60% of MATH, but on JEEBench-Math it solves close to a mere 20% (Figure 2). If a benchmark's job is to separate models that can genuinely reason from ones that pattern-match, the popular reasoning benchmarks of the day were running out of headroom.

The paper's fix is to source problems from a test that was never designed with LLMs in mind: the IIT JEE-Advanced, India's high-stakes entrance exam for its premier engineering institutes, with an acceptance rate the paper puts at "approx. 5%." A JEE-Advanced problem typically demands three things at once — retrieving the right domain-specific concept (e.g. the rules of static equilibrium, redox balancing, integration by parts), grounding that concept into the specific equations/constraints of the problem instance, and then carrying out the algebraic manipulation and arithmetic to reach a final numeric or symbolic answer. That triad — concept retrieval, grounding, algebraic manipulation — becomes the paper's own vocabulary for classifying where GPT-4 later fails.

JEEBench also differs from prior math/science benchmarks in a structural way: instead of a single free-form numeric answer, it inherits JEE-Advanced's own four response formats (single-correct MCQ, multi-correct MCQ, integer, and decimal numeric) and JEE's own partial-credit and negative-marking rules, which the paper carries all the way through to its evaluation metric and to a dedicated experiment on whether GPT-4 can manage risk under a scoring scheme that punishes wrong guesses.

// how it works

The method

JEEBench's 515 problems are harvested from the past 8 editions of JEE-Advanced (2016-2023, publicly archived at jeeadv.ac.in), each year contributing two exam papers of 50-60 questions split roughly evenly across Physics, Chemistry and Mathematics. The authors extract the PDFs to LaTeX with off-the-shelf OCR tooling, then discard every problem whose statement depends on a diagram — about 40% of the raw pool — since those can't be posed to a text-only model, and manually quality-check the rest. That diagram exclusion is why Physics and Chemistry end up with fewer surviving problems than Math.

Each of the 515 problems belongs to one of four response formats — single-correct MCQ (one of four options), multi-correct MCQ (any subset of four options), integer-type (an unbounded non-negative integer), or numeric-type (a decimal answer to two places) — and JEE's own sub-topic taxonomy is broad within each subject (Figure 9): Mathematics spans Calculus (29.7%), Algebra (28.0%), Coordinate Geometry (23.3%) and Combinatorics & Probability (12.7%) as its largest slices; Chemistry is dominated by Inorganic Chemistry (46.8%); Physics is led by Mechanics (33.3%) and Modern Physics (22.8%).

Scoring mirrors JEE-Advanced's actual answer-key logic rather than plain accuracy throughout: single-correct MCQs and integer-type questions score 1 for an exact match and 0 otherwise; numeric-type questions score 1 if the model's answer is within 0.01 of the gold value; multi-correct MCQs score 0 if the model selects any incorrect option, and otherwise award 0.25 per correct option selected (so, e.g., a gold answer of "ABD" against a model answer of "BD" scores 0.5) — deliberately reproducing JEE's own incentive against blind guessing on multi-select questions.

Two open-source models (Falcon7B-Instruct, Alpaca-LoRA) and four proprietary models — OpenAI's text-davinci-003 (GPT-3), gpt-3.5-turbo (GPT-3.5), gpt-4-0314 (GPT-4), and Google's text-bison-001 (PaLM-2) — are prompted zero-shot between May 17 and June 23, 2023 (temperature 0, max 2048 tokens), with the answer manually extracted from each response and recorded as "None" whenever the response is gibberish, hedges that no option is correct, or returns a value of the wrong response type. On top of vanilla zero-shot prompting for GPT-4, the paper layers: zero-shot chain-of-thought ("Let's think step by step"); 1-shot CoT (one worked example per question-type/subject pair, drawn from the 2014 exam); Self-Critique (a second GPT-4 call is shown the problem and the first GPT-4's solution and asked to find and fix errors); and Self-Consistency@8 (SC@8 — 8 non-zero-temperature samples, majority vote for single-answer formats, an independent per-option ≥50%-frequency rule for multi-correct MCQs).

// what they found

The results

Table 2 (reproduced below, scores converted to percent) is the paper's central result, breaking every model/method down by subject and by question type, with the aggregate in the last column. Random guessing scores 10.5% overall — a nonzero floor purely because MCQ options give partial credit even to blind guesses, while Integer- and Numeric-type questions score essentially 0% at random. Both open-source baselines (Falcon7B-Instruct, Alpaca-LoRA) land within a couple of points of random (9.8% and 8.9% overall), and every proprietary model clears them by a wide margin, with performance rising monotonically through the GPT family: GPT-3 at 12.2%, GPT-3.5 at 17.7%, vanilla GPT-4 at 30.9% — a jump the paper itself calls "far superior... by a large margin," and one that Table 2 puts at roughly 13 points on the Total column.

The subject-level breakdown backs a specific claim the paper makes: comparing GPT-3.5 to vanilla GPT-4, "the performance boost is the highest for Chemistry, followed by Physics, and lastly Maths." The Chemistry column moves 22.8→42.3 (+19.5 points), Physics moves 17.3→35.2 (+17.9 points), and Mathematics moves only 14.6→21.2 (+6.6 points) — exactly that ordering, which the authors attribute to Mathematics questions demanding the deepest reasoning chains. Zero-shot CoT ("Let's think step by step") lifts GPT-4's Total from 30.9% to 35.0%, and Self-Consistency@8 adds a further, exactly-reported +3.9 points on top of CoT (35.0%→38.9%) — the best score in the table, driven largely by a jump on Single-Correct MCQs (47.3%→61.8%). 1-shot CoT, by contrast, underperforms zero-shot CoT (29.2% vs. 35.0%); the authors' hypothesis is that few-shot examples don't transfer the specific conceptual/grounding fixes GPT-4 needs, and may narrow the range of reasoning paths it tries. Of the augmentations layered on top of GPT-4+CoT, Self-Critique is the one that regresses the aggregate, exactly as the paper states: "the performance goes down from 35% to 33.9%" (1-shot CoT is a separate prompting variant rather than a CoT add-on, and it regresses too, landing below even vanilla GPT-4's 30.9%).

A separate function-calling experiment (only run with the newer gpt-4-0613 snapshot, since the calculator API wasn't available on gpt-4-0314) found zero-shot CoT scoring 30.3% on gpt-4-0613 — already weaker than 35.0% on gpt-4-0314 — and adding a 4-function arithmetic calculator tool dropped it further, to 27.4%, with GPT-4 sometimes hallucinating invalid function names or arguments.

A manual error analysis on a random 100-problem subset of GPT-4+CoT responses (subset score: 27.25%) found 20 fully "Perfect" solutions (correct answer, correct reasoning) and, among the 80 non-perfect cases, 34 conceptual errors (failing to retrieve the right domain concept), 30 computation errors (algebra/arithmetic slips), 15 grounding errors (right concept, wrong equation/application), and 1 problem-miscomprehension case. The authors separately note that of the questions where the final answer was correct (27 of the 100), only 20 also had fully correct reasoning — i.e., "28% of the time, the model gives a correct answer for the wrong reasons."

The paper also tests whether GPT-4 can act as its own verifier ("Self-Critique"): a second GPT-4 instance is shown the problem plus the first GPT-4's CoT solution and told to find and fix errors. Manually re-examining the same 100-problem subset, the paper finds that on the 80 solutions that actually contained an error, GPT-4-as-verifier found no error at all in 46 of them (57.5%), found an error but failed to fix it in 25, converted a correct part of the solution into an error in 7, and only successfully found and fixed an error in 2. On the 20 solutions that had no error to begin with, the verifier introduced a spurious error in 1 and correctly left 19 untouched.

Section 4.5 turns to exam-realistic scoring: JEE-Advanced awards +3/−1 for single-correct MCQs and +4/−2 (with +1 per correct option on partial answers) for multi-correct MCQs. Prompting GPT-4+CoT with this exact marking scheme and asking it to skip questions it's unsure of lowers its total score on all MCQ questions from 308 to 198 (out of 1074 possible marks) — evidence, the authors argue, that GPT-4 cannot assess its own answer-risk from instructions alone. Their alternative is a post-hoc method: convert Self-Consistency's per-option vote frequency into a confidence score (calibration: maximum calibration error 0.136, average calibration error 0.098 — the model is somewhat overconfident at high confidence), then only include an option in the final answer if its confidence clears a tuned threshold τ. Hyperparameter search on a 2016-2021 validation split finds τsingle=0.125 and τmultiple=0.75 optimal; applied to the held-out 2022-2023 MCQ test set, thresholding raises the total score from 69 (GPT-4+CoT+SC) to 72 — a +4.3% relative gain the paper reports directly, matching (111−39)/(118−49) exactly.

Finally, using the 2023 JEE-Advanced paper (65 of its 102 questions were text-only; total possible marks 360), GPT-4+CoT+SC+Thresholding on MCQs plus plain SC on Integer/Numeric scores 49 of 229 available marks on the textual subset; normalizing that up to the full 360-mark scale (assuming image-based questions are no easier or harder) projects a score of 77/360. The actual 2023 human cohort's top-10% cutoff was about 97/360 and top-20% cutoff about 70/360 — so GPT-4's projected 77 lands between them, i.e. in the 80th-90th percentile of that year's human test-takers.

// does it hold up?

The eval read

JEEBench's central methodological virtue is that it borrows its grading rubric wholesale from a real, decades-old, professionally-designed exam rather than inventing one — the partial-credit multi-correct-MCQ scoring, the ±3/±4 marking scheme, and the percentile bands all come from JEE-Advanced itself, which makes the human comparison in Section 4.6 unusually well-grounded compared to benchmarks that invent their own "difficulty" framing. The concept-retrieval / grounding / algebraic-manipulation error taxonomy is also a genuinely useful diagnostic lens, and the paper is honest that "a question can have multiple types of errors, however, we only investigate the first error that is noticed" — a real limitation it flags rather than hides.

That said, the headline "38.9% overall" (or the abstract's "less than 40%") flattens enormous variance the table itself contains: GPT-4+CoT+SC@8 hits 61.8% on Single-Correct MCQs but only 23.4% on Numeric-type — nearly a 40-point spread within one model/method cell. A single aggregate makes JEEBench read as "one difficulty level" when it's really closer to four benchmarks (by format) crossed with three subjects, and the paper's own Total column is a straight, unweighted average across an uneven mix of MCQ formats (with generous partial credit for multi-correct guessing) and free-response formats (integer/numeric, essentially no partial credit) — so a method that trades MCQ gains for numeric losses, or vice versa, can move the aggregate without a clean interpretation. The GPT-4+CoT+Self-Critique row is a good example: it improves Chemistry (46.8→48.7) and Integer (25.6→28.0) but Single-Correct MCQ drops sharply from 47.3 to 35.5, and the aggregate drop is driven almost entirely by that one column.

baselines

The open-source comparison is thin and dated even for mid-2023: Falcon7B-Instruct and Alpaca-LoRA are both 7B-parameter models pitted against GPT-3/3.5/4 and PaLM-2, so "open-source models perform as good as random" is really a statement about picking small, weak open checkpoints rather than the strongest open weights available at the time (e.g. LLaMA-65B-class models aren't tested). The function-calling ablation is run only on a different GPT-4 snapshot (gpt-4-0613) than the rest of the paper (gpt-4-0314), which the paper itself notes makes it "weaker" even before the calculator is added, muddying that specific comparison.

contamination

Addressed directly and reasonably carefully in Appendix A.8, not an afterthought: a C4-corpus substring search (50 sampled Physics+Chemistry questions, Math excluded as "more LaTeX heavy") found no matches; a Common Crawl URL-index search across popular JEE-solution sites found 30 of 515 questions (19 Chemistry, 11 Math — no Physics matches reported) with solutions publicly indexed, under 6% of the dataset; and prompting GPT-4 to complete a truncated question prefix produced no verbatim "precarious" completions on the same 50-question sample. Removing the 30 flagged questions moves GPT-4+CoT's score only from 0.350 to 0.347. The strongest evidence, though, is structural: the 2023 exam (released June 4, 2023) necessarily postdates every tested model's training cutoff, and GPT-4+CoT+SC scores 33.8% on that year's questions versus 39.6% on the rest of the dataset — if anything lower, not inflated, on the guaranteed-uncontaminated slice (the paper attributes some of this gap to certain exam years, e.g. 2017, being easier rather than to contamination elsewhere).

what's missed

The 100-problem manual error analysis and the 100-problem self-critique analysis appear to draw on the same subset, are entirely manual/qualitative (no inter-annotator agreement reported), and use a "first error noticed" convention that likely undercounts multi-error solutions. The human-percentile claim rests on one untested assumption — that the 37 image-containing 2023 questions are, on average, no harder or easier than the 65 textual ones used to compute GPT-4's score — which the paper states but cannot verify from text alone. And the partial-credit multi-correct scoring, while faithful to JEE's own rules, means the "Total" column mixes a guess-resistant scoring scheme (MCQs) with a strict exact-match scheme (Integer/Numeric) in a single unweighted average.

would raise confidence

Re-running the calculator/function-calling ablation on the same snapshot used everywhere else; a larger, annotated error-analysis sample with multiple raters and multi-error tagging; and following through on the paper's own proposed mitigation — adding "40-50 new uncontaminated problems... annually" from each fresh JEE-Advanced sitting — to keep JEEBench a genuinely rolling, leakage-resistant benchmark rather than a fixed 515-problem snapshot that ages the same way GSM8K and MATH did.

// bottom line

JEEBench is a well-motivated, exam-grounded stress test that correctly identified in 2023 that popular STEM benchmarks were saturating for GPT-4, and its contamination analysis and human-percentile framing are more rigorous than most benchmark papers of its era. But its headline number is an unweighted blend across four scoring regimes and three subjects with very different difficulty profiles, its open-source baselines were never competitive, and its qualitative error/verifier analyses rest on a single 100-problem manual pass — read the subject-by-format table, not the Total column, to know what the benchmark is actually measuring.

// takeaways

What to remember

  1. JEEBench draws 515 Physics/Chemistry/Math problems from 8 years of India's IIT JEE-Advanced exam across four answer formats (single-correct MCQ, multi-correct MCQ, integer, numeric); even GPT-4 with chain-of-thought and self-consistency (SC@8) reaches only 38.9% overall, against ~85-90% GPT-4 solves on the equivalent MMLU subjects.
  2. Self-critique doesn't help here: when a GPT-4 CoT solution actually contains an error, a second GPT-4 instance asked to find and fix it misses the error entirely 46 of 80 times (57.5%), and the aggregate score falls from 35.0% to 33.9% after applying its edits — the opposite of the direction self-refinement helps in other domains.
  3. GPT-4 can't manage exam-style risk from instructions alone — prompting it with JEE's own negative-marking scheme drops its MCQ total from 308 to 198 (out of 1074) — but a post-hoc confidence-threshold on self-consistency (τsingle=0.125, τmultiple=0.75) recovers it, lifting the 2022-2023 test-set total from 69 to 72 (+4.3%) and projecting GPT-4 into the 80th-90th percentile of 2023 JEE-Advanced human test-takers.
