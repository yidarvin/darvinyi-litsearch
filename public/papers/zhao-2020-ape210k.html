Ape210K: A Large-Scale and Template-Rich Dataset of Math Word Problems

A Yuanfudao/Fenbi team describes 210,488 Chinese elementary-school math word problems spanning 56,532 unique equation templates — 9x the problem count and 25x the template diversity of what the paper calls the previous largest public dataset, Math23K — plus a copy-augmented seq2seq baseline that beats both prior state-of-the-art solvers the authors re-ran on the new data. The paper was withdrawn by its own authors in October 2020; the official release URL is now dead, though a third-party copy has circulated publicly since days after the withdrawal — see below.

// withdrawn by the authors

arXiv 2009.11506 carries a withdrawal notice: "This paper has been withdrawn by Liang Wang", with the comment "We decide to withdraw this paper, since the proposed Ape210K dataset is not going public, the experiments in this paper is meaningless and irreproducible without access to the dataset." The v1 abstract's own claim to "make Ape210K dataset publicly available" is exactly what that notice retracts. The GitHub URL printed in the v1 abstract, github.com/yuantiku/ape210k, is dead today — but a full third-party copy has circulated publicly on GitHub since days after the withdrawal, and been redistributed on HuggingFace since 2023, with no established chain of custody back to the original team. Every number on this page comes from the v1 PDF (24 Sep 2020); v2 (9 Oct 2020) is the one-page withdrawal itself, and the abs page now serves no PDF at all.

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

Math word problem (MWP) solving — turning a natural-language description into an equation and a numeric answer — is a task where "the performances of the aforementioned systems often drop dramatically" as dataset diversity increases, and by 2020 the field's public datasets were all compromised in the same two ways: too small and too narrow. Alg514 had 514 problems and 28 templates; Dolphin1878 had 1,878 problems; Dolphin18K reached 18,460 problems with 5,871 templates; and the most widely used dataset, Math23K, tops out at 23,160 problems with only 2,187 equation templates. Small scale blocks the use of data-hungry neural models, and low template diversity means a model can do well by memorizing a handful of equation shapes rather than actually understanding the text — "a serious question on the generalization capability of the models trained on Math23K," as the authors put it.

The paper's proposed fix isn't just "collect more problems," but specifically more template diversity: humans posed hundreds of thousands of original problems to Yuanfudao's online production system, and the authors argue that scale without template variety just reproduces Math23K's narrowness at a larger size. They also flag a second axis prior datasets under-covered: problems whose equations require external constants — numbers that never appear in the problem text and must be supplied from commonsense or domain knowledge (e.g., "a triangle has 3 edges," or the volume relationship between a cone and a cylinder of equal base and height). Math23K's equations contain such constants only 0.80% of the time (by template); the authors position this as evidence that even the largest prior dataset mostly tests shallow number-plugging rather than the kind of world knowledge required to actually solve grade-school math in the wild.

// how it works

The method

Ape210K's construction starts from roughly 900K Chinese math word problems for elementary-school students, pulled from Yuanfudao's own online production system and written by humans (problem-solving rationales and answers are attributed to professional math teachers). The authors filter this pool down, discarding problems that: have no extractable answer, have a non-numeric answer (e.g. "a+b"), contain no numbers in the text, embed images or tables in the description, or pose multiple questions at once (about one-third of the raw pool). The result — 210,488 single-unknown-variable problems, each with a gold numeric answer — is deliberately narrower in problem type than the raw 900K, but far broader in template diversity than any prior public dataset.

Because the source data gives human-written rationales rather than explicit equations, the authors run a rule-based equation-extraction pipeline: candidate equations consistent with the gold numeric answer are identified from the rationale text, and heuristics pick at most one — if several candidates exist, the longest is kept (short equations are more likely partial/intermediate calculations); if none can be extracted, the trivial equation "x = answer" is substituted. A preprocessing pass then normalizes unit strings (e.g. "kilogram/kg/KG/Kg" → "kg"), converts full-width to half-width characters, converts number-words to digits, and reorders equation symbols to track the order numbers appear in the problem text ("equation normalization"), following a technique the authors credit to Wang et al.'s prior work on Math23K.

To establish the dataset's difficulty and give future work a baseline, the paper also proposes a copy-augmented, feature-enriched seq2seq model — an encoder-decoder with soft (Bahdanau) attention. The embedding layer combines five handcrafted per-token features (character identity, part-of-speech tag, word identity, a numeric-token flag, and a numeric-token rank index) via a learned weighted sum; character/word embeddings are pretrained with word2vec on the problem-description text. The encoder is a 4-layer BiLSTM; the decoder is a 2-layer BiLSTM with input-feeding attention. A copying mechanism — borrowed from pointer-generator summarization and applied here to MWP solving "for the first time," per the authors — lets the decoder mix a distribution over tokens copied from the problem text with the ordinary target-vocabulary distribution, controlled by a learned balance factor.

p(w) = α × pcopy(w) + (1 − α) × pvocab(w) // the copying mechanism's output distribution — a per-timestep balance α between copying a token straight from the problem description and generating from the target vocabulary
// what they found

The results

The headline comparison is scale and diversity against six prior public MWP datasets. Ape210K's 210,488 problems and 56,532 templates dwarf every predecessor — roughly 9x Math23K's problem count and 25x its template count — and 37.70% of Ape210K's equation templates contain an external constant, against just 0.80% for Math23K.

The dataset is split into 200,488 / 5,000 / 5,000 train/valid/test problems (5,000/5,000 held out for validation and test), with 54,425 / 2,835 / 2,732 distinct templates respectively appearing in each split.

On the difficulty side, the paper runs its own baseline against prior systems on both datasets. On Math23K (5-fold cross-validation, matching prior work), the proposed model reaches 77.5% accuracy, ahead of the previous best, GTS-model, at 74.3% — the "outperforms … by 3.2%" figure the abstract advertises. On Ape210K's held-out test set, the same model scores 70.20%, clearly ahead of two open-source baselines re-run on the new data: StackDecoder (52.28%) and GTS-model (56.56%) — StackDecoder in particular struggles because it can't represent external constants other than 1 and π. The authors read the across-dataset drop for every system (e.g. StackDecoder falls from 65.8% on Math23K to 52.28% on Ape210K, though these come from different eval protocols — 5-fold CV vs. a single held-out test split) as evidence that "Ape210K is a more challenging benchmark," even with far more training data on offer.

An ablation on Ape210K isolates each design choice's contribution to the 70.20% baseline: removing the copying mechanism costs 2.92 points (67.28%), removing the four handcrafted input features (keeping only the character feature) costs 2.90 points (67.30%), dropping the pretrained embeddings costs 1.22 points (68.98%), and dropping equation normalization costs 1.00 point (69.20%); stripping all four at once drops the model to 66.22% (−3.98 points total). A companion error analysis isolates three "hard" subsets of the Ape210K test set — problems whose equation template is ≥15 tokens long (47.37% accuracy, 15.96% of the test set), problems whose template occurs only once in the whole dataset (49.11% accuracy, 44.06% of the test set), and problems whose equations contain external constants (52.89% accuracy, 37.66% of the test set) — every one well below the 70.20% overall test accuracy.

// does it hold up?

The eval read

The single most important fact about this paper is not in the PDF at all: arXiv 2009.11506 carries a withdrawal notice, "This paper has been withdrawn by Liang Wang," with the comment "We decide to withdraw this paper, since the proposed Ape210K dataset is not going public, the experiments in this paper is meaningless and irreproducible without access to the dataset." That retracts the v1 abstract's own claim that the dataset was made "publicly available" on GitHub. In effect, the authors are on record saying their own reported experiments are irreproducible without access to the dataset as they controlled it — and the URL from the v1 abstract is dead today. Yet Ape210K is still cited by name, years later, by GSM8K and JEEBench, and a full third-party copy has circulated publicly on GitHub since days after the withdrawal, and on HuggingFace since 2023. Everything below should be read with that in mind: the authoritative source is gone, and what circulates today has no established chain of custody back to it — so this describes what the paper claims, not something a reader can go verify against a source the authors stand behind.

Ape210K's own reported evaluation also predates the LLM era entirely — StackDecoder, GTS-model, and the paper's own copy-augmented seq2seq baseline are all pre-2020 neural architectures trained from scratch per-dataset, not pretrained language models. That's not a flaw in itself (the paper is honest that it's proposing a dataset, with the baseline model serving mainly to demonstrate the dataset has headroom left to solve), but it means none of the accuracy numbers on this page tell you anything about how a modern LLM performs on Ape210K — which is precisely why later papers like GSM8K and JEEBench cite Ape210K only as a comparison point in prose, not as an eval target they run their own models against.

The equation labels themselves are not gold — they're extracted from human-written natural-language rationales by a heuristic rule (longest equation among those consistent with the numeric answer, or "x = answer" as a fallback). The paper reports no inter-annotator agreement, no manual audit sample, and no estimate of how often this heuristic picks the "wrong" equation among several that would also produce the correct number — a real risk given that "one equation may have many different but equivalent forms," which is exactly the ambiguity the extraction rule has to resolve automatically at 210K-problem scale. Math23K, by contrast, ended up smaller not because its equations were curated any more carefully, but because a large share of its candidate templates were thrown out during construction — the paper's own Related Work says only that Math23K "comes from multiple online education websites, but a large number of templates are discarded, with only 23,160 math problems and 2,187 templates left."

The dataset is also explicitly narrower than the raw problem pool it's drawn from: about one-third of the original ~900K problems were multi-question and dropped, along with anything containing images/tables or a non-numeric answer. "Large-scale and diverse" describes Ape210K well relative to prior single-unknown-variable MWP datasets, but the paper is candid that multi-variable and richer problem types are left for future work — so the 210K figure headlines scale within a still-constrained problem format, not the full breadth of what Yuanfudao's system actually contains.

Finally, this is a Chinese-language dataset of Chinese elementary-school curriculum problems — a fact the paper states plainly but that's easy to lose if you only see "Ape210K" cited alongside English benchmarks like GSM8K or the MATH dataset. It measures something related but not identical to what those benchmarks measure, and cross-lingual transfer of models/results between them is not something this paper tests or claims.

baselines

Two open-source pre-neural-LLM seq2seq systems (StackDecoder, GTS-model) plus the paper's own proposed model, which is also the reported state of the art — there is no independently-run baseline the authors didn't also design or heavily adapt.

contamination

Not discussed, and the paper's own premise here has been overtaken by events. The withdrawal notice says the dataset "is not going public," and the URL printed in the v1 abstract is dead — but a full third-party copy has been publicly mirrored on GitHub since days after the withdrawal, and redistributed on HuggingFace since 2023. A widely-mirrored Chinese-language math word problem corpus is exactly the kind of thing a Chinese-web-scale pretraining crawl can pick up, so leakage risk is live, not foreclosed. The harder problem runs the other way: because the authoritative source is gone, the copies now circulating can only be checked so far. Their structure does match — the mirrored validation and test files hold exactly 5,000 problems each, the split sizes the paper states — but structural agreement is not record-for-record identity, and there is no way to confirm the problems themselves are the ones the reported numbers were measured on. The paper offers no canary or decontamination mechanism regardless.

what's missed

No human-accuracy ceiling is reported (the abstract only says the "gap is still significant between human and our baseline model" without a number), no audit of the heuristic equation-extraction pipeline's label noise, and no statement of whether the train/valid/test split is template-stratified or simply random.

would raise confidence

A manual-audit accuracy figure for the extracted equations on a sampled subset; an explicit human-solver accuracy number comparable to what Math23K papers report; and a stated policy on template overlap across the train/valid/test split.

// bottom line

As a scale-and-diversity argument against Math23K, the numbers hold up cleanly — the dataset comparison table and the cross-dataset accuracy drop are both internally consistent. As a rigorously-labeled gold-standard benchmark, it's weaker: the equations are heuristically extracted at scale with no reported quality audit, and the paper's own baseline evaluation is now a historical curiosity rather than a live leaderboard. Its lasting role, visible in how GSM8K and JEEBench cite it, is as the standing large, template-diverse, but Chinese-only reference point on the MWP-dataset landscape — even though the paper's own withdrawal means there is no authoritative copy of the dataset behind that reference point, only third-party mirrors of unverified fidelity to what the paper measured.
