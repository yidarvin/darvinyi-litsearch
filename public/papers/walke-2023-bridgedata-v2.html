BridgeData V2: A Dataset for Robot Learning at Scale

60,096 real-robot trajectories across 24 environments, evaluated with six offline learning methods and replicated zero-shot at a second lab — the real-world scale argument for robot learning, with the small-sample caveats that come with 10-trial success rates.

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

Robot manipulation research keeps re-learning a lesson computer vision and NLP had already banked: broad generalization comes from training expressive models on large, diverse data, not from clever inductive bias alone. The obstacle in robotics is that collecting that data on a real arm is slow and expensive, so most prior manipulation datasets pay for scale by narrowing scope. MIME (8.30k trajectories) and RoboTurk (2.10k) each cover a single environment; RoboNet (162k) and MT-Opt (800k) are enormous but essentially single-skill; BC-Z (26.0k) and RT-1 (130k) are diverse and language-conditioned but built on proprietary robots, and RT-1's data was never publicly released at all. A researcher who wants to reuse any of these either has to physically replicate the exact scene it was collected in, or lacks access to the hardware in the first place. The original Bridge Dataset (7.20k trajectories, 12 environments) got the multi-environment idea right, but it was collected for a fixed, small set of predefined tasks per scene — which means a policy trained on it can often infer the task from the scene alone, rather than actually reading a goal image or instruction.

BridgeData V2 targets both gaps at once: enough environments and task variety that skills learned on it transfer to a lab that never saw the collection scenes, and enough within-environment task density — many feasible tasks collected simultaneously in the same scene — that a multi-task policy has to attend to the task specification rather than guess from the picture. It is also built entirely on hardware ($4,000, publicly available, under two weeks to assemble) that any academic lab can acquire, which is itself part of the argument: a dataset only functions as shared infrastructure if the robot it was collected on is reproducible too.

// how it works

The dataset and how it was built

BridgeData V2 pairs a WidowX 250 arm with four cameras — a fixed RGBD "over-the-shoulder" view, two RGB cameras whose poses are randomized every 50 trajectories, and a wrist-mounted RGB camera — recording 640×480 images at a 5 Hz control frequency, with a 7D action space (6D Cartesian end-effector motion plus a discrete gripper dimension). The full rig costs about $4,000 in publicly available parts with a turnaround under two weeks.

Data collection is deliberately open-ended: a human teleoperates the arm with a VR controller through whatever feasible task a given scene affords — open a drawer, put a utensil in the sink — without resetting object positions between trajectories or labeling the task at collection time. Every 50 trajectories the collector reshuffles camera poses, swaps objects, and moves the workspace, so no single environment overfits one camera geometry or object set. Because tasks aren't labeled during collection, natural-language descriptions are added post-hoc by crowdworkers, instructed to emphasize the final location of any moved object.

The result is 50,365 human-teleoperated demonstrations spanning 13 skills (pick-and-place, pushing, wiping, sweeping, stacking, cloth folding, opening/closing drawers/doors/cardboard-box-flaps, knob twisting, switch flipping, zipper zipping, and lever turning) across 24 environments, plus 9,731 additional trajectories from a heavily randomized scripted pick-and-place policy — described as one that "fails frequently" but runs unsupervised, aimed at offline-RL methods that can exploit broad, suboptimal coverage. That totals 60,096 trajectories (Table 1), a >7× increase in demonstrations over the original Bridge Dataset's 7,200, and a mixture the authors describe as 84% human-collected / 16% scripted. Object manipulation (pick-and-place, pushing, reorienting) is the largest single task category at 34,509 of the trajectories the composition breakdown accounts for, with more compositional skills — stacking (2,769), cloth manipulation (2,960), granular sweeping (1,136) — making up the long tail; by environment, the majority of that same breakdown (33,111 trajectories) comes from 7 toy kitchens, with the rest spread across tabletops (12,290), toy sinks (3,102), and other setups.

To demonstrate the dataset isn't tied to any single algorithm's assumptions, the authors evaluate six offline methods spanning both conditioning modes and several policy architectures: goal-image-conditioned behavioral cloning (GCBC, a ResNet-34 encoder), a diffusion variant of it (D-GCBC, DDPM-style), action-chunking transformers (ACT, adapted to be goal-conditioned with a reduced chunk size of 5 to match the 5 Hz control rate), contrastive goal-conditioned RL (CRL), language-conditioned behavioral cloning (LCBC, using a frozen MUSE sentence encoder with FiLM conditioning), and RT-1, the only method given a history of observations and a higher (320×256, versus 128×128 for the rest) input resolution.

// what they found

The results

On the 8 tasks seen during training, evaluated over 10 trials each, the goal-conditioned methods land in a tight band — GCBC, D-GCBC, and RT-1 all average 0.49 success, CRL 0.42, ACT 0.41 — while LCBC, the language baseline without RT-1's larger images, action discretization, or observation history, trails at 0.23. That gap widens sharply on the 6 unseen-generalization tasks (new objects, new environments, or both): RT-1 averages 0.50 against LCBC's 0.08, with LCBC scoring exactly zero on 4 of the 6 (sweep rice, fold thick cloth, put a marker in a bowl, put a spoon on a cloth) because those instructions' object names are never grounded in the training language — LCBC is the one method of the six that does not reach non-zero success on most (≥4 of 6) unseen tasks; the other five all do. GCBC's unseen-task average (0.60) actually exceeds its own seen-task average (0.49). The authors read this pattern as evidence that the dataset's scale and diversity enable "a considerable degree of generalization," while noting explicitly that the language-conditioned methods "particularly struggled on tasks involving unseen objects."

The paper also runs a second-institution replication: the same six policies, trained at UC Berkeley, evaluated zero-shot (no new data collected at the new site) on 3 shared tasks at a different lab. Every method still scores above zero there, and the aggregate success rate across all 6 methods drops from 0.215 (Lab 1) to 0.165 (Lab 2). RT-1 has the smallest decline among the methods whose performance dropped (0.47→0.40); but two of the six methods, ACT (0.03→0.10) and CRL (0.13→0.20), actually improved at the new lab — a reminder that each of these cells is only 10 trials on 3 tasks.

A scaling analysis probes what drives that transfer. Swapping GCBC's image encoder from ResNet-18 to ResNet-34 to ResNet-50, on a single spoon-moving task, lifts its success rate from roughly 0.3 to roughly 0.6 to roughly 1.0 — strictly monotonic with model capacity (read from the figure's plotted markers; the paper states the trend but doesn't print these three values as text). Subsampling the training set to 25% / 50% / 75% / 100% of its size similarly improves both a seen task (≈0.50 → 0.70 → 0.70 → 1.00) and an unseen one (≈0.00 → 0.60 → 0.60 → 0.60). And holding dataset size roughly fixed (~28k vs. ~27k trajectories) while varying skill diversity — 3 skills vs. all 13 — moves an unseen pick-and-place task's success rate from 0.30 to 0.65 over 20 trials, the one exact pair of numbers this figure prints as text: diversity in skills, not just in objects, appears to transfer. A parallel appendix ablation trains GCBC on the original Bridge Dataset plus PTR data (13k trajectories, 15 environments, 11 skills) versus the full 60k-trajectory BridgeData V2, and reports average success on two unseen tasks rising from 0.08 to 0.70 (individual cells 0.05→0.65 and 0.10→0.70 — note the printed 0.70 average is itself 0.025 above the exact mean of those two cells, a small arithmetic loose end in an appendix table with no stated trial count).

// does it hold up?

The eval read

The dataset's contribution — scale, diversity, public hardware, cross-method compatibility — is real and well-documented. The specific generalization numbers used to argue for it, though, are thinner than they look. Every success rate in Tables 2–4 is a mean of exactly 10 binary trials per task (20 for the skill-diversity comparison); a single trial flipping outcome changes a cell by 0.1. The paper reports zero error bars, confidence intervals, standard deviations, or repeated seeds anywhere — a full-text search turns up no hits for "confidence interval," "error bar," "std," "standard deviation," "variance," "seed," or "p-value," and the word "significant(ly)" appears 9 times, always as a qualitative descriptor never backed by a reported statistic. That matters most exactly where the paper leans on small deltas: the cross-institution comparison shows an aggregate drop from 0.215 to 0.165 across 6 methods, but 2 of those 6 (ACT and CRL) individually improve at the new lab, which is at least as plausibly 10-trial sampling noise as it is a real robustness difference. The claim that RT-1 shows "only a small degradation" (0.47→0.40, a 0.07 drop) is true only when scoped to the 4 methods that got worse — a scoping the paper itself doesn't state explicitly.

The cross-institution test is also narrow in a way worth naming: only 3 of the paper's 8–14 evaluation tasks are replicated at Lab 2, at a single second site, zero-shot, with no attempt to isolate which factor — camera placement, lighting, object instances, robot calibration — drove the aggregate drop. "Works at a second institution" is a genuinely valuable claim for a dataset paper to substantiate at all, and few prior robot datasets even attempt it, but it is one replication rather than a systematic multi-site study. Similarly, the skill-diversity result (0.30→0.65) is a single comparison — one draw of a 3-skill subset, one draw of the 13-skill full set, evaluated once on one unseen task — not a swept study across multiple diversity levels or held-out skills.

Two more gaps: the scripted "autonomous" trajectories (9,731 of 60,096, 16% of the dataset) are explicitly described as coming from a policy that fails frequently, but that failure rate is never quantified, and no ablation removes this data and re-runs Tables 2–4 to show how much of the reported success depends on it being present. And the crowdsourced post-hoc language labels that LCBC and RT-1 depend on carry no reported quality-control pass or inter-annotator agreement check — an unknown fraction of the 60k trajectories' instructions could be mis-described, which would specifically undercut the language-conditioned results rather than the goal-image-conditioned ones.

baselines

Six methods across three architectures and two conditioning modes is broad for a dataset-introduction paper, and a genuine strength — most prior single-robot datasets are evaluated with one or two methods. But hyperparameters are shared loosely (four of the six use the same Adam 3e-4 / 2000-step warmup recipe) with no compute-matching discussion, and RT-1 alone gets higher-resolution (320×256) input and an observation history the others don't.

contamination

Not applicable in the pretraining-leakage sense — this is teleoperated real-robot data, not scraped text or images. But the post-hoc crowdsourced language labels have no reported quality-control step, which is the closest analogue to a label-noise concern here.

what's missed

Every headline number is a 10-to-20-trial point estimate with zero reported uncertainty; the cross-lab comparison in particular rests on deltas of 0.05–0.17, well within what 10-trial binomial noise alone could produce. The skill-diversity result is a different case: its 0.30→0.65 gap runs over 20 trials per policy, i.e. 6/20 against 13/20, which is borderline rather than clean — a normal approximation gives z ≈ 2.2 (p ≈ 0.03) but Fisher’s exact test, the appropriate one at this sample size, gives p ≈ 0.06. (Those are this page’s computations from the paper’s printed rates, not the paper’s — it reports no statistic of its own.) The sharper objection there is the one made above — a single draw on one unseen task, with no sweep between the two diversity levels.

would raise confidence

Report trial-level variance (or raise N materially); replicate at more than one second lab; ablate the 9,731 scripted trajectories and re-run Tables 2–4 with and without them; state a trial count for the appendix Table 6 ablation and reconcile its 0.70 average against its own 0.65/0.70 row values.

// bottom line

BridgeData V2 earns its influence on scope and reproducibility: public robot, public data, an 84%-human/16%-scripted mixture usable by six structurally different offline methods, and a demonstrated (if single) replication at a second institution — more than almost any prior real-robot manipulation dataset attempted. Treat the dataset's existence, scale, and cross-method compatibility as the paper's durable contribution; treat every specific success-rate comparison (RT-1's smallest cross-lab decline, the 0.30→0.65 skill-diversity jump, the monotonic scaling curves) as directional evidence from a handful of 10-trial point estimates, not as a statistically established result.

// takeaways

What to remember

  1. 60,096 trajectories (50,365 human-teleoperated + 9,731 scripted-autonomous) across 24 environments, 13 skills, and 100+ objects, collected on a $4,000 publicly available WidowX 250 rig — designed so results transfer to labs that never saw the collection scenes.
  2. Six structurally different offline methods (GCBC, D-GCBC, ACT, CRL, LCBC, RT-1) all train on the same data; 5 of the 6 reach non-zero success on most unseen tasks, the exception being LCBC (non-zero on only 2 of 6). RT-1 leads on language-conditioned tasks (0.49 seen / 0.50 unseen vs. LCBC's 0.23 / 0.08).
  3. Scale helps along every axis tested: model capacity, dataset size, and — held roughly fixed against dataset size — skill diversity (3 skills vs. 13) more than doubles success on an unseen task, 0.30→0.65.
  4. Zero-shot transfer to a second institution works but degrades (aggregate 0.215→0.165 across 6 methods on 3 shared tasks); every number in the paper is a 10-to-20-trial point estimate with no reported variance, so read comparisons as directional rather than statistically established.
