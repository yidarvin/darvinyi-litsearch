Evaluation of Text Generation: A Survey

By 2020, NLG had scaled past what BLEU and ROUGE were built to score — dialog, story generation, data-to-text — while human evaluation, the acknowledged gold standard, was itself inconsistently run and rarely validated. This survey sorts evaluation methods into a human-centric / untrained-automatic / machine-learned taxonomy, then revises itself eleven months later to fold in the GEM benchmark, GENIE, and Stochastic Parrots.

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

Natural language generation (NLG) — machine translation, dialog response, summarization, image captioning, data-to-text, and by 2020 increasingly open-ended story and long-form generation — is judged against a shifting target: many of these tasks are open-ended, so there is no single correct output to check against. Because of that, the paper opens by stating that "human evaluation remains the gold standard for almost all NLG tasks." But the authors immediately complicate that claim rather than resting on it: human evaluation is expensive, slow, and — the paper documents this with numbers rather than asserting it — poorly standardized. Hashimoto et al. (2019) found that 20 of the 26 generation papers published at ACL 2018 ran some form of human evaluation, yet Lee et al. (2021) found that in a sample of ACL and INLG papers, only 57% even reported how many participants took part. Howcroft et al. (2020) found that roughly 25% of generation papers published over the preceding twenty years never stated what dimensions (fluency? coherence? adequacy?) their human evaluators were rating on, and — of that same twenty-year population, not just the ones that did name dimensions — fewer than half defined those dimensions. Agreement reporting is worse still: Amidei et al. (2019a) found that only 18% of the 135 generation papers they reviewed included any inter-annotator agreement measure at all.

Meanwhile, automatic metrics had proliferated without a shared map. BLEU and ROUGE, both built for machine translation and summarization respectively, get reused across tasks whose failure modes they were never designed to catch, and newer BERT-based learned metrics were arriving with no common frame for comparing them to the overlap-based metrics they were meant to replace. Prior surveys existed, but each covered a narrower slice: task-specific reviews of evaluation for image captioning, machine translation, or dialog, or general NLG surveys (the closest being Gkatzia & Mahamood, 2015) that treat evaluation as one section among many rather than the subject. The gap this paper targets is a single, up-to-date taxonomy spanning all three modes of NLG evaluation — human judgment, untrained automatic metrics, and machine-learned metrics — together with a documented account of what each mode gets right and where it breaks down.

The taxonomy

The survey groups NLG evaluation into three categories, and spends 29 of the 46 pages before its bibliography working through them section by section, then turns to shared tasks and two case studies. Human-centric evaluation (its Section 2) splits into intrinsic evaluation — asking people to rate generated text directly, either on a Likert/sliding scale or by ranking/comparing outputs (Figure 1) — and extrinsic evaluation, which measures how a generated text performs in a downstream task a user actually cares about (e.g., counting the mistakes people make following a generated instruction). The survey also works through how evaluator disagreement is measured: simple percent agreement doesn't correct for chance; Cohen's κ and Fleiss' κ (for two and for many evaluators respectively) both subtract out the probability that evaluators would agree by chance alone; and Krippendorff's α generalizes further by letting different kinds of disagreement be weighted differently rather than treating every mismatch as equally bad — it is defined as one minus the ratio of observed to chance-expected weighted disagreement, not a raw agreement percentage.

Untrained automatic metrics (Section 3) are metrics that score generated text against reference text without any learned model in the loop. The survey groups them into five families: n-gram overlap (BLEU's weighted geometric mean of n-gram precision; ROUGE's recall-oriented variants — ROUGE-1/2/3/4, ROUGE-L, ROUGE-S/SU; METEOR's precision/recall harmonic mean with stemming and synonym matching; CIDEr's tf-idf-weighted consensus scoring for captioning; NIST), distance-based metrics (edit distance measures like WER and TER; embedding-distance measures like Word Mover's Distance and Sentence Mover's Distance, shown in Figure 2), n-gram diversity metrics (type-token ratio, Self-BLEU), explicit content-overlap metrics (SPICE's scene-graph matching for captions, the Pyramid method for summaries), and syntactic-similarity metrics.

Machine-learned metrics (Section 4) replace hand-built formulas with models trained to imitate human judgment. The survey walks through sentence-embedding similarity models (from Skip-Thoughts through DSSM, InferSent, and Quick-Thought), regression-based metrics like RUSE, models trained directly on collected human ratings like ADEM, BERT-based metrics, factual-correctness models, and composite metrics that combine several signals into one learned score. BERTScore (the survey's own Figure 8, reproduced below as Figure 3) is its central BERT-based example: it embeds both the reference and candidate sentence with a pretrained contextual encoder, computes pairwise cosine similarity between every reference token and every candidate token, takes each reference token's maximum similarity to any candidate token, and optionally weights that by idf before averaging into a single precision/recall/F1 triple. BLEURT extends the idea by fine-tuning a BERT checkpoint first on synthetic sentence pairs scored by automatic metrics like BLEU/ROUGE, and then on real system outputs scored by humans — a two-stage recipe the survey singles out as the reason BLEURT holds up better than a metric fine-tuned directly on human ratings alone (more on this in the next section).

// what they found

The survey's judgments

The paper's central empirical demonstration of why n-gram overlap fails is a small worked example (its Table 3) rather than a large benchmark run — and even this table's examples are, in the paper's own words, "adapted from Liu et al. (2016) and Kilickaya et al. (2017)," not built from scratch. Given a dialog context ("Hey John, what do you want to do tonight?" / "Why don't we go see a movie?"), a plausible model response — "Oh sure! Heard the film about Turing is out!" — shares zero words with the ground-truth response ("Nah, I hate that stuff, let's do something active."), so BLEU, ROUGE, and WMD all score it exactly 0.0, indistinguishable from a nonsensical response. The paired image-captioning example is less extreme but makes the same point at a smaller scale: a plausible re-description of the same image ("a guy wearing a life vest is in a small boat on a lake" against the ground truth "a man wearing a red life jacket is sitting in a canoe on a lake") scores BLEU 0.20, ROUGE 0.57, and WMD 0.10 — all far from a perfect match despite being a correct paraphrase. Beyond this worked example, the survey compiles findings from the literature on individual metrics: Reiter (2018) is cited for finding there's not enough evidence that BLEU is the best available metric for NLG tasks other than machine translation; Caccia et al. (2018) found that generated text with a perfect BLEU score was often grammatically fine but semantically incoherent; and Mathur et al. (2020) found that BLEU's correlation with human judgment is inflated by outliers — remove them and the correlation drops. ROUGE fares somewhat better within its home domain (generic summarization), but the survey's own verdict is that “the correlation is lower for topic-focused summarization like extractive meeting summarization” — and it reports two separate findings from Liu & Liu (2008) about exactly such tasks: cleaning the transcripts' disfluencies (repetitions, filler words) directly raises the ROUGE score itself, while it's a different intervention — including the speaker information of the extracted sentences — that the paper credits with improving ROUGE's correlation with human judgment.

For machine-learned metrics, the survey's clearest quantitative evidence concerns robustness rather than raw correlation: it reports that BLEURT's authors (Sellam et al., 2020) found that training-set skew "has a disastrous effect on BLEURT without pre-training," while the synthetic pre-training step "makes BLEURT significantly more robust to quality drifts" — the survey's own Figure 9, reproduced below as Figure 4, shows Kendall's τ against human ratings falling sharply as test-set skew increases for the no-pretrain variant, while the pretrained variant degrades more gently and stays closer to BERTScore's line across the same skew range. The survey folds this into a broader point about factual correctness, a dimension none of the overlap or embedding metrics above are built to check: it reports that abstractive summarization models have been shown to produce factually inconsistent output at rates up to 30% (citing Kryscinski et al., 2019b; Falke et al., 2019; Zhu et al., 2020), and that Maynez et al. (2020) found over 70% of generated single-sentence summaries contained hallucinations — a finding the survey notes held across several different summarization modeling approaches, not just one.

The paper's two case studies apply this taxonomy to specific tasks. For document summarization, it extends Steinberger & Jezek's (2009) taxonomy (Figure 5) into intrinsic evaluation — split into content (mostly ROUGE), text quality (coherence, readability, focus, informativeness), and factual consistency — and extrinsic evaluation (using the summary for a downstream task like question answering). For long-text generation, the case study is qualitative: comparing sample outputs from Grover (Zellers et al., 2019) and PlotMachines (Rashkin et al., 2020) — both reproduced from Rashkin et al.'s (2020) own side-by-side comparison — against a shared outline of six plot points, the survey observes that Grover tends to finish one story and restart a new one partway through a document (often re-inserting a "1:" section-header token it picked up from training data), while PlotMachines more consistently follows a single beginning-middle-end arc — illustrating why the survey argues that discourse-level structure needs its own evaluation criteria (inter-sentence and inter-paragraph coherence) beyond what sentence-level metrics like BLEU or BERTScore can capture.

// does it hold up?

The eval read

As a narrative survey rather than a systematic review, the paper never states a search protocol or inclusion criteria — no PRISMA-style flow diagram, no stated keyword search or date cutoff, no accounting for what was excluded and why. That's normal for the genre, but it means the reader has to trust the authors' coverage judgment rather than reproduce it; a metric or finding's absence from the survey isn't evidence it doesn't exist. Most of the survey's comparative claims about individual metrics (BLEU's outlier sensitivity, ROUGE's disfluency problem, BLEURT's skew robustness) are also secondhand: they're each drawn from a single external paper's own reported experiment rather than a head-to-head comparison the authors ran themselves under a shared protocol. That's a reasonable choice for a survey of this scope, but it means the reported numbers aren't necessarily comparable to each other — a BLEU correlation coefficient from one paper's dataset and setup is not directly commensurable with a BERTScore correlation coefficient from a different paper's.

The clearest sign of how fast the underlying field was moving is the survey itself: it needed a substantial revision within eleven months of its original posting to stay current, adding coverage of the GEM benchmark, the GENIE leaderboard, and Bender et al.'s Stochastic Parrots critique — three things that didn't exist when v1 was written. That churn cuts both ways as an eval read: it shows a survey engaged enough with the field to update, but it also means the underlying "map" is a moving target, and even the May-2021 v2 has a dating blind spot of its own. Across all six categories of machine-learned metrics it surveys (sentence-embedding similarity, regression-based, human-judgment-trained, BERT-based, factual-correctness, and composite), every example is a small, purpose-built or purpose-trained component — a fine-tuned encoder like BERTScore, BLEURT, ADEM, RUSE, or the comparator evaluator; an entailment or information-extraction classifier, as in Section 4.5's factual-correctness models; or a learned combination of existing metric scores, as in Section 4.6's composite metrics — never a general-purpose language model simply prompted to judge. Searching the staged v2 text, GPT-3 (Brown et al., 2020) is cited twice in the body, both times in the introduction as an example of a fluent generator ("can generate long texts that are almost indistinguishable from human-generated texts"); it never resurfaces in Section 4's discussion of machine-learned evaluation metrics, and nothing in that section discusses prompting a large pretrained language model directly as a judge. That's an accurate description of where the field stood in mid-2021, not a flaw in the writing, but it means a reader coming to this survey today for a map of "machine-learned evaluation" is getting the pre-LLM-as-judge half of that map only.

baselines

Most quantitative claims (BLEU's outlier sensitivity, ROUGE's disfluency problem, BLEURT's skew robustness) are imported from a single external paper's own experimental setup rather than run head-to-head by the survey's authors — reasonable for a survey, but it means cross-metric comparisons in the prose are not apples-to-apples unless the source papers happened to share a test bed. Even Table 3, the survey's own worked example, isn't independent of outside material: the paper credits its examples as "adapted from Liu et al. (2016) and Kilickaya et al. (2017)."

contamination

Not applicable in the usual train/test sense — this is a survey, not a trained model — but the eleven-month v1→v2 revision is itself evidence that the survey's own claims have a short shelf life relative to the field's pace.

what's missed

No stated search protocol or inclusion criteria; the "machine-learned metrics" category, even in the 2021 revision, covers only purpose-trained/fine-tuned scoring models and never discusses prompting a general-purpose LLM as an evaluator.

would raise confidence

A stated search methodology; a single shared-test-bed comparison table of correlation coefficients across the metrics it surveys (rather than one coefficient per metric drawn from a different paper's setup each time); a further revision covering LLM-as-judge protocols.

// bottom line

The survey's own worked example — BLEU/ROUGE/WMD all scoring a plausible dialog response 0.0 against a reference it shares no words with — is a more convincing argument against overlap metrics than any of the borrowed correlation numbers around it. Not because it's independent of outside material (its examples are themselves adapted from Liu et al., 2016, and Kilickaya et al., 2017), but because the specific claim is simple enough — a response sharing zero words with its reference — to check directly against the reproduced table, rather than trusting a correlation coefficient computed under some other paper's separate experimental setup. The taxonomy itself (human-centric / untrained-automatic / machine-learned) has held up as a standard reference framework; the specific metric-by-metric verdicts are a snapshot of where the literature stood as of a May-2021 revision, already missing the LLM-as-judge paradigm that would dominate the next several years of this same subfield.

// takeaways

What to remember

  1. Three categories, one taxonomy: human-centric evaluation (intrinsic/extrinsic, with agreement measured by percent agreement, Cohen's κ, Fleiss' κ, or the chance-corrected, weightable Krippendorff's α), untrained automatic metrics (n-gram overlap, distance-based, diversity, content-overlap, syntactic), and machine-learned metrics (embedding similarity, regression-based, human-judgment-trained, BERT-based, factual-correctness, composite).
  2. The survey's own Table 3 example is its sharpest evidence against overlap metrics: a plausible dialog response that shares no words with its reference scores BLEU/ROUGE/WMD 0.0 — indistinguishable from nonsense.
  3. Factual correctness is a documented blind spot for the metrics above it: abstractive summarizers have been reported to produce factually inconsistent output up to 30% of the time, and Maynez et al. (2020) found hallucinations in over 70% of generated single-sentence summaries across multiple modeling approaches.
  4. BLEURT's two-stage fine-tuning (synthetic pairs first, then human ratings) is the survey's central robustness case study: without the synthetic pretraining stage, its correlation with human ratings collapses as train/test skew increases; with it, the metric degrades far more gently.
  5. The paper's own eleven-month revision cycle (June 2020 → May 2021, adding GEM, GENIE, and Stochastic Parrots) is a built-in demonstration of how quickly NLG-evaluation surveys go stale — and even the revised version predates LLM-as-judge, this subfield's next major shift.
