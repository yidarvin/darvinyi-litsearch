KdConv: A Chinese Multi-domain Dialogue Dataset Towards Multi-turn Knowledge-driven Conversation

A 4,500-dialogue, 85,596-utterance Chinese benchmark that grounds long, open-ended, multi-topic conversations — film, music, and travel domains — in domain-specific knowledge graphs, with every utterance sentence-level annotated to the exact knowledge triples it draws on.

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

By 2020, knowledge-grounded conversation datasets split into two camps, and each left a real gap. Corpora built from unstructured text — CMU DoG (Zhou et al., 2018), which grounds two-party chat in Wikipedia movie articles; Wizard of Wikipedia, which retrieves candidate passages from a much larger pool of dialogue topics; India DoG, which adds fact tables — give a model prose to draw on, but no explicit, checkable structure. Two of the three — CMU DoG and India DoG — restrict a conversation to a single topic (Table 1: both average 1.0 topics per dialogue); Wizard of Wikipedia averages 2.0, so none of the three unstructured-text corpora exceeds two topics per dialogue. Corpora built from knowledge graphs did better on structure but worse on topic breadth: OpenDialKG grounds chit-chat in Freebase triples but likewise averages just 1.0 topic across a short 5.8 turns; DuConv — "to the best of our knowledge... the only existing Chinese human-labeled knowledge-grounded dialogue dataset," per the authors — samples two linked knowledge-graph entities up front, a transitional topic and a fixed goal topic, and scripts the conversation toward that target path, which caps topic diversity at 2.0 and, the authors argue, is unrealistic: real speakers rarely commit to a conversation's final topic in advance.

The gap KdConv targets is a dataset where two-party conversations are genuinely open-ended (no scripted goal path), range from one to four topics per dialogue instead of being capped at one or two, are long enough for multi-hop topic transitions to actually occur (KdConv's own average is 19.0 turns per dialogue — longer than any of the knowledge-graph-grounded corpora it compares against, OpenDialKG's 5.8 and DuConv's 9.1, though still short of CMU DoG's 22.6, which is unstructured-text- rather than graph-grounded), and carry sentence-level annotation tying each individual utterance to the specific knowledge triples it draws on, rather than one knowledge-graph label for an entire conversation. A second, related gap is multi-domain coverage under one consistent annotation scheme: film, music (similar domains), and travel (a dissimilar one), so that knowledge adaptation and transfer learning between domains become directly studyable. OpenDialKG is the one prior corpus that also combines a knowledge graph with sentence-level annotation across multiple domains (film, book, sport, music) — but caps every dialogue at a single topic and averages only 5.8 turns; none of the five prior corpora combines that multi-domain, sentence-level graph annotation with KdConv's longer, genuinely multi-topic (avg. 2.3, up to 4) conversations.

// how it works

The method

Building a domain-specific knowledge graph

Rather than tackle open-domain background knowledge directly — the authors note ConceptNet alone holds over 8 million concepts and 21 million relations, far too large to ground conversations against from scratch — KdConv narrows the scope to three domains. For each, the authors crawled a set of popular "start entities": top-ranked films and film stars, top-ranked songs and singers, and popular travel attractions, from Douban Movie/Music and Qunar respectively. After filtering out start entities with too few associated knowledge triples, 559 film, 421 music, and 476 travel start entities remained. For film and music, the authors then expanded three hops outward from these seeds using XLORE, a large-scale English-Chinese bilingual knowledge graph, merging the retrieved neighbor entities and relations into a domain-specific graph; for travel, XLORE's coverage was too sparse, so that domain's graph was built entirely from web-crawled knowledge instead. The resulting knowledge graphs (Table 2) total 13,072 entities (1,456 start entities plus 11,615 entities reached through expansion), 9,115 distinct relation types, and 157,029 knowledge triples across the three domains — film alone contributes 7,477 entities and 89,618 triples, versus travel's much sparser 1,154 entities, 7 relation types, and 10,973 triples.

Collecting the conversations

Crowdsourced worker pairs then chatted with both parties given access to the domain knowledge graph — a deliberate contrast with Wizard of Wikipedia's asymmetric design, where only one worker sees a retrieval system surfacing relevant passages. Workers could dynamically swap between leading and following the conversation, were told to start from one of the graph's start entities, and were encouraged — not required — to shift the topic toward other connected entities, with no predefined goal or target path (a deliberate contrast with DuConv's fixed transitional-topic/goal-topic path). Whenever an utterance drew on a specific knowledge triple, the annotator recorded which triple(s) were used, producing the dataset's signature sentence-level knowledge annotation. Low-quality dialogues — containing grammatical errors or inconsistencies with the knowledge facts — were filtered out; the retained corpus's distinct-4 diversity score is 0.54 (film), 0.51 (music), and 0.42 (travel), which the authors report as comparable to DuConv's 0.46. The paper does not state how many crowdworkers were recruited for this collection stage, nor report any inter-annotator agreement or other quality-control statistic for the raw dialogue-and-triple annotation itself (as distinct from the later human evaluation of model outputs, which does report one — see below) — a real gap in what can be checked about how consistently different annotators applied the "record the triple you used" instruction.

The result (Table 3): 4,500 dialogues, 1,500 per domain, each domain split 1,200/150/150 into train/dev/test (8:1:1, as stated in the text — the paper does not specify whether this split is dialogue-random or holds any knowledge-graph entity disjoint between train and test); 85,596 utterances total (36,618 film, 24,885 music, 24,093 travel); an average of 19.0 utterances per dialogue (24.4 film, 16.6 music, 16.1 travel) and 2.3 topics per dialogue (2.6 film, 2.1 music, 2.2 travel). "Topics" are operationally defined as the distinct head entities among a dialogue's annotated knowledge triples that are also central nodes — knowledge-graph degree greater than 1. Figure 2 shows, per domain, how many dialogues discuss at least k = 2, 3, or 4 topics within the first n conversation turns: across all three domains, most dialogues reach 2 topics only once they pass roughly 15 turns, and 3–4-topic dialogues become common only past 20 turns — evidence, in the authors' reading, that topic diversity needs conversation length to emerge.

Table 4 lists the top-3 most frequent topic-transition patterns in the film domain at 1-, 2-, and 3-hop depth (e.g. 1-hop: T1 –Major Work→ T2, T1 –Star→ T2, T1 –Director→ T2). Transitions overwhelmingly follow structured knowledge-graph relations rather than the catch-all "Information" relation, which points to unstructured background text describing an entity; the low frequency of "Information" transitions is read by the authors as evidence that, when a graph relation is available, people prefer to pivot the conversation along it rather than through free text.

Benchmark models

Four base architectures are evaluated: a plain language model (LM), tested only without knowledge access; and three architectures each tested both with and without a knowledge module — Seq2Seq, an attention-based encoder-decoder generating the k-th utterance from the previous k−1 (k=8); HRED, a hierarchical encoder-decoder with an explicit context RNN over prior utterances; and BERT, used as a retrieval-based ranker over 10 BM25-retrieved candidates (including the gold response) per turn, following the keyword-extraction recipe of Wu et al. (2017)'s Sequential Matching Network. Knowledge is injected via a key-value memory module (Miller et al., 2016) over the triples mentioned in that dialogue, attended to with a query vector — defined only for Seq2Seq (its encoder's final hidden state), HRED (its context vector), and BERT (its <CLS> vector); LM has no such query point and so has no knowledge-conditioned variant in the paper — and, critically, trained with an auxiliary loss Latt that directly supervises the memory's attention weights against the gold sentence-level triple annotation:

Latt = −1|{truth}| Σi∈{truth} log αi // forces the memory's attention α over candidate triples toward the triples actually used in the gold response

This attention-supervision loss is a training signal, not an evaluation metric — no automatic metric in the paper's own evaluation directly scores whether a generated response used the correct knowledge triple (see the critique section).

The results

Note that KdConv is not the longest corpus by raw turn count — CMU DoG's 22.6 average turns exceeds KdConv's 19.0 — but among the graph-grounded corpora (OpenDialKG, DuConv, KdConv), KdConv's turns and topic-diversity are both the highest. OpenDialKG is graph-grounded, sentence-annotated, and multi-domain too (film, book, sport, music), so KdConv's real distinction over it isn't domain span or annotation level — it's averaging more than double OpenDialKG's topics per dialogue (2.3 vs. 1.0) over more than triple the turns (19.0 vs. 5.8).

On the benchmark models (Table 5: all seven rows — LM, Seq2Seq, HRED, BERT, and the three "+know" variants — report all 11 metric columns per domain, with one exception: PPL is defined only for the five generative rows and prints as "-" for BERT/BERT+know, since retrieval outputs aren't scored by a language-model perplexity — generative models are instead ranked for Hits@1/3 by candidate PPL, per the paper's own footnote), the paper's own headline claim is that "after introducing the knowledge, all the models were improved in terms of all the metrics except PPL in all the domains" — true for the large majority of the grid (85 of the 90 non-PPL model×metric×domain cells across Seq2Seq/HRED/BERT strictly improve) but not literally universal: sweeping every base-vs-+know pair surfaces four strict decreases and one exact tie — Film HRED's Hits@3 (40.62 → 39.79), Film BERT's Hits@3 (91.79 → 91.79, an exact tie), Music HRED's BLEU-1 (29.92 → 29.73), Music BERT's Hits@3 (86.90 → 86.87), and Travel HRED's Distinct-1 (4.15 → 3.98). Two of those five are BERT rows, which deserve an asterisk: the paper freezes the transformer weights when training BERT+know's knowledge modules — “the parameters of the transformers were frozen during training the knowledge related modules… to exclude the impact of the deep transformers but only examine the potential effects introduced by the background knowledge” — so those rows are a memory-module ablation on a fixed encoder, not an end-to-end comparison. Even narrowed to just the generation-model metrics the ablation is really about (BLEU-k/Distinct-k), there are two counter-examples, not one: Music HRED's BLEU-1 and Travel HRED's Distinct-1. Everywhere else the direction the paper claims holds, including the three specific deltas it calls out in the text, all of which check out exactly: BERT's Hits@1 improves by 0.4 in the music domain (55.64 → 56.08, difference 0.44); Seq2Seq's BLEU-4 improves by 7.2 in the travel domain (11.74 → 18.94); and HRED's Distinct-4 improves by 12.4 in the music domain (20.97 → 33.37).

Which specific model wins a given column also shifts by domain — the paper doesn't claim a single model dominates everywhere. In the film domain, knowledge-aware Seq2Seq (Seq2Seq+know) has the best Hits@1/3 among generative models (17.77/41.66) and knowledge-aware HRED (HRED+know) sweeps every BLEU-k and Distinct-k column; in music, HRED+know still wins BLEU-2/3/4 but knowledge-aware Seq2Seq wins Distinct-1–4 instead, and plain HRED (no knowledge) edges out HRED+know on BLEU-1 specifically (one of the five non-improving cells noted above); in travel, Seq2Seq+know sweeps Hits@1/3, BLEU-1–4, and Distinct-1–3, with HRED+know winning only Distinct-4. The plain LM baseline has the lowest perplexity of the five generative models in every domain — a real, correctly-scoped result (it is genuinely the best of the five on that one column), though a lower-entropy, dialogue-history-blind model having the lowest PPL is an expected and not very informative pattern rather than evidence LM is a strong model overall. Knowledge-aware BERT is the best performer on most columns overall in every domain, since it retrieves an actual human-written candidate rather than generating one from scratch. Across domains, retrieval performance is best in film and worst in travel — tracking corpus size (36,618 vs. 24,093 utterances) — while generation performance actually improves from film to travel, which the authors attribute to travel's shorter dialogues (16.1 vs. film's 24.4 average utterances per dialogue) being an easier generation target, despite travel's far sparser knowledge graph (699 entities, 7 relation types, versus film's 1,837 entities and 318 relation types).

A separate manual evaluation (Table 6) samples about 500 test contexts total, drawn across the three domains' test sets combined (not 500 per domain), and generates a response to each from three models — HRED, HRED+know, and BERT+know (plain BERT was dropped as "performing similarly to knowledge-aware BERT" per the paper) — for 500 × 3 = 1,500 context-response pairs total, which three human annotators then rate on a 0–2 fluency and 0–2 coherence scale. Inter-annotator Fleiss' kappa ranges from 0.37 (music, fluency) to 0.74 (travel, coherence); raw 3-of-3 agreement ranges 68.14%–81.33% across the three domains (reported in the text, not broken out per-domain in the table itself). Knowledge-aware BERT scores highest on both metrics in all three domains — fluency exactly 2.00 in every domain, since its outputs are always real human-written sentences, and coherence 1.79/1.80/1.76 (film/music/travel). Adding knowledge to HRED raises its coherence score in every domain (e.g. travel: 1.10 → 1.31), which the paper reads as evidence that knowledge grounding is legible to human judges even where the automatic BLEU/Distinct metrics are a much weaker proxy for it (see critique).

// does it hold up?

The eval read

KdConv's real contribution is the corpus and its sentence-level triple annotation; the four benchmark architectures are a floor to show the data is usable, not a state-of-the-art claim, and the paper's own framing supports reading it that way. Several aspects of the collection and evaluation design are worth a closer look for anyone building on the dataset in 2026.

The dialogues are crowdsourced against a knowledge graph the workers can see in full, which raises a real question the paper doesn't directly address: how much of the "natural conversation" is workers exploring the graph itself — effectively narrating triples in dialogue form — versus organically recalling shared background knowledge the way two people who both happen to know a topic would talk about it. The paper's evidence for naturalness is a distinct-4 diversity score (0.54/0.51/0.42 for film/music/travel) compared favorably to DuConv's 0.46 — a lexical-diversity proxy, not a naturalness judgment, and one dataset beating another by roughly this margin doesn't itself establish that either corpus reads as natural human conversation rather than fluent knowledge transcription. The manual evaluation's fluency scores (HRED+know: 1.78–1.86 out of 2 across domains; BERT+know: 2.00, because it retrieves real sentences) are the closest the paper comes to a naturalness check, but that's a rating of individual model outputs, not of the underlying human-authored dialogues themselves — there is no reported human judgment of whether the original crowdsourced conversations read as natural rather than as knowledge-graph walkthroughs.

The paper is also silent on basic annotator/QC statistics for the dialogue-collection stage itself: it never states how many crowdworkers were recruited, and reports no inter-annotator agreement or spot-check statistic for how consistently different worker pairs applied the "record the triple you used" instruction (the only reported agreement statistic — Fleiss' kappa 0.37–0.74, 3 annotators — covers the later manual evaluation of model outputs, a different task from the dialogue construction). "Low-quality dialogues" were filtered for "grammatical errors" and "inconsistencies of knowledge facts," but no count of how many dialogues were rejected, nor any measured triple-annotation accuracy, is given.

baselines

LM, Seq2Seq, HRED, and a fine-tuned-BERT retrieval ranker were a reasonable 2020 toolkit, but all four generation architectures pre-date pretrained transformer decoders — no GPT-2/BART/T5-class generator, with or without the knowledge module, is tested. BERT itself is used only as a retrieval ranker over BM25 candidates, never as a generator. Six years on, these numbers are a floor for what any pretrained generative model conditioned the same way should be able to beat, not a ceiling to compare against.

contamination

The paper states only that train/validation/test is split 8:1:1 by dialogue and never says whether the split holds knowledge-graph entities disjoint across the three sets. With only 1,456 total start entities feeding 4,500 dialogues, and no reported entity-level split statistic, it is likely that many test-set entities and triples also appear in training dialogues — which would let a model's apparent "knowledge grounding" partly reflect memorized entity-triple pairs rather than genuine graph-reasoning generalization to unseen knowledge. This is a plausible, unaddressed gap in the paper's own reporting, not a demonstrated leak (the paper simply doesn't report the statistic either way).

what's missed

None of the automatic metrics (Hits@1/3, PPL, BLEU-k, Distinct-k) directly measures whether a response actually used the correct knowledge triple — they're generic fluency/diversity/retrieval-ranking metrics repurposed from non-knowledge-grounded dialogue work. The one knowledge-specific signal in the whole pipeline, the attention-supervision loss Latt, is a training objective, never reported as an evaluation number (e.g., no "attention accuracy against gold triples" metric at test time). The closest thing to a knowledge-grounding-specific signal is the manual evaluation's "coherence" dimension — human-rated on only ~500 sampled contexts total across all three domains combined (1,500 context-response pairs after multiplying by 3 models), which works out to roughly 167 rated contexts per domain per model — a thinner sample than a "500 per domain" reading would suggest, and a real but thin substitute for an automatic, scalable knowledge-faithfulness metric.

would raise confidence

A reported entity-disjoint (or at least entity-overlap-quantified) train/test split; a stated annotator count and an inter-annotator agreement statistic for the dialogue-and-triple-annotation stage itself, not just for the later model-output evaluation; and a real automatic knowledge-grounding metric — e.g. triple-recall or triple-precision of a generated response against the gold annotation — rather than relying on BLEU/Distinct as proxies for whether knowledge was actually used correctly.

// bottom line

KdConv is a genuinely useful artifact for 2020's knowledge-grounded dialogue landscape: it's the first Chinese corpus to pair a knowledge graph with sentence-level triple annotation across more than one domain, and its topic-transition analysis (Table 4, Figure 2) is a real methodological contribution beyond the raw data. But its own headline claim that knowledge improves "all the metrics" in "all the domains" doesn't hold at the cell level: a full sweep of Table 5 finds four strict decreases and one exact tie across Seq2Seq/HRED/BERT (Film HRED Hits@3, Film BERT Hits@3 tied, Music HRED BLEU-1, Music BERT Hits@3, Travel HRED Distinct-1) — a real, if narrow, overstatement rather than a fabrication, and worth knowing before quoting the paper's "all metrics" line verbatim. More consequentially for anyone building on the dataset today: the paper reports no entity-level train/test split statistic and no data-collection-stage quality-control statistic, and none of its automatic metrics directly checks knowledge faithfulness — all three gaps a 2026 reader benchmarking against KdConv would want closed before trusting a new model's numbers on it.

// takeaways

What to remember

  1. KdConv is a 4,500-dialogue, 85,596-utterance Chinese benchmark across three domains (film, music, travel — 1,500 dialogues each), grounding open-ended, multi-topic (avg. 2.3, up to 4) conversations in domain-specific knowledge graphs (13,072 entities, 157,029 triples total) with sentence-level annotation tying each utterance to the exact knowledge triples it used — unlike prior corpora that cap topics at one or two, or annotate knowledge only at the whole-dialogue level.
  2. Seq2Seq, HRED, and BERT (LM has no knowledge-conditioned variant) each get a knowledge-module version, and adding it improves the large majority of Table 5's cells in every domain — but not literally all of them: a full sweep finds four strict decreases and one exact tie (Film HRED Hits@3; Film BERT Hits@3, tied; Music HRED BLEU-1; Music BERT Hits@3; Travel HRED Distinct-1), a real exception to the paper's own "all metrics" claim. Which specific model wins which metric column also shifts by domain, so there is no single "best knowledge-aware model" across the board.
  3. The paper doesn't report an entity-level train/test split statistic, an annotator count or agreement figure for the dialogue-collection stage, or any automatic metric that directly checks whether a generated response used the correct knowledge triple — three real gaps to keep in mind when citing KdConv's benchmark numbers or building new evaluations on top of the dataset.
