Understanding by Understanding Not: Modeling Negation in Language Models

BERT predicts the same top answer for "The capital of Cuba is [MASK]" and "The capital of Cuba is not [MASK]" — negation barely moves its output. This paper mines negated sentences from Wikipedia and trains BERT with an unlikelihood objective, cutting mean error on Kassner & Schütze's negated-LAMA probe from about 9% to under 4% while leaving un-negated LAMA scores essentially untouched, with its clearest downstream gain on the low-resource RTE-with-negation split.

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

Negation is a small, closed-class grammatical device — insert or remove a "not" — but it inverts the truth of a sentence, and pre-trained language models handle it badly. Kassner & Schütze (2019) established the canonical demonstration: PLMs cannot correctly distinguish between the negated and non-negated forms of fill-in-the-blank tests. Asked to predict the [MASK] token in "The capital of Cuba is [MASK]" and "The capital of Cuba is not [MASK]," BERT often generates the same answer, "Havana," for both — treating the positive and negated cloze prompts as functionally the same query. The model has clearly stored the fact that Cuba's capital is Havana, but it has not learned that "not" should flip which continuations are likely — it is, in the authors' framing, ignoring the negation cue and answering from the subject alone.

Prior work on this gap split into two camps that didn't quite meet: diagnostic papers (Kassner & Schütze's negated-and-misprimed-probes benchmark; Naik et al.'s NLI stress tests; Hossain et al.'s negation-augmented NLI splits) that measured the failure without proposing a general training-time fix, and a separate literature on negation *scope* and *focus* detection (Fancellu et al.; Li & Lu; Zou et al.) that treats negation as a standalone parsing subtask rather than something a language model's own predictive distribution should get right. Nobody had shown a training objective that directly targets a pre-trained LM's negation blindness — using the negated-LAMA benchmark and negation-augmented NLI splits purely as held-out evaluation, with no negated-LAMA examples in the training loop — while leaving the model's ordinary, un-negated factual recall intact. That is the gap this paper closes: a data-augmentation-plus-loss recipe that fine-tunes BERT to distinguish negated from non-negated continuations, tested strictly out-of-distribution against the two benchmarks that diagnosed the problem in the first place.

// how it works

The method

The recipe has two parts: a way to manufacture negated sentences at scale, and a loss that uses them to directly suppress the wrong completion. First, a syntactic negation augmentation pipeline: sentences mined from Wikipedia are parsed with Stanza (dependency tree, POS tags, morphology, capped at 20 words), then matched against a hand-built set of Semgrex dependency-tree patterns — one rule per syntactic construction (simple past, simple present, imperative, present with auxiliary or modal, past perfect, copula statements, already-negated-with-not, NPI words like "anywhere"/"anyone," and a handful of others) — and transformed by a small action language (move, replace, insert, lemmatize) into a grammatically negated version, with one token flagged as the unlikelihood token, i.e. the word that should now become improbable. Coverage: of 930 sampled Wikipedia sentences, 899 (96.7%) matched some rule; of 100 sentences manually reviewed by a native English speaker, 94% were correctly negated.

Second, unlikelihood training with reference (Fig. 1). Applying an unlikelihood penalty to a random negated sentence in isolation is unreliable — many negated statements aren't grounded in any fact the model could be expected to know — so each training example is a pair <sentence A, sentence B>, where A is left untouched as a factual reference and B is A's negated form with the unlikelihood token replaced by [MASK]. The pair is concatenated and fed to BERT, and the unlikelihood loss penalizes the probability BERT assigns the (now-false) token at that mask position, conditioned on the true sentence A sitting right there as context.

LUL(xu) = −log(1 − p(xu|x1:T)) // x_u = the unlikelihood token (e.g. "improvements"); x_1:T = reference sentence A concatenated with masked negated sentence B

To keep the model from simply learning "always predict something implausible" and forgetting how to do ordinary masked-language-modeling, every unlikelihood example is balanced with a non-contradictory pair where B is just a copy of A with one token masked, and a knowledge-distillation (KL-divergence) loss pulls BERT's prediction there back toward its own pre-fine-tuning distribution. A second KD step, run against unmodified Wikipedia sentences with no reference-pair structure, further guards against catastrophic forgetting of general language modeling. The two losses (unlikelihood and KD on the contrastive pairs) are combined with weight γ = 0.4, and the model — BERTNOT, a BERT-base-cased checkpoint further trained for 5 epochs with 20k examples per objective, batch size 32, learning rate 1e-5 — never sees a single negated-LAMA or negation-NLI example during training; those are reserved entirely for evaluation.

// what they found

The results

On negated LAMA (Kassner & Schütze's four-subset cloze probe — SQuAD, ConceptNet, T-REx, Google-RE), mean top-1 error drops from 9.01% for plain pre-trained BERT (averaging the paper's own re-run baseline: 8.61 / 2.24 / 21.42 / 3.76 across the four subsets) to 3.95% for BERTNOT (2.10 / 0.73 / 11.86 / 1.10) — BERTNOT wins all four subsets outright. That 5.06-point drop is exactly the paper's own headline: "reduce the mean top 1 error rate to 4% on the negated LAMA dataset," restated in the introduction as "a mean error rate of 4% (a improvement of 5 points)." The comparison is against un-fine-tuned BERT specifically, not the BERT+KL ablation (which reaches 7.98% on its own, showing the knowledge-distillation half of the objective does some of the work by itself — the unlikelihood term is responsible for roughly 4 of the remaining ~5.1 points).

Crucially, this doesn't come at the cost of the model's ordinary factual recall: on original (un-negated) LAMA (Table 1, precision@1, higher is better), BERTNOT's mean across the same four subsets is 17.26 versus 17.13 for plain BERT — flat to marginally better, not degraded. BERTNOT does lose one individual column (ConceptNet: 15.49 vs. BERT's 15.65), so it isn't a clean sweep of every subset, but there is no broad recall cost from suppressing negated completions. Table 3 shows this qualitatively: for "Mac OS is developed by [MASK].", both BERT and BERTNOT still lead with Apple; for the negated form, BERT still predicts Apple — and is in fact more confident in it after negation (log-prob −1.9 → −1.3, roughly 1.8× the probability) — while BERTNOT's top prediction flips to Microsoft.

On the NLI transfer tests — RTE, SNLI, and MNLI, each fine-tuned on both its original dev split and Hossain et al.'s (2020) negation-augmented split — the picture is more mixed. RTE, the low-resource task, shows the largest single gain in Table 4: accuracy on the negation split jumps from 65.47% to 74.47% (+9.00 points), while original RTE dev is essentially flat (70.04% → 69.68%, −0.36). SNLI's negation split moves from 44.18% to 45.96% (+1.78), with SNLI dev flat (89.47% → 89.00%, −0.47). MNLI's negation split moves only 60.62% → 60.89% (+0.27 — smaller than the ±0.31 spread the paper itself reports for that cell over 3 runs; the paper doesn't say whether that ± is a standard deviation or a standard error), while MNLI dev actually improves 82.95% → 84.31% (+1.36). The authors attribute the shrinking gain on SNLI and MNLI to catastrophic forgetting during large-data fine-tuning eroding whatever negation-specific behavior BERTNOT's pre-training instilled; an appendix experiment that tries mixing the unlikelihood and KD objectives back in during MNLI fine-tuning does not help (81.17% dev / 60.20% w/neg, versus 81.42% / 62.00% without the extra objectives) — a fix the authors tried and explicitly report as unsuccessful.

// does it hold up?

The eval read

The sharpest way to read this paper is to separate what it actually tests from what "fixing negation" would require. Negated LAMA is a masked-cloze probe: the model sees a negated template and must not put high probability on the object that would complete the un-negated fact — precisely the distributional pattern the unlikelihood loss was built to suppress, using training data mined from the same kind of raw declarative sentences the LAMA templates are derived from. Near-elimination of error there (9.01% → 3.95%) is closer to "the training signal matches the test signal" than to an independent confirmation that BERT now understands negation as a general phenomenon. NLI is the genuine transfer test — negation appears embedded in premise/hypothesis pairs the model was never trained to negate — and there the gain is real but far narrower and uneven: a strong result on RTE, a small one on SNLI, and on MNLI a w/neg improvement (+0.27) smaller than the ±0.31 spread the paper reports for that cell over 3 runs (the paper never says whether ± denotes a standard deviation or a standard error) — and dwarfed by BERT's own MNLI w/neg spread of ±1.32, more than four times the size of the gain, so it is not distinguishable from run-to-run noise on the paper's own numbers. The paper's own hedge — "we also see some improvements on the negated NLI benchmarks" — is accurate; a page that upgraded it to "the method generalizes to natural language inference" would be overclaiming what MNLI actually shows.

One overclaim does slip into the paper's own prose, worth flagging precisely: §5.2/§6 say BERTNOT achieves its NLI gains "while keeping roughly the same scores on the original dev splits." That's accurate for RTE (−0.36) and SNLI (−0.47), but MNLI dev rises by +1.36 points — the third-largest magnitude change in Table 4 (behind RTE w/neg's +9.00 and SNLI w/neg's +1.78), the largest change on any original dev split, and five times the size of the MNLI negation-split gain (+0.27) it's supposed to be a control for. It's a minor inconsistency, not a load-bearing one, but it's the kind of self-contradiction between a summary sentence and its own table that's worth catching rather than repeating.

A gap the paper doesn't address at all: nothing in the text discusses whether the raw Wikipedia sentences mined for unlikelihood training might overlap, at the level of the underlying facts, with the specific subject–relation–object triples that negated LAMA probes (both are ultimately drawn from Wikipedia/Wikidata). Negated LAMA's cloze templates aren't literal running text, so exact-sentence leakage is unlikely, but fact-level leakage — training the model to suppress the negation of a fact it also happens to be evaluated on — is a live possibility the paper simply doesn't rule out or discuss.

baselines

Reasonable and cleanly ablated: BERT (no fine-tuning), BERT+KL (isolates the knowledge-distillation half of the objective), and BERTNOT (full method) let you attribute roughly 1.0 of the 5.06-point negated-LAMA gain to KD alone and the rest to the unlikelihood term. No comparison to Kassner & Schütze's own paper, which is a diagnosis rather than a competing mitigation, so there isn't an obvious prior fix to benchmark against.

contamination

Not discussed. The unlikelihood-training sentences and the negated-LAMA facts both trace back to Wikipedia/Wikidata; the paper never checks for fact-level overlap between the two, even though it explicitly and correctly avoids literal example-level leakage (no negated-LAMA or negation-NLI examples appear in training).

what's missed

Scale and architecture: everything beyond the appendix is BERT-base-cased; the one BERT-large experiment (Appendix E) shows a larger un-fine-tuned model starts more negation-blind (9.18% mean negated-LAMA error vs. BERT-base's 9.01%), and pushing BERTNOT-large harder with a higher learning rate improves negated LAMA only by degrading original LAMA — the method does not obviously scale even within the BERT family, let alone to decoder-only LLMs.

would raise confidence

A held-out negated-LAMA relation split with zero fact overlap with the unlikelihood training corpus; the same recipe applied to a larger or decoder-only model at comparable relative effort; and an NLI result with variance small enough that the MNLI negation-split gain isn't inside its own error bar.

// bottom line

Two papers now in this corpus — the Reversal Curse (LLMs trained on "A is B" fail to generalize to "B is A") and Physics of Language Models: Knowledge Manipulation (LLMs that store a fact still can't reliably invert, compare, or classify it) — document, years later and at vastly larger scale, the same family of failure this paper targets: a stored fact that the model cannot correctly transform. Neither paper re-tests this specific unlikelihood-with-reference recipe on a modern model, so the corpus evidence can't directly show the fix "failed to generalize" — nobody tried. What it does support is the weaker but more interesting reading: negation blindness wasn't an idiosyncrasy of BERT-base's masked-LM objective that a single, narrowly-targeted training patch was always going to close. The same shape of failure — correct storage, incorrect manipulation — keeps resurfacing across eras and architectures, which is evidence the underlying problem sits deeper than any one relation-specific fix, this one included.

// what to remember
  1. Mining negated sentences from raw text and training BERT with an unlikelihood objective, gated by a factual reference sentence, cuts mean negated-LAMA error from 9.01% to 3.95% (a ~5-point drop, matching the paper's own headline) without touching negated-LAMA or negation-NLI examples during training — and without degrading original-LAMA precision (17.13 → 17.26 mean P@1).
  2. The transfer signal is real but uneven: RTE-with-negation jumps 9.00 points, SNLI-with-negation 1.78, and MNLI-with-negation only 0.27 — smaller than the ±0.31 spread the paper reports for that cell, and dwarfed by BERT's own ±1.32 spread on the same MNLI w/neg metric — while the paper's claim of "roughly the same" original-task performance is accurate for RTE and SNLI but not MNLI, which actually improves by 1.36 points.
  3. The fix is BERT-base-specific and doesn't obviously scale: BERT-large starts out more negation-blind than BERT-base, and pushing BERTNOT-large harder only trades negated-LAMA gains for original-LAMA losses — a limitation the authors' own appendix surfaces but doesn't resolve, and one that fits a broader pattern of stored-fact-manipulation failures (reversal, inversion, negation) that recur at far larger scale in later work.
