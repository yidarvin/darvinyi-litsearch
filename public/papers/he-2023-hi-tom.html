Hi-ToM: A Benchmark for Evaluating Higher-Order Theory of Mind Reasoning in Large Language Models

A Sally-Anne-style narrative QA benchmark that pushes Theory-of-Mind evaluation past the 1st/2nd-order ceiling of prior datasets, asking LLMs to track nested beliefs up to the 4th order under optional deceptive agent communication — and finds joint accuracy, in the paper's own framing, falling from near-perfect to near-zero as the ToM order climbs from the zeroth to the fourth.

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

Theory of Mind (ToM) — reasoning about what others believe, including beliefs that are false — has been evaluated in LLMs almost entirely at the first and second order: template questions of the form "where does A1 think O is?" (1st order) or "where does A2 think A1 thinks O is?" (2nd order). Nematzadeh et al.'s ToM-QA (2018) and Le et al.'s ToMi (2019) — both Sally-Anne-style QA benchmarks generated from bAbI-style story templates — stop there. But higher-order ToM, third order and beyond, is what recursive social reasoning actually looks like outside the lab: multi-party negotiation, tacit coordination, and the kind of layered "They don't know that we know they know we know" inference the paper's own Figure 1 borrows from a scene in Friends. The authors argue this gap exists mainly because the automatic story-generation scripts used by prior work don't carry enough information in a short template to support a well-posed higher-order question.

Hi-ToM's contribution is a benchmark that extends the Sally-Anne generation pipeline to 0th through 4th-order questions on the same story, and — new relative to ToMi — adds agent communication (public and private statements between agents, some of them deliberately deceptive) as a mechanism to make the higher-order questions genuinely require tracking who told whom what, rather than just who saw what. The paper frames its central empirical claim plainly: claims that LLMs have genuine ToM ability (it cites Kosinski, 2023 and Bubeck et al., 2023's "Sparks of Artificial General Intelligence") are, in the paper's own words, "questionable, especially in the cases of higher-order ToM, where several rounds of recursive reasoning are required." A concurrent paper by Ma et al. (2023) surveyed the broader landscape of situated ToM benchmarks around the same time; Hi-ToM's distinguishing move is specifically the order ladder plus deception.

// how it works

The method

Each Hi-ToM story is built from a pool of 30 rooms, 37 objects, 39 containers, and 40 agents (Table 1), and features five agents per story. A story has one to three chapters; each chapter is an "entry" (agents enter a room and observe everything in it), followed by optional sequential object movements, followed by optional outside-the-room agent communication — public statements heard by everyone, or private statements heard only by the listener, and the paper deliberately makes some of this communicated information false (Figure 2's worked example has a character publicly claiming the milk is in the fridge when it is not). Random distractor sentences (e.g. "Jack likes the red_container") are also inserted to reduce template regularity. Compared to ToM/ToM-easy and ToMi, Hi-ToM stories are longer and structurally richer on average (26.47 lines / 5 agents / 7.39 containers per story, vs. 15.05/3.22/5 for ToM/ToM-easy and 8.86/2.75/2 for ToMi per Table 4), and the correct answer is spread roughly evenly across the story rather than concentrated near the end — 28.7%, 27.2%, 18.8%, and 25.3% of correct answers fall in the story's first, second, third, and final quarter respectively.

Every story is paired with five multiple-choice questions of increasing order, all about the same object O (Table 3): 0th ("where is O really?"), 1st ("where does A1 think O is?"), 2nd ("where does A2 think A1 thinks O is?"), up through 4th ("where does A4 think A3 thinks A2 thinks A1 thinks O is?"), with A1–A4 randomly assigned from the five story agents.

Two prompting styles are tested: Vanilla Prompting (VP), where the model picks an answer with no explanation, and Chain-of-Thought Prompting (CoTP), where it must reason step by step first. Four LLMs are evaluated zero-shot: GPT-3.5-turbo and GPT-4-32k (OpenAI, runs dated June 14–15, 2023), Claude-instant (Anthropic), and Guanaco-65B (an open-source LLaMA fine-tune, via QLoRA) — the paper does not date the latter two models' runs. Two metrics are reported: standard accuracy per question, and the stricter joint accuracy, adapted from ToMi.

JointAcc(order k, story) = 1  iff  every question of order 0…k in that same story is answered correctly, else 0 // a strict, cumulative metric: one wrong lower-order answer zeroes out every higher-order joint-accuracy score for that story, since a higher-order question is defined as "a recursive successor of the lower-order ones for the same story"
// what they found

The results

On standard accuracy (Table 5), averaged across both prompting styles and both deception conditions, GPT-4-32k leads at 58.99% overall, ahead of Claude-instant (46.00%), Guanaco-65B (32.17%), and GPT-3.5-turbo (31.50%) — notably, Guanaco's overall average edges out GPT-3.5-turbo's even though GPT-3.5-turbo posts sharply higher CoTP scores in both deception conditions (35.67 / 35.33 vs. Guanaco's 35.00 / 26.99); Guanaco's advantage comes entirely from its VP scores (33.33 / 33.33 vs. GPT-3.5-turbo's 28.67 / 26.33). Every model's row-level and grand-overall accuracy stays under 60%, which the paper reads as evidence that "Hi-ToM is challenging even for the most sophisticated LLMs" — though GPT-4-32k's own CoTP, no-deception cell (64.04%) does exceed 60% individually.

Chain-of-thought prompting barely helps, and sometimes hurts. Moving from VP to CoTP changes overall accuracy by only +0.66 (Claude), +1.77 (GPT-4), −2.34 (Guanaco), and a comparatively large +8.00 (GPT-3.5-turbo). On stories with deception specifically, CoTP makes three of the four models worse (Guanaco −6.34, Claude −1.67, GPT-4 −0.09), which the authors attribute to a deceptive detail derailing an early reasoning step and then cascading through the rest of the chain.

Two variables independently push joint accuracy down for GPT-4 and GPT-3.5: longer stories (more chapters) and higher ToM order, and — separately — deceptive communication. Isolating deception's effect for GPT-4 (Figure 4), the paper reports that going from 0 to 4 deceptive sentences per story costs 32%, 18.1%, 16%, and 11% of joint accuracy for the 1st, 2nd, 3rd, and 4th orders respectively — the largest hit is at the order LLMs otherwise handle best (1st).

The paper's error analysis adds two more findings. First, a position bias: for three-chapter stories, GPT-4 is correct 70% of the time when the answer is the last-mentioned container vs. 46% otherwise, and 62% of the time when it's the first-mentioned container vs. 38% otherwise — it tends to miss containers mentioned in the middle. Second, an answer-coincidence effect: GPT-4's own predicted answers at order 2/3/4 match its order-1 answer 72.4% / 64.6% / 59.8% of the time, well above the rate at which the correct answers actually coincide across orders in the dataset (30.9% / 20.9% / 22.2%) — a sign that some of GPT-4's higher-order "successes" may be a side effect of defaulting to its 1st-order guess rather than genuine recursive reasoning.

A manual review of 300 step-by-step CoTP responses (150 each from GPT-4 and GPT-3.5) sorts failures into five types: insufficient reasoning-depth (Table 6's example: the 3rd-order question "Where does Jack think Hannah thinks William thinks the carrot is?" gets the shortcut answer "green_box, as that's where Jack last saw it," collapsing three nested beliefs into a single, first-hand one), commonsense errors, hallucinations, temporal ignorance, and spurious causal inference. "As the ToM order increases, LLMs tend to demonstrate a higher frequency of errors" — true for four of the five categories, which climb fairly steadily from the zeroth to the fourth order; spurious causal inference is the exception, peaking at the 1st order (~0.12) before falling back and only partially recovering by the 4th (~0.06).

// does it hold up?

The eval read

Hi-ToM's synthetic construction is a real strength for contamination: with 30×37×39×40 story components and randomized generation, memorized answers are unlikely, and the paper is transparent that it's the first to add deceptive communication to ToM benchmarks. But the same template-based generation the paper leans on is exactly what a later benchmark, OpenToM (2024), singles out by name — citing Hi-ToM among the template-based benchmarks it names — as producing "overly-structured and ambiguous narratives". That's a second paper's judgment, not something Hi-ToM claims about itself, but it's worth reading alongside Hi-ToM's own framing of its stories as presenting "greater diversity and challenges" compared to previous datasets.

The bigger methodological question is what the joint accuracy metric is actually measuring. Because it's defined cumulatively — a story's order-k joint score requires every order 0…k question to be right — a single slip on an easy 1st-order question mechanically zeroes out that story's 2nd-, 3rd-, and 4th-order joint scores too, whether or not the model could have reasoned correctly at the higher order in isolation. The steep collapse in Figure 3 is therefore a mix of two effects the paper doesn't disentangle: genuinely harder recursive reasoning at higher orders, and the compounding of independent per-question error rates. The GPT-4 answer-coincidence finding (Section 6.1) is itself evidence the two are tangled: a model that just repeats its 1st-order guess would score better on plain accuracy at higher orders without doing any additional reasoning, while joint accuracy would punish it for any earlier misstep regardless of cause.

The model roster is also dated relative to a 2023 fast-moving field: the GPT-3.5-turbo and GPT-4-32k runs are dated June 14–15, 2023 (the paper does not date the Claude-instant or Guanaco-65B runs), and all four are run with the paper's stated "default parameter configurations" and no mention of repeated sampling, temperature control, or run-to-run variance. There's no human baseline reported, so a reader can't tell how far even the near-perfect 0th-order scores or the near-zero 4th-order joint-accuracy scores — GPT-4's own standard accuracy at the 4th order still ranges 0.2–0.6 across conditions — sit from a ceiling a careful human annotator would hit on the same stories. Table 5 also has an internal wrinkle worth reading cautiously: Guanaco's VP w/o-dec. and VP w/ dec. cells both print as exactly 33.33%, yet averaging Guanaco's own per-story-length breakdown in Appendix Figure 13 gives roughly 45% and 48% for those same two conditions — a far larger figure-vs-table gap than GPT-4 shows on its one directly comparable pair (Figure 10's CoTP w/o-dec. cells average ~63.7%, against Table 5's printed 64.04%). The paper doesn't address the discrepancy.

baselines

Four mid-2023 models (GPT-3.5-turbo, GPT-4-32k, Claude-instant, Guanaco-65B); only the two OpenAI runs are dated (June 14–15, 2023), and none of the four come with any mention of repeated sampling or run-to-run variance — no newer frontier models, no human upper bound for calibration.

contamination

Low risk: stories are synthetically generated from a large randomized component pool, not scraped or hand-written, and the paper releases its generation code rather than a fixed static set alone.

what's missed

Joint accuracy conflates "genuinely fails at deeper recursion" with "compounds an earlier, unrelated slip" because it requires every lower-order question in the same story to also be correct; the paper doesn't report a non-cumulative higher-order-only accuracy that would isolate the first effect.

would raise confidence

A human baseline on the same stories; repeated sampling to report variance; an ablation reporting order-k accuracy conditioned only on that question (not requiring the lower-order chain); and a rerun on post-2023 frontier models to see whether the collapse persists.

// bottom line

Hi-ToM is a genuine methodological step up from 1st/2nd-order ToM-QA and ToMi — it's the first to push the order ladder to four and to add deception — and its headline finding (accuracy and joint accuracy both fall sharply as order and story length increase, and further under deception) is well supported by the data. But the joint-accuracy metric's cumulative design means the paper's steepest-looking result is partly an artifact of how errors compound across a story rather than a clean measurement of higher-order reasoning in isolation, and the four-model, single-run, no-human-baseline setup limits how much the specific percentages should be trusted as a lasting yardstick.

// takeaways

What to remember

  1. Hi-ToM extends Sally-Anne-style ToM QA from the 1st/2nd-order ceiling of ToM-QA and ToMi to a full 0th–4th-order ladder on the same story, and is the first to layer in deceptive agent communication.
  2. All four tested LLMs stay under 60% overall accuracy, and joint accuracy — a stricter, cumulative metric — collapses toward zero by the 3rd/4th order, worse still with deceptive communication (a 32%/18.1%/16%/11% joint-accuracy drop for orders 1–4 as deception sentences rise from 0 to 4 for GPT-4) and with longer, multi-chapter stories.
  3. Chain-of-thought prompting gives only small, inconsistent gains (−2.34 to +8.00 across the four models) and actively hurts three of four models on deceptive stories — and because joint accuracy requires every lower-order answer in a story to also be right, the collapse at higher orders reflects both harder recursive reasoning and compounding earlier errors, a distinction the paper's own metric doesn't separate out.
