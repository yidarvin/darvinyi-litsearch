Survey of Hallucination in Natural Language Generation

Ji et al. canonicalize the intrinsic/extrinsic hallucination split — output that contradicts the source vs. output the source can neither confirm nor deny — across summarization, dialogue, question answering, data-to-text, translation and vision-language generation, then, across six later arXiv revisions, bolt on an entire section for hallucination in LLMs (labeled by the authors' own footnote as updated in Jan 2024). Now the field's most-cited reference for the taxonomy; the paper's own text admits the split isn't always clean.

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

By early 2022, natural language generation had improved fast on the strength of Transformer-based models — BERT, BART, GPT-2, GPT-3 — and that fluency had a side effect: these models routinely generate text that is nonsensical, or unfaithful to the source content they were supposed to be grounded in. Researchers had started calling this hallucination, borrowing the term from psychology (a "percept, experienced by a waking individual, in the absence of an appropriate stimulus from the extracorporeal world," per Blom's definition, which the paper quotes) because hallucinated text shares the same unsettling property: it feels real. The stakes are not abstract — the paper opens with two concrete harms: a hallucinatory patient-information summary in a medical setting, or a hallucinated instruction for a medicine generated by machine translation, both of which "could pose a risk to the patient" or worse. It also folds in a third, less obvious harm: Carlini et al.'s finding that language models can be prompted to regurgitate memorized personal data — email addresses, phone numbers — from their training corpus, which the survey frames as a form of hallucination too, since that private information is not "faithful" to the given input.

The trouble was that this literature had grown up in silos. Each downstream task — abstractive summarization, dialogue generation, generative question answering (GQA), data-to-text generation, machine translation — had accumulated its own hallucination papers, its own metrics, and its own vocabulary, with existing surveys covering only one slice at a time (the paper names prior surveys restricted to summarization and to translation). Terminology itself was inconsistent: "faithfulness," "factuality," "factual consistency," and "hallucination" were used interchangeably or with conflicting definitions depending on which paper you read. Nobody had synthesized a single cross-task account of what hallucination is, why it happens, how it's measured, and how it's mitigated. That synthesis — not a new experiment or a new model — is this paper's contribution.

// how it works

How the survey is organized

// version note

arXiv 2202.03629 exists in seven versions (v1–v7). This page is built from the staged v7 (14 Jul 2024, per the arXiv stamp), which runs 59 pages; v1 (Feb 2022) ran only 41 pages — roughly two-thirds v7's size. The source ↗ link above resolves to the same v7 by default, so there's no link/content mismatch, but a reader who knows "the hallucination survey" by reputation may know the smaller v1: about 7 of the 18 added pages are one addition, an entire new section on hallucination in large language models, which carries its own footnote reading "This section was updated in Jan 2024" — the paper's own label for material that did not exist when v1 was posted (its citations include Llama 2, GPT-4, and decoding methods like DoLa, all of which postdate Feb 2022); the rest of the growth is bibliography expansion (v7's reference list runs to 329 entries) and in-section additions elsewhere. Everything below is drawn from this v7 text; where a claim comes from that later-added section, it's noted as such.

The survey has three parts. Part one (Sections 2–6) lays a task-agnostic foundation: definitions and categorization, contributors to hallucination, metrics, mitigation methods, and future directions. Part two (Sections 7–12) works through six downstream tasks in turn — abstractive summarization, dialogue generation, GQA, data-to-text generation, machine translation, and vision-language generation — each with its own definition/metrics/mitigation/future-directions structure. Part three (Section 13, the 2024 addition) turns to hallucination specifically in LLMs.

Definitions and categorization (Section 2). "Following the categorization from previous works" — the paper is explicit that it borrows rather than coins the split — it adopts two hallucination types: intrinsic hallucination, generated output that contradicts the source content, and extrinsic hallucination, generated output that the source can neither support nor contradict. In the paper's own worked example (its Table 1), given the source "The first vaccine for Ebola was approved by the FDA in 2019 ... clinical trials have already started," a summary reading "The first Ebola vaccine was approved in 2021" is intrinsic (it directly contradicts "2019"), while a summary adding "China has already started clinical trials of the COVID-19 vaccine" is extrinsic (unstated by the source, but also not contradicted by it). Table 1 repeats this pairing across the survey's other unimodal tasks — task-oriented and open-domain dialogue, GQA, data-to-text (using a basketball box-score example), and machine translation — each with its own intrinsic/extrinsic example pair; the survey extends the same split to vision-language tasks too, but in Figures 2 and 3 rather than the table, per Table 1's own caption ("The examples for VL tasks are shown in Figure 2 and Figure 3"). Section 2.3 separately untangles faithfulness (staying consistent with the source, the antonym of hallucination) from factuality (being consistent with a broader notion of "fact"), noting that different cited authors define "fact" differently — Maynez et al. anchor it to world knowledge, Dong et al. to the source input itself — and that the survey adopts Maynez et al.'s reading.

Contributors to hallucination (Section 3). The survey splits causes into two families. From data: heuristic dataset construction that lets a target reference contain information absent from its paired source (e.g. WIKIBIO's targets are taken from Wikipedia's first sentence while the source is the infobox, and Dhingra et al. found 62% of those first sentences contain information the infobox doesn't), unfiltered duplicate examples that bias a model toward memorized-phrase repetition, and "innate divergence" — tasks like open-domain dialogue that intentionally reward diverse, not-strictly-source-grounded responses. From training and inference: imperfect encoder representations, decoders attending to the wrong part of the source or mixing up similar entities, exposure bias (the mismatch between teacher-forced training and autoregressive inference), and parametric knowledge learned during pretraining overriding what the current input actually says.

Metrics and mitigation (Sections 4–5). Metrics are grouped into statistical (n-gram-overlap methods like PARENT, which — unlike ROUGE/BLEU — uses both source and target as reference), model-based (five families: information-extraction-based and QA-based approaches, both of which the paper flags for error propagation from the auxiliary IE/QA model each depends on; NLI-based approaches, whose off-the-shelf models it says transfer poorly to summarization and can't localize which part of a generation is wrong; task-specific faithfulness-classification datasets built to improve on NLI; and LM-based methods that compare a conditional and an unconditional language model's loss on each token), and human evaluation (scoring vs. pairwise comparison). Mitigation methods split the same way the causes did: data-related (building cleaner datasets by hand, filtering noisy source-reference pairs automatically, or augmenting inputs with retrieved/extracted knowledge) and modeling-and-inference methods, summarized in the paper's own Figure 1 below — architectural changes to the encoder/attention/decoder, and three training-time strategies (planning/sketching, reinforcement learning with hallucination-aware rewards, and multi-task learning) plus post-hoc correction of already-generated output.

Sections 7–12 then repeat this definition/metrics/mitigation pattern once per task, and Section 13 — the 2024 addition — does the same for LLMs specifically, but with a broadened definition: because LLMs draw on vast parametric knowledge rather than only a supplied source, the survey extends "the source" to include the model's training data itself, pushing LLM hallucination further toward the extrinsic end of the original taxonomy. That section catalogs reference-dependent metrics (hallucination benchmarks like TruthfulQA, PopQA, and Med-HALT, plus atomized-decomposition scorers like FActScore), reference-free metrics (token-probability uncertainty and self-consistency checks), and LLM-era mitigation techniques — RLHF, retrieval-augmented generation, contrastive decoding methods like DoLa, chain-of-thought variants like Chain-of-Verification, and post-hoc correction tools like CRITIC.

// what they found

What the survey documents

As a synthesis rather than an experiment, the survey's "results" are the findings it compiles from the literature it reviews, plus the map it draws connecting them. A handful of individually-sourced statistics recur across the page: Falke et al. found that 25% of summaries generated by state-of-the-art abstractive summarization systems contain hallucinated content, evaluated with ROUGE/BLEU/METEOR — metrics the survey stresses are blind to this failure mode; Dhingra et al. found 62% of WIKIBIO's target sentences contain information absent from their paired infobox source, illustrating the "hallucination from data" contributor above; and Zhong et al. found 74% of QMSum samples (a dialogue-summarization dataset) "consist of inconsistent facts." Each of these is a single reported figure from a single external study the survey cites — not something the survey authors measured themselves, and the four numbers span four different tasks/datasets with no shared denominator, so they should not be read as comparable to one another.

The vision-language section applies the same intrinsic/extrinsic split to image captioning (Figure 2 below): "there is no 'mirror' or 'football' on top of the chest" in the source image, so a caption claiming either is intrinsic hallucination, while "we cannot verify whether there are 'letters' in the drawer or a 'fan' on the roof" makes those claims extrinsic. To measure this at scale, Rohrbach et al.'s CHAIR metric checks what fraction of a caption's mentioned objects actually appear in the ground-truth captions, with two variants: CHAIRi (fraction of hallucinated object instances) and CHAIRs (fraction of captions containing at least one hallucinated object). The survey is candid that image captioning is, in its own words, "the only VL task that has corresponding previous research works" as of this writing — other VL tasks like visual question answering are illustrated with observed examples rather than an established metric (Figure 3 below, taken from a cited paper): a phone-screen question wrongly answered "a text message from a friend" and the question "Whom is the person texting?" wrongly answered "The driver" both show a model producing a plausible-sounding but image-contradicting answer, while the window question ("what can you see out the window?" answered "a parking lot," when the image shows no window at all) shows a model hallucinating an answer to a question the image cannot support — an example the survey itself describes only as triggered by "adversarially prompting an unanswerable question" (its own §2.1 intrinsic/extrinsic framework would classify an invented, unverifiable answer like this as extrinsic, though the survey doesn't apply that label to this particular example).

From the 2024 LLM addition, the headline reported reduction is RLHF's effect on InstructGPT: applying RLHF to fine-tune GPT-3 "significantly reduces the hallucination rate from 41% to 21% in closed domain tasks" — again a figure the survey cites from the InstructGPT paper rather than reproduces. The section otherwise functions as a benchmark and technique catalog rather than a source of new comparative numbers: it lists reference-dependent hallucination benchmarks (human-annotated ones like TruthfulQA, PopQA, and Med-HALT; automatically-generated ones built from Wikipedia and news text), reference-free detection methods (uncertainty-based and consistency-based), and mitigation techniques spanning pretraining data curation, instruction-tuning data curation, reward-model training, RAG, decoding-time interventions (DoLa, Context-Aware Decoding, Inference-Time Intervention), chain-of-thought variants (Chain-of-Verification, Chain of Natural Language Inference), post-processing (PURR, CRITIC), and model ensembling/debate.

// does it hold up?

The eval read

A survey isn't graded like a benchmark paper — there are no baselines to check or a leaderboard to interrogate — so the right question is whether the taxonomy it proposes actually carves the phenomenon cleanly, and whether the survey's own coverage is as even as its structure implies. On both counts, the paper's own text supplies the evidence for a more qualified read than "the field's canonical taxonomy" suggests.

The intrinsic/extrinsic split is looser in practice than the diagram suggests. The definitions section itself flags the first crack: extrinsic hallucination "is not always erroneous because it could be from factually correct external information," so the same category spans a harmless, even helpful, elaboration and a safety-relevant unverifiable claim, with no further subdivision offered to tell them apart — the survey's own Section 6.1 (Future Directions in Metrics Design) proposes "fine-grained metrics that can distinguish between the two types" as future work precisely because the field hasn't built one. More striking, that same section states plainly that "most of the existing hallucination metrics measure intrinsic and extrinsic hallucinations together as a unified metric" — meaning the two-way split the survey teaches as foundational is, by the survey's own account, rarely operationalized by the literature it surveys. Section 6.2 adds a second admission in the same vein: "most [mitigation] works do not distinguish between intrinsic and extrinsic hallucination," and extrinsic hallucination specifically "has been somewhat overlooked as it is more challenging to reduce." A taxonomy whose own authors describe it as under-operationalized by the field it organizes is a real caveat, not a nitpick.

Coverage across tasks isn't as even as the six-plus-one section structure implies. By page count, the original six task sections run roughly 3 pages (summarization), 4 (dialogue), 3 (GQA), 2 (data-to-text), 4 (machine translation), and 2 (vision-language) — about 18 pages total — while the 2024-added LLM section alone runs roughly 7 pages — nearly double the longest single original task section (dialogue and machine translation, 4 pages each), though under half of the six combined — and carries an explicit "updated in Jan 2024" footnote that no other section of the paper carries. That asymmetry is more than a page count: the vision-language section (Section 12) reviews object hallucination in single-sentence image captioning and pre-LLM VQA models with the same CHAIR metric and Figure 2/3 examples that the v1-vs-v7 token diff gives no sign of having been substantively revised (its only v1-only tokens anywhere in the paper are dropped/renumbered reference ids, not removed figures or metrics), even though by the time the LLM section was updated (per its own Jan 2024 footnote), GPT-4V-class multimodal LLMs had already made that earlier VL literature only partially representative of where the field's hallucination problem now lives. The LLM section's own Future Directions subsection (13.4.1) does acknowledge "Hallucination in Large Multimodal Models" as a live open problem — and at some length, about half a page of dedicated discussion (around 260 words across 11 sentences, the longest of §13.4's five future-direction subsections, touching GPT-4V vs. prior VLMs, the levels of information an image carries, CLIP's representational shortcomings, vision-language alignment gaps, annotation cost, and visual RLHF) — but that discussion still sits in future-work prose rather than being folded back into a revised Section 12, whose CHAIR-metric-and-captioning framing predates it — a gap concrete enough that a separate, dedicated survey of multimodal-hallucination now exists to fill it.

The definitional distinction (hallucination vs. factuality) doesn't fully propagate into the survey's own literature review. Section 2.3 does real definitional work separating faithfulness, factuality, and hallucination. But Section 4.3 (Human Evaluation) turns around and lists "faithfulness," "factual consistency," "fidelity," "factualness," "factuality," and "hallucination" as terms different cited papers use more or less interchangeably to describe the same measurement — which means the survey's careful vocabulary doesn't retroactively resolve the inconsistency in what it's surveying; it mostly documents that the inconsistency exists.

baselines

Not applicable in the usual sense — this is a synthesis, not an experiment, so there's no model comparison to audit. The closer analogue is coverage: the six original tasks get roughly even treatment (2–4 pages each, 18 pages total); the 2024 LLM addition alone runs about 7 pages — longer than any single task section, though short of the six combined — and wasn't integrated back into the per-task sections it updates.

contamination

Not applicable to the survey's own claims, but worth flagging for a reader who goes on to use the LLM-era benchmarks it catalogs (TruthfulQA, PopQA, etc.) — the survey doesn't discuss whether those hallucination benchmarks are themselves at risk of leaking into newer LLMs' training data, a live concern for exactly the kind of long-tail-knowledge probing questions Section 13.2.1 describes.

what's missed

A fine-grained sub-typing of extrinsic hallucination by harm potential (the paper itself calls for this in Section 6.1 and never delivers it); an updated, LLM-era vision-language section to match the LLM-era text section; and, since the survey is a narrative rather than a systematic review, no stated search protocol or inclusion criteria — normal for the genre, but it means a reader has to trust the authors' scope judgment rather than reproduce it.

would raise confidence

An explicit accounting of which fraction of the surveyed literature actually applies the intrinsic/extrinsic split operationally (vs. treating hallucination as a single undifferentiated category, which its own Section 6.1 says is the norm); a revised VL section synchronized with the 2024 LLM update; and a stated protocol for what was and wasn't included, task by task.

// bottom line

The paper earned its 5,480 citations by being the first single place to read the intrinsic/extrinsic hallucination vocabulary applied consistently across NLG's major tasks, and its 2024 update kept it relevant through the LLM shift by bolting on rather than rewriting. But treat the taxonomy as a useful organizing lens, not a settled ontology: the survey's own text says most of the literature it organizes doesn't actually separate intrinsic from extrinsic, and the newest, largest section sits structurally apart from the six it's meant to extend.

// takeaways

What to remember

  1. Ji et al. didn't coin the intrinsic/extrinsic hallucination split — the paper says it follows prior work — but by applying it consistently across summarization, dialogue, GQA, data-to-text, translation, and vision-language generation in one place, it became the field's standard reference for that vocabulary (5,480 citations).
  2. The paper grew from 41 pages (v1, Feb 2022) to 59 pages (the staged v7, Jul 2024) — about +44% (18 added pages), not "nearly doubled"; v1 runs roughly two-thirds v7's length. Only about 7 of those 18 pages are the new LLM-hallucination section (§13, footnoted "updated in Jan 2024") — the rest is bibliography growth (329 references by v7) and in-section additions elsewhere.
  3. The taxonomy is more contested than it looks on first read: the survey's own text admits extrinsic hallucination "is not always erroneous," and that most existing hallucination metrics and mitigation methods don't actually separate intrinsic from extrinsic at all — a caveat worth carrying into any paper that cites this survey's categories as settled.
