Why Language Models Hallucinate

Hallucination recast as an ordinary binary-classification error: a reduction proves pretrained models must generate some falsehoods even from error-free data, and a meta-evaluation of ten leaderboard benchmarks argues post-training can’t fix it because nine of the ten give zero credit for admitting uncertainty.

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

Hallucinations — plausible, confident, and wrong — are still present in the newest systems (the paper cites OpenAI’s own GPT-5 System Card, OpenAI 2025a), and the field is not short on taxonomies, benchmarks, or mitigation techniques for them. What it has lacked, the authors argue, is a statistical account of why a well-trained model would produce them at all, and why years of post-training haven’t finished the job. Their opening examples make the everyday version of the problem concrete: asked “What is Adam Tauman Kalai’s birthday? If you know, just respond with DD-MM,” a 600-billion-parameter open-source model (DeepSeek-V3, accessed via the DeepSeek app on 11 May 2025) returned three different wrong dates on three separate attempts — “03-07,” “15-06,” “01-01” — despite being told to answer only if known; the real date falls in autumn. Asked to count the D’s in “DEEPSEEK,” the same model answered “2” or “3” across ten independent trials, while Meta AI and Claude 3.7 Sonnet (accessed 9 May 2025) performed similarly, including answers as large as “6” and “7.” Table 1 supplies a third example: asked for the title of Adam Kalai’s own PhD dissertation, ChatGPT (GPT-4o), DeepSeek, and Llama each returned a different, confidently wrong title and year — none matches the real answer (Kalai, 2001) — all three freely accessed on 2025-05-09, none searching the web (ChatGPT alone got the school, CMU, right).

The paper’s answer has two independent halves. First, pretraining: even if the training corpus contained zero errors, the paper shows that the ordinary statistical objective minimized during pretraining — not a flaw specific to next-token prediction or Transformers — would still produce a base model that generates some falsehoods, via a reduction to binary classification. Second, post-training: the reduction alone doesn’t explain why hallucinations survive years of refinement aimed at removing them, so the paper turns to how models are graded. Of the ten influential leaderboard evaluations it surveys — the backbone of the leaderboards used to select and market models — nine score purely binary right/wrong, with zero credit for an honest “I don’t know.” Under that grading scheme, a confident guesser always beats a model that abstains when unsure, so post-training optimized against those leaderboards has every incentive to keep the guessing behavior in place.

// how it works

The method

The paper’s central device is a reduction it calls Is-It-Valid (IIV). Take the space of plausible outputs X and partition it into valid outputs V and errors E (for hallucination, E is the set of plausible-but-false statements). IIV is the binary classification problem of telling members of V from members of E, given a training set that is a 50/50 mix of real training examples (labeled +) and uniformly random draws from E (labeled −). The key move is that any base language model p̂ can be turned into an IIV classifier for free, just by thresholding its own probability estimate — call an example a “+” if p̂(x) exceeds 1/|E|, “−” otherwise. Generation, the paper argues, is at least as hard as this classification problem: a model that generates well must implicitly be answering “is this valid?” correctly about everything it could output. That reduction lower-bounds a base model’s generative error rate (err) by roughly twice its IIV misclassification rate (erriiv):

err ≥ 2 · erriiv − |V||E| − δ // Corollary 1 (no prompts, noiseless training data). Theorem 1 generalizes this to prompted models, replacing |V|/|E| with maxc|Vc| / minc|Ec|. δ is a calibration gap, shown to stay small at a local optimum of the ordinary cross-entropy pretraining loss.

The three regimes in Figure 1 map onto three distinct mechanisms the paper works through in turn. Poor models: the paper notes that DeepSeek-R1’s chain-of-thought reasoning correctly counts letters by spelling a word out token-by-token (“D-E-E-P-S-E-E-K… First letter: D…”), something the base DeepSeek-V3 model above could not do — evidence that specific failure is a poor-model problem, not an inherent statistical limit, since a better-fit model in the same family solves it. Arbitrary facts: for prompts like birthdays, where a single correct answer is chosen essentially at random per prompt (Definition 1), the paper defines the singleton rate sr — the fraction of training prompts that appear exactly once, ignoring abstentions — and proves (Theorem 2, extending the classical Good–Turing missing-mass estimator) that any pretraining algorithm’s error is at least roughly sr, and that there exists an efficient, calibrated algorithm whose error is at most roughly sr as well — making the bound tight — with probability ≥99% over the random assignment of correct answers and the training sample. This generalizes Kalai & Vempala (2024)’s earlier version of the same bound to include prompts and IDK responses. As the paper’s own worked illustration puts it: if 20% of a category of birthday facts appear exactly once in the training data, base models should hallucinate on at least about 20% of birthday questions in that category — a consequence of the theorem, not a measurement on a real model. Statistical complexity: for pure multiple-choice with a single correct answer among C options, Theorem 3 gives err ≥ 2(1−1/C)·opt(G), where opt(G) is the best achievable misclassification rate for the model family G. As an illustration, the paper applies this to classic two-word-context trigram models on the ambiguous pair “She/He lost it and was completely out of her/his mind”: since a two-word window can’t distinguish the two prompts, opt(G) = 1/2 and Corollary 2 concludes any trigram model’s error rate on this pair must be at least 1/2.

The paper is explicit about what these bounds do not say. Language models in general are not claimed to be doomed: “hallucinations are inevitable only for base models” — a trivially non-hallucinating system is easy to build (a fixed question-answer database plus a calculator, defaulting to IDK on everything else). The lower bounds apply to statistically-trained base models under the paper’s own noiseless-data, thresholded-classifier construction; they are not a claim that no system, ever, can avoid error, and the paper says so directly.

// what they found

The results

The paper’s one piece of empirical evidence for the calibration argument is borrowed rather than run by the authors: Figure 2 is reprinted, with permission, from OpenAI’s GPT-4 Technical Report (OpenAI, 2023a, Figure 8). On multiple-choice questions with four plausible answers (A/B/C/D), the pretrained GPT-4 base model’s stated confidence tracks its actual accuracy closely — Expected Calibration Error (ECE) of 0.007 — while the same model’s confidence badly overshoots its accuracy after reinforcement learning, ECE 0.074, roughly a 10× jump. The paper reads this as consistent with its calibration argument: the cross-entropy objective used in pretraining pushes the calibration gap δ toward zero at a local optimum, while RL post-training optimizes a different objective with no such guarantee.

The second, larger piece of evidence for the post-training argument is a meta-evaluation (Appendix F) of ten evaluation benchmarks — drawn from two curated leaderboards, HELM Capabilities and the Open LLM Leaderboard, whose selections overlap on three of the ten (MMLU-Pro, GPQA, IFEval), plus two standalone, independently influential benchmarks, SWE-bench and Humanity’s Last Exam (HLE). Nine of the ten benchmarks grade purely binary, with zero credit for an IDK response. Several of the same ten also show up, unprompted, in the model cards the paper cites as evidence of their influence: Google’s Gemini 2.5 Pro card reports GPQA, MMLU, SWE-bench, HLE, and AIME; OpenAI’s own recent releases report GPQA, MMLU, SWE-bench Verified, IFEval, MATH, and HLE. The paper’s proposed fix is not another hallucination-specific benchmark but a scoring change to these existing ones: state an explicit confidence threshold t directly in each question’s own instructions (“Answer only if you are >t confident, since mistakes are penalized t/(1−t) points, while correct answers receive 1 point, and an answer of ‘I don’t know’ receives 0 points”), so abstention becomes an explicitly rational choice for an uncertain model rather than a strictly dominated one. With an explicit threshold, one policy — answer only when your true confidence exceeds t, which the paper terms behavioral calibration — is simultaneously optimal at every threshold, sidestepping the practical problem that a correct response can be phrased in exponentially many equivalent ways. The paper explicitly recommends folding this into mainstream evaluations already in wide use (its own example: SWE-bench) rather than adding another bespoke hallucination eval.

// does it hold up?

The eval read

There’s no new model, no new benchmark run, and no new dataset to audit here — the pretraining half is a proof, the post-training half is a meta-evaluation plus an untested scoring proposal. The critique has to be about what the theorems actually license, whether the persistence argument is causal or merely consistent, and what the proposal is missing.

The reduction’s assumptions are stronger than the framing lets on, and the paper is upfront about most of them. Theorem 1/Corollary 1 require a noiseless training distribution (p(V) = 1 — “even if the training data were error-free”), and the specific 50/50-mixture construction used to define erriiv is a modeling choice, not something read off a real pretraining corpus. The paper flags the direction of the gap itself: “with realistic training data containing shades of error, one may expect even higher error rates” — the right direction for a lower bound to err in. What the bound does not do is quantify how far short of that conservative floor any actual deployed model’s hallucination rate falls, or connect erriiv to a measurable property of a real training run; the paper’s own worked numbers (the 20%-singleton-rate example, the trigram-model 1/2 bound) are illustrative constructions, not measurements on a GPT-4-class model. It is a genuine explanation of why some hallucination is unavoidable under these statistical conditions — not evidence about how much of a modern model’s hallucination the mechanism accounts for.

“Benchmarks cause the persistence” is asserted, not tested against the obvious alternative. The evaluation-scoring argument is a mechanism story — Observation 1 shows, correctly and almost trivially (the proof is one line), that under any binary grader an optimal test-taker never abstains — plus an inventory (Table 2) of how ten real benchmarks grade. What the paper does not supply is anything that would distinguish “binary grading is why hallucination persists” from “binary grading and unreduced hallucination are both downstream of the same thing” — e.g., that binary accuracy is simply cheaper to compute and market than a calibration-aware metric, independent of any causal effect on model behavior. Nor does the paper report whether a model trained or selected under an explicit-confidence-target version of these benchmarks actually hallucinates less; Section 4.2’s “simple calculation” establishing when abstention beats guessing is analytic, not an experiment run on a real model. The socio-technical fix is, by the paper’s own framing, a conjecture about incentives — plausible and consistent with the reduction’s logic, but untested at leaderboard scale.

The paper names the abstention trade-off but doesn’t size it. Section 4.1 frames binary grading as weighing two “desirable factors” — accuracy among what’s output, and how comprehensive responses are — and states “weighing (a) more than (b) is important for reducing hallucinations,” which acknowledges that rewarding IDK more has a coverage cost, but the paper doesn’t quantify it: how much correct, useful information would a behaviorally-calibrated model at, say, t = 0.75 withhold on a real benchmark, relative to the hallucinations it avoids? Without that number, the proposal’s central selling point — that it’s a scoring change, not a capability tax — is asserted rather than shown.

The ten-benchmark sample is two curated leaderboards plus two standalone giants, not an audit of the field. HELM Capabilities and the Open LLM Leaderboard were chosen because they’re widely used and curated by others — a reasonable proxy for influence, but also a convenience sample of exactly ten evaluations, drawn from leaderboards the paper itself calls a much larger compendium (HELM: “hundreds of high-quality evaluations”; the Open LLM Leaderboard sits atop EleutherAI’s larger LM Evaluation Harness). The paper does note, in passing, that part of HELM’s own broader evaluation set allows IDK responses — it names BBQ, on HELM’s Safety leaderboard — which cuts slightly against reading the nine-of-ten figure as representative of “most evaluations” broadly, versus representative of the specific ten highest-visibility ones the paper chose to examine.

One fact worth stating plainly, without insinuation: three of the paper’s four authors (Kalai, Nachum, Zhang) are at OpenAI, and the paper’s central policy recommendation is that the field change how it scores the exact leaderboard benchmarks OpenAI’s own models are marketed against.

baselines

None in the empirical-comparison sense — no new model is trained or scored. The nearest thing to a baseline is Table 1’s three off-the-shelf, publicly-accessed models (GPT-4o, DeepSeek, Llama) on one hand-picked prompt each, and Figure 2’s reused GPT-4 pre-/post-RL comparison from OpenAI’s own 2023 report.

contamination

Not applicable to the theorems. The illustrative model queries were one-off, freely-accessed ad-hoc queries rather than a controlled measurement — Table 1’s dissertation-title prompt was run once per model, the DeepSeek birthday prompt three times, and the letter-counting prompt ten times — on 2025-05-09 or 11 May 2025, against models that could plausibly have seen the authors’ own prior work about themselves — a minor irony given the subject is a Kalai-authored PhD thesis, not a confound the paper addresses.

what’s missed

Whether behavioral calibration, applied to a real benchmark, changes accuracy and coverage in practice; a size estimate for the abstention-comprehensiveness trade-off; and any measurement, on any model, of the erriiv or singleton-rate quantities the theorems are stated in terms of.

would raise confidence

Running the explicit-confidence-target prompt on even one existing benchmark (e.g. the SWE-bench example the paper names) and reporting the accuracy/abstention trade-off it actually produces; a broader or more systematic sample of evaluations beyond the ten hand-picked leaderboard entries; and an empirical estimate of erriiv or the singleton rate on a real pretraining corpus, however rough, to connect the bound to a measured number.

// bottom line

The pretraining half is a clean, honestly-scoped piece of theory: hallucination reduced to an ordinary binary-classification lower bound, with the paper explicit about which assumptions (noiseless data, the 50/50 IIV construction) do the work and which direction the approximation errs in. The post-training half is a sharper, more consequential argument — that leaderboard scoring, not model capability, is the bottleneck on hallucination — but it is argued from a ten-benchmark inventory and a one-line optimality observation, not tested: nothing in the paper shows a model actually hallucinating less once graded this way. The recommendation is worth taking seriously precisely because it’s cheap to test and the paper hasn’t tested it yet.

// takeaways

What to remember

  1. Hallucination doesn’t need a mysterious explanation: the paper’s Is-It-Valid reduction shows any base language model’s generative error rate is bounded below by roughly twice its own binary valid/error misclassification rate (Theorem 1), a bound that holds even for training data with zero errors in it — and it strictly generalizes Kalai & Vempala (2024)’s earlier singleton-rate result to include prompts and IDK responses.
  2. The bounds are about statistically-trained base models specifically, not a claim that hallucination-free systems are impossible in principle — the paper points out that a fixed QA database plus a calculator, defaulting to IDK, trivially never hallucinates.
  3. Of the ten influential leaderboard evaluations the paper surveyed (GPQA, MMLU-Pro, IFEval, Omni-MATH, WildBench, BBH, MATH L5, MuSR, SWE-bench, HLE), nine grade purely binary with zero credit for saying “I don’t know” — the paper’s explanation for why hallucination survives post-training, and its proposed fix (explicit confidence-threshold instructions baked into existing benchmarks) is a scoring change nobody has yet tested at leaderboard scale.
