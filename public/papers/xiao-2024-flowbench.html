FlowBench: Revisiting and Benchmarking Workflow-Guided Planning for LLM-based Agents

FlowBench hands an LLM agent the identical business workflow three ways — prose, pseudocode, and a Mermaid flowchart — across 51 scenarios in 6 domains, and finds that even GPT-4o clears well under half its sessions.

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

LLM-based agents plan by chaining tool calls, but when a task requires expertise-intensive, business-specific procedure — the exact order to check a flight's availability, then reserve it, then handle a failed reservation — a model's parametric knowledge alone produces what the authors call "planning hallucinations": plausible-looking but wrong action sequences. Prior work fixes this by handing the agent external "workflow knowledge," but each system invents its own ad hoc representation with no comparison against the alternatives: KnowAgent writes action rules in natural language, ProAgent encodes control flow in Python-like code, and neither is evaluated against the other, or against a no-workflow baseline, on a shared task set.

Existing agent-planning benchmarks don't fill the gap either. As FlowBench's own comparison table lays out, PlanBench evaluates one-shot textual-script generation over toy PDDL domains (Blocksworld, logistics) with no multi-turn interaction and no injected workflow knowledge at all; ProAgent's own evaluation is confined to Robotic Process Automation with a single knowledge format (code); and TravelAgent covers one domain (travel planning) with neither workflow knowledge nor a real environment/user loop. FlowBench's goal is to isolate the one variable none of them isolate: hold the underlying task and the underlying procedure fixed, vary only how that procedure is represented — text, code, or flowchart — and measure which representation actually helps an LLM agent plan, across 6 domains, 22 roles, and 51 scenarios rather than one narrow niche.

// how it works

The method

FlowBench organizes its data in a four-level hierarchy: domain → role → scenario → task-related knowledge base. It spans 6 domains (Customer Service, Personal Assistant, E-tailing Recommendation, Travel & Transportation, Logistics Solutions, and Robotic Process Automation), 22 agent roles, and 51 task scenarios — for example, the apartment_manager role under Customer Service covers apartment search, viewing scheduling, bill inquiry, and rent payment, each with its own knowledge base.

Construction runs a three-phase pipeline. Task collection seeds scenarios from the STAR schema-guided dialogue dataset and extends them by hand for both personal-customer and business-enterprise use cases. Workflow organization has human annotators write each scenario's procedure as a natural-language document first, cross-verify it for correctness and completeness, and only then have GPT-4 convert that same, already-verified procedure into Python-style pseudocode and Markdown-Mermaid flowchart form, with manual re-verification for consistency — so the text/code/flowchart variants encode one underlying procedure rather than three independently authored ones. Interactive session generation pairs a human annotator with a GPT-4 annotator to co-author multi-turn ground-truth user-agent sessions turn by turn, deliberately seeding some sessions with off-topic chit-chat and irrelevant requests to keep them realistic. Overall, 12% of scenarios and 26% of sessions were filtered out after initial annotation during quality control.

Evaluation runs at two granularities. Static turn-level evaluation feeds the agent the ground-truth interaction history up through turn i−1 and scores its single next action against the ground truth: tool-invocation precision/recall/F1 (a call counts as correct only if both the tool name and every required parameter match), a separate parameter-collection F1, and a GPT-4-judged 0–10 response-preference score for correctness, helpfulness, and humanness. Simulated session-level evaluation instead drives a full multi-turn session with a GPT-4-based user simulator, conditioned on a task-user summary distilled from (but distinct from) the original annotation profile, and grades the whole session on tool-invocation F1, success rate (all of the summary's goals achieved) and task progress (percentage of goals achieved). Both granularities run twice — single-scenario, where the agent is told which of a role's scenarios applies and given only that scenario's workflow, and cross-scenario, where the agent receives every scenario's workflow for the role and must infer which one applies as the conversation unfolds.

The main-text experiments test three OpenAI models — GPT-4o (gpt-4o-0513), GPT-4-Turbo (gpt-4-0125-preview), and GPT-3.5-Turbo (gpt-3.5-turbo-16k-0613) — each run four ways: None (a tool description only, no workflow knowledge), Text, Code, and Flowchart, prompted with a ReAct-style thought/action/observation loop. Automatic judging (response score, success rate, task progress) uses gpt-4-turbo-0125-preview throughout for consistency. An appendix extends the static turn-level comparison to two open-weight models, Qwen2-7B-Instruct and Qwen2-72B-Instruct, to check that the format trend isn't specific to OpenAI's models.

// what they found

The results

Even at its best, no model comes close to solving FlowBench. Under simulated session-level evaluation, GPT-4o's own best configuration reaches only a 43.2% success rate in the single-scenario setting (with Code-format knowledge) and 40.9% in the cross-scenario setting (with Text-format knowledge) — the two figures the paper itself highlights as evidence that "even the best-performing LLM... struggles to deliver satisfactory performance." Every model does better with some workflow knowledge than with none: GPT-4o's knowledge-free (None) session-level success rate is 35.1% single-scenario / 34.2% cross-scenario, rising to 41.7–43.2% single-scenario and 38.2–40.9% cross-scenario once workflow knowledge of any format is supplied.

Format matters, but not uniformly across metrics. At turn level, flowchart-formatted knowledge gives the largest tool-invocation F1 gain over the next-best format for every model, in the single-scenario setting: +6.4 F1 for GPT-4o (75.5 vs. 69.1 for Text), +6.6 F1 for GPT-4-Turbo (73.6 vs. 67.0 for Code), and +5.6 F1 for GPT-3.5-Turbo (65.4 vs. 59.8 for Text). Code's standing relative to Text is inconsistent across models, and not in a way that tracks capability: Code clearly beats Text for GPT-4-Turbo (67.0 vs. 62.9, +4.1), is essentially a rounding error for the strongest model tested, GPT-4o (69.0 vs. 69.1, -0.1), and trails Text for the weakest, GPT-3.5-Turbo (57.9 vs. 59.8, -1.9) — no clean monotone trend, only the paper's own narrower observation that "the code format is less effective on weaker LLMs." At session level, the flowchart's edge narrows: it wins tool-invocation F1 in five of six model×setting cells (the exception is GPT-3.5-Turbo single-scenario, where Text edges it out, 49.1 vs. 48.4), and it's no longer always best on outcome metrics — GPT-4o's own single-scenario success rate peaks with Code (43.2%), narrowly ahead of Flowchart (42.7%) and Text (41.7%).

Cross-scenario evaluation is harder than single-scenario across every session-level metric and every turn-level tool-invocation-F1 cell, confirming that the task-awareness split measures something real: being able to follow a known procedure is not the same as first working out which procedure applies. That pattern isn't universal, though — the turn-level response-preference score often runs the other way, scoring higher cross-scenario in 8 of 12 model×format cells, and even parameter-collection F1 has at least one cross-higher cell (GPT-3.5-Turbo Code: 69.2 single vs. 70.2 cross). Because Mermaid flowcharts are graphs, FlowBench can additionally check whether a flowchart-guided plan lands on the correct state node. Node-prediction accuracy tracks model capability closely: GPT-4o holds up best under the scenario switch (92.5% single-scenario → 84.0% cross-scenario, a 8.5-point drop), GPT-4-Turbo falls further in absolute terms (91.8% → 70.7%, a 21.1-point drop), and GPT-3.5-Turbo — while its absolute drop is comparable (77.9% → 57.3%, a 20.6-point drop) — ends up with the lowest cross-scenario node accuracy of the three.

Two further ablations, run on GPT-4-Turbo only, sharpen the picture. Stripping even the bare tool descriptions out of the None condition (a "None w/o tool" variant retaining only tool/parameter names) drops cross-scenario success rate from 31.1% to 25.3% — some of what looks like "commonsense planning" in the None baseline is really the tool schema doing the work. An Ensemble variant that hands the agent text, code, and flowchart simultaneously beats every single format on nearly every metric: single-scenario success rate reaches 45.3% (vs. 40.6% for Flowchart alone) and cross-scenario success rate reaches 43.4% (vs. 39.4% for Flowchart alone) — the three formats are not fully redundant with each other.

// does it hold up?

The eval read

FlowBench's grading pipeline is thorough on paper — multi-tiered (turn and session), multi-metric (tool F1, parameter F1, response score, success rate, task progress) — but a large share of it is itself an LLM judge (GPT-4-Turbo grading response quality, success, and progress for every model, including itself and GPT-4o). That raises the usual construct-validity question: is the benchmark measuring workflow-following ability, or measuring what a GPT-4-family judge rewards? The camera-ready EMNLP Findings version — not the arXiv preprint, which omits this entirely — does address it with a genuine human-alignment study: two independent groups of human annotators scored 50 sampled turn-level responses per scenario setting, for each of the four knowledge formats, for GPT-4-Turbo, and the resulting human-to-LLM Pearson correlation (0.76–0.87 across the eight format×setting cells) tracks close to the human-to-human correlation (0.80–0.89), in two cells even exceeding it. That is a real, useful check the arXiv version doesn't attempt — but it is narrow: it covers only the turn-level response-preference score, only for GPT-4-Turbo, on a modest 50-sample-per-setting subset. The session-level success-rate and task-progress judgments that the paper's headline claim actually rests on (GPT-4o's 43.2%/40.9% success rates) are never checked against a human rater at all, for any model.

The "workflow knowledge helps most in expertise-demanding domains" finding also leans partly on E-tailing Recommendation, which by the paper's own domain statistics is the smallest of the six domains by a wide margin — 2 roles, 5 scenarios, and only 21 single-scenario / 11 cross-scenario sessions, versus 73–78 single-scenario sessions for Robotic Process Automation and Travel & Transportation, the other two domains cited alongside it in the paper's own sentence (p.8: "RPA, E-tailing Recommendation, and Travel"). That specific per-domain claim is resting on noticeably thinner evidence than the other two it's grouped with.

baselines

Three real OpenAI capability tiers (GPT-4o, GPT-4-Turbo, GPT-3.5-Turbo) in the main text, plus an appendix check on open-weight Qwen2-7B/72B-Instruct (turn-level only) that reproduces the same flowchart-F1 advantage — a reasonable, if OpenAI-centric, spread for 2024; no fine-tuned or retrieval-augmented agent baseline is tried against the prompted ReAct loop.

contamination

Not classic train-set leakage — the scenarios and sessions are synthetic, GPT-4-and-human-authored. The analogous risk here is judge leniency: the same GPT-4/GPT-4-Turbo family both helps author the ground-truth sessions (as the LLM co-annotator) and grades every model's session-level output, and the paper reports no same-family-vs-cross-family judging-gap check that would let a reader see whether GPT-4-family responses get graded more generously.

what's missed

Human validation covers only the turn-level response score for one model (GPT-4-Turbo); success rate and task progress — the metrics behind the 43.2%/40.9% headline numbers — are graded exclusively by GPT-4-Turbo with no human check, for any of the three models with session-level results. Every number reported is the mean of three runs (p.13: "All experiments are conducted three times, and the mean results are displayed"), but the spread across those three runs is never reported, so close format rankings (e.g., GPT-4o's 43.2% Code vs. 42.7% Flowchart single-scenario success rate) can't be distinguished from run-to-run noise.

would raise confidence

Extend the human-alignment check (Table 11 in the appendix) to success-rate/task-progress judging and to the models the headline claims are actually about, especially GPT-4o; report per-domain sample sizes alongside per-domain findings; report the per-run spread across the three runs already performed, especially for close format comparisons.

// bottom line

FlowBench earns its "first benchmark for workflow-guided planning" claim — holding the underlying procedure fixed while varying only its representation (text/code/flowchart) is a genuinely careful control, and the camera-ready's human-alignment appendix is a real validity check the arXiv preprint doesn't even attempt. But the paper's two headline numbers are graded by the same model family that helped write the ground truth, and the human check that exists validates a different metric, on a different model, than the one those headline numbers describe.

// takeaways

What to remember

  1. FlowBench isolates workflow-knowledge format as a controlled variable: the same human-verified procedure is expressed in text, Python-style code, and a Mermaid flowchart, so format comparisons across text/code/flowchart aren't confounded by differences in the underlying knowledge itself.
  2. Even GPT-4o, the strongest model tested, tops out at a 43.2% single-scenario / 40.9% cross-scenario session-level success rate at its best format — more workflow knowledge narrows the gap to a solved task but doesn't close it.
  3. Flowchart-formatted knowledge gives the most consistent turn-level tool-invocation gain for the three main-text models (+5.6 to +6.6 F1 over the next-best format on single-scenario tasks, across GPT-4o/GPT-4-Turbo/GPT-3.5-Turbo; the cross-scenario margins are far more uneven — +3.8, +10.9 and +4.6, straddling the single-scenario band rather than tracking it), a direction that also holds in the camera-ready's Qwen2-7B/72B appendix check but at far smaller magnitude (+1.2 and +2.8 F1) — its edge over Text/Code narrows or disappears on session-level outcome metrics like success rate.
