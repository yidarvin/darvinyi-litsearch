LIBERO: Benchmarking Knowledge Transfer for Lifelong Robot Learning

130 procedurally-generated manipulation tasks, split into four suites that each isolate a different kind of distribution shift — spatial layout, object identity, task goal, or all three entangled — built to ask what a robot forgets, and why, when it learns tasks one after another. The headline result is uncomfortable for the field it's built for: plain sequential finetuning beats three purpose-built lifelong-learning algorithms on forward transfer.

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

Lifelong learning research has mostly been built and measured on image and text domains, where what an agent needs to retain is declarative knowledge — facts about entities and concepts (a picture is a "dog", a sentence is "positive"). A robot manipulating objects needs that too, but it also needs to retain procedural knowledge: how to physically open a fridge, how to grasp a bottle, how to place a bowl without knocking it over. The paper's framing example makes the failure mode concrete: a robot trained to retrieve juice from a fridge, after learning new tasks, might fail — and it's not obvious from the outside whether that's because it forgot where the juice or fridge is (declarative) or forgot how to open the fridge or grasp the juice (procedural). Existing lifelong-learning benchmarks can't separate these causes because they don't isolate the two kinds of knowledge at the task-suite level.

Prior robot-learning benchmarks aren't built for this question either: they target meta-learning (Meta-World), causal structure (CausalWorld), general multi-task manipulation (RLBench), or generalization to unseen objects (ManiSkill) — none of them constructs task suites whose entire point is to isolate which type of knowledge transfers or fails to transfer across a learning stream. Adapted vision/language continual-learning benchmarks (Core50, GLUE/SuperGLUE-style splits) and game-based ones (Atari, XLand, VizDoom, plus CORA's Atari/Procgen/MiniHack/ALFRED suites) sit on the other side of the same gap: real lifelong-learning protocols, but no procedural, embodied decision-making. LIBERO is built to sit in the intersection — a procedurally-generated manipulation benchmark whose four task suites are explicitly designed to disentangle spatial, object, and goal knowledge transfer, plus a fourth suite where all three are entangled, so that a lifelong learner's failures can be attributed to a specific kind of knowledge rather than reported as one undifferentiated forgetting number.

// how it works

The method

LIBERO is built around a three-stage procedural generation pipeline (Figure 2) that can in principle produce infinitely many tasks. First, behavioral templates are mined from Ego4D, a large-scale egocentric human-activity dataset, and turned into natural-language task instructions (e.g. "Open the top drawer of the cabinet and put the bowl in it"). Second, an initial state distribution is specified: a scene layout is chosen to match the instruction's objects and behaviors, and the object categories, placements, and initial states are written out as a PDDL (Planning Domain Definition Language) description. Third, a task goal is specified as a conjunction of PDDL predicates — unary predicates like Open(X) or TurnOff(X), and binary spatial predicates like On(A,B) or In(A,B) — and the episode terminates successfully when every predicate is satisfied. The whole pipeline sits on top of robosuite, a modular manipulation simulator.

For benchmarking, this pipeline is used to fix 130 tasks across four suites. LIBERO-SPATIAL, LIBERO-OBJECT, and LIBERO-GOAL each contain 10 tasks and are designed to disentangle one kind of shift at a time (Section 4.2 of the paper): every LIBERO-SPATIAL task asks the robot to place one of two identical bowls on a plate, with the same object set every time but a different spatial relationship to distractors — so success requires continually learning new spatial relationships. Every LIBERO-OBJECT task asks the robot to pick and place a different, unique object, so success requires continually learning new object identities. Every LIBERO-GOAL task keeps the same objects and layout but changes the goal (what to do with them), so success requires continually learning new motions/behaviors. The fourth suite, LIBERO-100, has 100 tasks with diverse objects, layouts, and backgrounds, entangling all three kinds of knowledge; the paper splits it into LIBERO-90 (90 short-horizon tasks, used only as pretraining data) and LIBERO-LONG (10 long-horizon tasks, used as the actual downstream lifelong-learning evaluation suite in the main results tables). Every one of the 130 tasks ships with 50 human-teleoperated demonstration trajectories, collected with a 3Dconnexion Spacemouse, so that lifelong learning can be studied via behavioral cloning rather than sparse-reward RL.

On top of the task suites, the paper implements a 3×5 factorial of policy architectures and lifelong-learning algorithms that every result in the paper is reported against. The three architectures (Figure 6) share a GMM (Gaussian-mixture-model) policy head and a ResNet or ViT visual encoder over two camera views (AgentView, Eye-in-Hand), but differ in their temporal backbone: RESNET-RNN uses an LSTM, RESNET-T uses a transformer decoder over a sequence of ResNet visual tokens, and VIT-T uses a Vision Transformer encoder with a transformer decoder. All three are FLOP-matched at ~13.5G FLOPS so that comparisons aren't confounded by raw compute. The five algorithms are SEQL (sequential finetuning, no forgetting mitigation — the lower-bound baseline), MTL (multitask learning on all tasks jointly — the upper-bound baseline), and three actual lifelong-learning methods representing the three main algorithmic families: ER (Experience Replay, memory-based), EWC (Elastic Weight Consolidation, regularization-based), and PackNet (dynamic-architecture-based, allocating and freezing sub-networks per task).

Every combination of algorithm, architecture, and task suite is trained for 50 behavioral-cloning epochs per task (Adam, batch size 32, cosine LR 1e-4→1e-5), evaluated every 5 epochs over 20 test rollouts (max length 600 steps), with the best checkpoint kept per task. This is repeated over 3 seeds, giving 5 algorithms × 3 architectures × 4 suites × 3 seeds = 180 experiments total. Performance is scored with three metrics computed purely from success rate rather than training loss (the paper found loss to be a misleading proxy for manipulation policies, Appendix E.2): FWT (forward transfer, higher is better) measures how well the agent learns each new task; NBT (negative backward transfer, lower is better) measures how much performance on earlier tasks degrades after learning later ones — i.e. forgetting; and AUC (higher is better) is a combined area-under-the-curve score folding in both forward transfer and retained performance on prior tasks.

// what they found

The results

With the architecture fixed to RESNET-T (the architecture the paper reports performs best on average across policies — Table 2), the headline algorithm comparison is stark: SEQL has the highest FWT of the four algorithms that report it (SEQL, ER, EWC, PackNet — MTL trains jointly and reports no FWT) on all four task suites — 0.54 vs. ER's 0.48, EWC's 0.13, and PackNet's 0.22 on LIBERO-LONG; 0.72 vs. 0.65/0.23/0.55 on LIBERO-SPATIAL; 0.78 vs. 0.67/0.56/0.60 on LIBERO-OBJECT; 0.77 vs. 0.64/0.32/0.63 on LIBERO-GOAL. This is the empirical basis for the abstract's claim that "sequential finetuning outperforms existing lifelong learning methods" — the paper is explicit that this holds specifically in forward transfer, not in the AUC metric that also credits retained performance: PackNet, for instance, posts a higher AUC than SEQL on LIBERO-SPATIAL (0.63 vs. 0.20), LIBERO-OBJECT (0.60 vs. 0.26), and LIBERO-GOAL (0.75 vs. 0.22), because SEQL has no mechanism to prevent forgetting at all — its NBT is the worst of the four algorithms on every suite (e.g. 0.63–0.82 vs. ER's 0.27–0.43). The paper's own reading of this: none of the three purpose-built lifelong-learning algorithms actually improves the rate at which the agent picks up a new task relative to just finetuning through it; they only trade some of that forward-transfer speed for reduced forgetting. In the paper's words, this "indicates all lifelong learning algorithms we consider actually hurt forward transfer."

The architecture study (Table 1, ER and PackNet across all four suites) shows RESNET-T and VIT-T "work much better than RESNET-RNN on average," but which of the two transformer-backbone architectures wins depends on the algorithm: under ER, RESNET-T beats VIT-T on every suite except LIBERO-OBJECT (e.g. AUC 0.56 vs. 0.50 on LIBERO-SPATIAL), while under PackNet there's no significant RESNET-T/VIT-T difference except on LIBERO-LONG, where VIT-T is clearly ahead (AUC 0.34 vs. 0.25). The paper reads the OBJECT-suite exception as ViT's visual encoder handling higher object variety better once network capacity is sufficient, and points to the multitask (MTL) upper-bound as supporting evidence: on LIBERO-OBJECT specifically, MTL's AUC is 0.78 with VIT-T vs. 0.54 with RESNET-T vs. just 0.10 with RESNET-RNN (Table 8) — a much larger architecture gap than on any other suite.

Two secondary studies round out the results. Swapping the task language embedding (BERT, CLIP, GPT-2, or a bare Task-ID string fed through BERT) while holding RESNET-T+ER fixed on LIBERO-LONG produces no statistically significant difference — CLIP's FWT/AUC (0.52/0.35) are numerically the best of the four but not significantly so, and even the semantics-free Task-ID embedding (0.50/0.33) performs comparably, which the paper attributes to sentence embeddings functioning like bag-of-words task discriminators rather than encoding usable semantic content. And pretraining hurts: across five algorithm/architecture combinations pretrained via behavioral cloning on the 90 short-horizon LIBERO-90 tasks and then evaluated on downstream LIBERO-LONG lifelong learning — the paper's AUC aggregate, plotted under a bare "Success Rate" axis — every single combination scores lower with pretraining than without: e.g. ER+RESNET-T drops from 0.32 to 0.22, ER+ViT-T from 0.25 to 0.10, EWC+RESNET-T from 0.02 to 0.01 (Figure 5) — even though the multitask upper bound (0.20–0.48 across the five combos) is above either pretrained or from-scratch in each case.

// does it hold up?

The eval read

The four-suite design is the benchmark's central claim, and it mostly earns it: LIBERO-SPATIAL, -OBJECT, and -GOAL really do hold two of {objects, layout, goal} fixed and vary the third, which is a cleaner disentanglement than most lifelong-RL benchmarks attempt. But the isolation is a controlled approximation rather than a guarantee — LIBERO-SPATIAL's 10 tasks all share the same place-a-bowl-on-a-plate skeleton (per §4.2, the target objects differ only in location or spatial relationship to other objects), so what's being measured is spatial-relationship transfer within one motor skill, not spatial transfer in general; a lifelong learner that memorizes bowl-placing geometry specifically wouldn't obviously transfer that to a different manipulation primitive. The paper doesn't claim otherwise, but the suite names invite a bigger generalization than 10 tasks around one skill can support.

The "sequential finetuning wins" result is the paper's most citable finding, and it is reported with real precision (RESNET-T fixed, FWT specifically, all four suites) — but it's worth being precise about what "existing lifelong learning methods" means here: three algorithms (ER, EWC, PackNet), chosen as one representative apiece from the memory/regularization/architecture families, evaluated with a fixed compute and epoch budget (50 epochs, 50 demonstrations) tuned for the benchmark itself rather than swept per-algorithm — and not strictly matched even so, since Appendix B.1 gives PackNet an additional 50 fine-tuning epochs per task on top of that shared budget (all metrics are computed before that extra stage, but the compute itself isn't free). EWC in particular under-performs by a wide margin (AUC as low as 0.02 on LIBERO-LONG), and the paper's own Appendix E.2 suggests this traces to EWC's loss-based regularization being a poor proxy for the success-rate objective — a specific, diagnosable weakness of one baseline rather than a general property of "algorithms that prevent forgetting." A newer or better-tuned regularization method might close some of this gap without overturning the AUC-side story (at RESNET-T, PackNet still leads AUC on 3 of 4 suites — though at RESNET-RNN it's ER that leads AUC on all four), but the FWT story — that none of the three help an agent learn a genuinely new task any faster than finetuning does — reads as more robust, holding in 11 of the 12 (suite × architecture) combinations in the full Table 8 breakdown, with one exact tie (SEQL and ER both post FWT 0.63 on LIBERO-SPATIAL with the VIT-T architecture).

Demonstration quality is a real confound that the paper is transparent about but doesn't quantify: all 130 tasks are seeded with only 50 human-teleoperated demonstrations, collected once per task rather than per-suite or per-difficulty. If some tasks (or suites) have systematically higher-quality or more consistent demonstrations than others — plausible given they were collected by different operators over an open-ended set of 130 tasks — that would show up as a suite effect indistinguishable from a genuine distribution-shift effect. Everything in the benchmark is also simulation-only (robosuite/MuJoCo), so none of the transfer, forgetting, or architecture findings have been checked against real-robot noise, contact dynamics, or a teleoperation-to-autonomy gap.

baselines

Reasonable spread — SEQL and MTL as lower/upper bounds plus one algorithm per major lifelong-learning family (ER/EWC/PackNet) — but each run at one fixed 50-epoch/50-demo training budget rather than independently tuned, and the weakest baseline (EWC) is diagnosed by the paper's own appendix as suffering from a loss/success-rate mismatch rather than a fundamentally miscalibrated method.

contamination

Not applicable in the usual LLM-eval sense — this is a from-scratch simulation benchmark with newly-collected teleoperated demonstrations, not text scraped from a corpus with potential train/test leakage.

what's missed

No real-robot validation; demonstration quality/consistency across 130 tasks isn't quantified or controlled for; the "disentangled" suites disentangle knowledge type only within one shared motor skill per suite, not across skills.

would raise confidence

A demonstration-quality audit (e.g. inter-operator variance) per task or suite; a sweep over each lifelong algorithm's own hyperparameters/budget rather than one shared training recipe; and at least one suite repeated with a second motor skill to test whether spatial/object/goal disentanglement generalizes beyond "place a bowl."

// bottom line

LIBERO is a carefully constructed benchmark whose core design — four suites that each vary one axis of knowledge — genuinely enables the paper's central, uncomfortable finding: on this benchmark, with this training budget, three representative lifelong-learning algorithms all sacrifice forward-transfer speed for reduced forgetting, while plain sequential finetuning does not. That finding is scoped correctly by the paper itself (FWT, not AUC; RESNET-T as the main-text architecture, though it replicates almost perfectly across all three). The open question the paper leaves for future work — better algorithms that don't trade away forward transfer — is exactly the direction its own numbers point to, and 130 tasks with human demonstrations plus a procedural generator built for infinitely many more give the field somewhere concrete to test that against.

// takeaways

What to remember

  1. LIBERO splits 130 procedurally-generated manipulation tasks into four suites — LIBERO-SPATIAL, -OBJECT, -GOAL (10 tasks each, one axis of knowledge held disentangled) and LIBERO-100/LONG (100/10 tasks, entangled) — specifically to separate declarative knowledge transfer (spatial relationships, object identity) from procedural knowledge transfer (goals/motions) in a way that image/text lifelong-learning benchmarks can't.
  2. With the architecture fixed to RESNET-T, sequential finetuning (SEQL) posts the highest forward-transfer (FWT) score of the four algorithms that report FWT on all four task suites (MTL trains all tasks jointly and reports no FWT) — meaning Experience Replay, EWC, and PackNet all learn new tasks more slowly than plain finetuning does, even though they reduce forgetting (lower NBT) and can post higher combined AUC scores.
  3. No single visual/temporal architecture dominates: RESNET-T and VIT-T both clearly beat RESNET-RNN, but which of the two wins swaps by algorithm and suite, and basic supervised pretraining on 90 short-horizon tasks measurably hurts downstream lifelong-learning performance in every one of five tested algorithm/architecture combinations rather than helping it.
