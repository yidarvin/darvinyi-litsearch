MMTEB: Massive Multilingual Text Embedding Benchmark

A GitHub-coordinated, point-scored effort by 87 contributors expands MTEB from 58 datasets across 112 languages to over 400 datasets (500+ tasks) across 250+ languages (1,051 ISO codes once ~800 bitext-mining-only languages are counted) — and finds that a 560M-parameter multilingual encoder beats billion-parameter LLM-based embedders on most language subsets, while a re-engineered English benchmark reproduces the original's model ranking (Spearman 0.90) from just 2% of its documents.

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

MTEB (Muennighoff et al., 2023) had already unified text-embedding evaluation into one harness — 8 task types, 58 datasets, 112 languages — replacing a landscape where every embedding paper picked its own slice of tasks. But MTEB's multilinguality was thin: most of its non-English coverage came from machine-translated datasets or bitext-mining pairs rather than genuinely native-language tasks, and its 8 task categories didn't cover instruction-following retrieval, long-document retrieval, code retrieval, or reasoning-as-retrieval, all of which had become active research areas by 2024. Two narrower multilingual retrieval efforts existed — MIRACL (18 languages, monolingual retrieval only) and MINERS (200+ languages, but limited to classification and bitext mining) — but neither matched MTEB's task diversity.

There was also a practical barrier baked into MTEB itself: running a single moderately-sized LLM through MTEB(eng, v1) took up to two days on one A100 GPU, and the paper cites HELM's evaluation of a single 7B model consuming over 4,000 GPU hours. That cost falls hardest on exactly the low-resource-language communities MMTEB set out to serve — what the paper calls the "low-resource double bind" (Ahia et al., 2021): the languages with the least benchmark coverage are also the ones whose contributors have the least compute to run a comprehensive one. So the gap MMTEB targets is two-sided: broaden task and language coverage far beyond MTEB's 58/112, and simultaneously cut the compute cost of running the larger benchmark, rather than trading one for the other.

// how it works

The method

MMTEB is a community-built superset of MTEB, coordinated entirely through GitHub with a points system (modeled on Lovenia et al., 2024's SEACrowd) to assign authorship credit: 2 points for implementing a task, 4 points per new language a dataset introduces, 1 point per metadata annotation, 2–10 points for fixes, 1 point per model run, and 2 points per PR review, awarded to each reviewer when a PR had several. The printed byline lists 87 names in the PDF (one more than the 86 names in arXiv's and Semantic Scholar's structured author metadata — Saksham Thakur appears in the print byline but not in either service's indexed author list). Of those 87, 11 are marked with a double-dagger as the "Managing Team," and first author Kenneth Enevoldsen carries the correspondence mark. Every submitted task had to pass a quality gate: at least one reviewer, required metadata (annotation source, license, dialect, citation), and a sanity check against two reference models (multilingual-e5-small, MiniLM-L12) — a task was flagged for further scrutiny if either model scored within 2% of a random baseline, near-perfect, or indistinguishably from each other, though a task could still be kept if the low score reflected genuine difficulty rather than a broken implementation.

From this pool of 500+ tasks, MMTEB derives several purpose-built benchmarks by the same three-stage funnel (Initial Scope → Refined Scope → Task Selection and Review): first restrict to tasks matching the benchmark's language/domain goal, then drop machine-translated, under-licensed, or overly narrow-domain tasks, then iteratively remove the most statistically redundant tasks. Redundancy is measured by treating task selection as feature reduction: an estimator (linear regression) is fit on all-but-one task's scores to predict the held-out task's score for each model, and the task whose score is most predictable from the others is dropped first — repeated until the most-predictable remaining task falls below a Spearman correlation threshold with its predicted value (0.8 for MTEB(Multilingual), 0.9 for the stricter MTEB(eng, v2)), while never removing a task that would eliminate a whole language or task category from the collection.

Running all of this affordably required three separate efficiency interventions (Section 2.3). Clustering is bootstrapped: instead of clustering the full corpus, MMTEB encodes a 4% subsample, draws 10 sets without replacement, and clusters each — validated on 9 English clustering tasks against 13 models, giving an average 16.11× speedup (re-derivable from the paper's own per-task table: the 9 printed speedups average to 16.10×, matching to rounding) at an average Spearman rank-correlation of 0.9634 between the fast and original model rankings. Retrieval is downsampled with TREC-pooling: the top 250 documents ranked by each of three models (BM25, e5-multilingual-large, e5-Mistral-Instruct-7B) are kept per query, and datasets with more than 1,000 queries are capped at 1,000 queries — arithmetically a ceiling of 250 × 1,000 = 250,000 documents, down from over 5 million in the largest original datasets. The 250-document threshold was chosen conservatively: the paper's own validation (Natural Questions for sparse judgments, TREC-COVID for dense ones) found NQ's model ranking stable even at 1 hard negative, but TREC-COVID's ranking "becomes unstable starting at 100 hard negatives" — so 250 leaves a margin above the point where it starts to break for the harder case. Bitext mining exploits shared source sentences across language pairs (e.g. Flores' English side appears in every English-X pair) to cache embeddings once instead of quadratically — cutting Flores' English document count from 410,000 in MTEB(eng, v1) to 1,012.

// what they found

The results

Twelve models — from all-MiniLM-L6 (22M) up through GritLM-7B and e5-mistral-7b-instruct (both Mistral-7B-based) — are evaluated on three flagship benchmarks and ranked by Borda count. On MTEB(Multilingual) (132 tasks, 12 rows), multilingual-e5-large-instruct tops every model with a Borda score of 1375 and an average-across-all-tasks of 63.2, ahead of GritLM-7B (1258, 60.9) and e5-mistral-7b-instruct (1233, 60.3) — despite being roughly 13× smaller (560M vs. ~7B parameters). The same model also tops MTEB(Indic) (23 tasks) at 70.2, with GritLM-7B and e5-mistral-7b-instruct falling all the way to ranks 5 and 6 there (60.2 and 60.0) — a much larger drop than on the other two benchmarks. But the ranking is not stable across benchmarks: on MTEB(Europe) (74 tasks) GritLM-7B actually finishes first (Borda 757, avg 63.0), with multilingual-e5-large-instruct second (732, 62.2) — the paper attributes this to Mistral-based models' narrower, more European-centric pre-training data disproportionately helping on Europe's language set while hurting on Indic's.

MTEB(eng, v2) reruns MTEB(eng, v1)'s 56 tasks through the optimizations above, drops tasks like MS MARCO and Natural Questions that are common fine-tuning targets (to keep it a genuine zero-shot benchmark), and lands on 41 remaining tasks after task selection — Table 1 and §2.4 both give 41, though the paper's own §4 later states "a reduction from 56 to 40 tasks" for the same benchmark, an inconsistency this page surfaces rather than silently resolving in either section's favor. Before selection, simply swapping in the optimized task variants already reproduces MTEB(eng, v1) at Spearman 0.97 (Pearson 0.99); the final v2 still correlates with v1 at Spearman 0.90 (Pearson 0.96), while cutting runtime for a 7B model from roughly two days to 3.11 hours on an H100 (0.81 hours for all-MiniLM-L12) using "only 2% of the original documents (6% of the original number of characters)," per the abstract. The one systematic casualty: smaller English models trained on MS MARCO / Natural Questions (all-MiniLM-L12, all-MiniLM-L6, all-mpnet-base) score worse on v2 specifically because those familiar fine-tuning targets were removed.

On the two domain-specific benchmarks reported in the appendix, GritLM-7B outperforms multilingual-e5-large-instruct on both: it's the top model on MTEB(Code) (Borda 88, avg 73.6 vs. e5-large-instruct's rank-3, 65.0), and it beats e5-large-instruct on MTEB(eng, v2) too (66.4 vs. 65.2), though e5-mistral-7b-instruct is actually the top-ranked model there (Borda 393, avg 67.0) — the paper's claim is specifically that GritLM beats e5-large-instruct on these two, not that it's the overall best on either.

// does it hold up?

The eval read

MMTEB's construction discipline is genuinely strong for a benchmark this size — a reviewed submission pipeline, an explicit points/authorship system, and an unusual amount of quantitative self-validation of its own shortcuts (Spearman correlations reported for every efficiency trick, not just claimed). But a few things are worth separating from the headline framing.

The paper's own headline language count contradicts itself, and the honest explanation is about depth, not double-counting. The abstract, §1, and the Limitations section all headline "over 250 languages"; the Conclusion instead headlines "more than 1000 languages" — the paper's own abstract and conclusion disagree with each other on its central multilinguality claim. The Related Work section is the only place that reconciles the two, in one sentence: "the number of languages [extends] to over 1000 (250 excluding bitext-mining tasks)," and Appendix D.2 gives the precise total, 1,051 ISO 639-3 codes. That sentence also gives the real mechanism, and it is not double-counting a language pair's two sides: subtracting the paper's own two numbers (1,051 total, 250 outside bitext-mining tasks) means roughly 800 distinct languages — almost all low-resource — enter MMTEB only through bitext-mining tasks. Figure 1's own per-category breakdown is consistent with this: Bitext Mining is credited with 1,043 languages across just 21 tasks, which cannot be pair-doubling (21 tasks × 2 sides would be 42, not 1,043) — it reflects individual bitext datasets such as BibleNLPBitextMining, which alone lists dozens of ISO codes (aai, aak, aau, …) drawn from Bible-translation corpora. So MMTEB genuinely reaches ~1,051 distinct languages, but for roughly 800 of them the benchmark offers exactly one shallow task type rather than the cross-category depth its ~250 "core" languages receive — a coverage-depth caveat, not an inflated headline.

The task-selection tables don't agree with the paper's own prose in three separate places, not just the language count. MTEB(eng, v2): Table 1 and §2.4 both give 41 as the final task count, but §4 later states "a reduction from 56 to 40 tasks" for the same benchmark — this page uses 41 (corroborated in two places against one) and states 40 as the paper's own alternate figure rather than silently picking one. MTEB(Multilingual): §2.4 states "a final selection of 131 diverse tasks," while Table 1 and Table 2's own dataset-count header both print 132. MTEB(Europe): Appendix H.1 states "a final selection of 96 tasks," while Table 1 and Table 2 both print 74 — the largest of the three gaps. None of these looks like a typo in isolation, but three flagship task-selection counts drifting from the paper's own prose is worth flagging on its own, independent of the language-count discrepancy above.

The efficiency validation is itself thin outside English. The clustering bootstrap (16.11× speedup, Spearman 0.9634) is validated on 9 tasks, all English (Biorxiv/Medrxiv/Reddit/StackExchange/TwentyNewsgroups). The retrieval downsampling (top-250 documents) is validated on exactly two English retrieval sets, NQ and TREC-COVID. Neither speedup technique is re-validated on a non-English or non-Latin-script retrieval or clustering task before being applied across the benchmark's 250+ languages, even though the paper is explicit that TREC-COVID's ranking is already unstable below 100 hard negatives for models tested only in English — a harder retrieval regime than most, but not one that rules out similar instability appearing on some of the many lower-resource retrieval tasks the same 250-document threshold is applied to uniformly. The one place the paper does check a non-English construction method — comparing GPT-4-generated synthetic queries against gold German QA data (GermanQuAD) for the new WikipediaRetrievalMultilingual/Reranking tasks — reports Spearman 0.93, but with a 95% CI of [0.69, 1.0], i.e. a wide interval consistent with a fairly small evaluation sample, and it covers 1 of the 17 languages that construction method was used to build tasks in.

Two limitations are the paper's own, stated plainly rather than surfaced by outside scrutiny, and are worth repeating because they bear directly on how much weight to put on the "250+ languages" framing: (1) the language distribution is "skewed toward high-resource languages" — English backs 290 tasks by the paper's own Figure 6 caption (Appendix Table 8's own tally for English is 303, a close but not identical count — both numbers come from the paper itself), while the next-highest languages, German and French, back only 58 and 57 tasks respectively (Table 8), an order of magnitude below English, with a long tail of the appendix's other 97-of-100 listed languages backing single digits to a few dozen; and (2) MMTEB permits human-translated datasets even though it filters out machine-translated ones, which the authors note can leak English-centric labeling into supposedly non-English tasks (their example: SIB200ClusteringS2S, where English-derived labels transfer onto translations).

baselines

12 encoders spanning 22M–7B parameters and both encoder-only and decoder-based (Mistral) architectures — a reasonable spread for a 2024/2025 multilingual embedding paper, though every model is evaluated zero-shot with generic prompts per task category rather than per-task-tuned prompts, which the paper itself notes may understate instruction-tuned models' true ceiling.

contamination

Not directly addressed for the retrieval/clustering downsampling (no train/pretraining-leakage analysis is attempted); the one contamination-adjacent design choice is constructing MTEB(eng, v2) as zero-shot by removing MS MARCO and Natural Questions, which are common embedding fine-tuning targets.

what's missed

The clustering and retrieval speedup validations are English-only; the one non-English construction check (German synthetic-vs-gold retrieval, Spearman 0.93) has a wide [0.69, 1.0] CI and covers 1 of 17 languages that method built tasks for; and the "250+" vs. "1000+"/"1,051" language-count figures need the depth-per-language caveat (~800 of the ~1,051 languages are reachable only via bitext-mining tasks) to be read correctly, which appears in one sentence of the Related Work section.

would raise confidence

Re-running the clustering/retrieval downsampling validation on at least one non-Latin-script, non-English task per category; reporting the per-language task-count distribution (not just the top-100 table) directly against the "250+ languages" headline; and reconciling Figure 1's 1090/550/17 with the body text's 500+/250+/10 explicitly, rather than leaving it to a footnote pointing at a live GitHub page.

// bottom line

MMTEB is a genuinely large expansion of MTEB with unusually well-documented efficiency tricks — the clustering and retrieval speedups are validated with real correlation numbers, not just asserted, and the finding that a 560M-parameter XLM-R-based encoder beats 7B-parameter LLM embedders on most language subsets is a solid, table-backed result. The parts worth double-checking before repeating them are the exact scale of "how multilingual": the headline "250+ languages" and the Related Work's "1000+"/"1,051" are both true, but the gap between them isn't double-counting — it's that roughly 800 of those ~1,051 languages are reachable only through bitext-mining tasks, so most of the benchmark's claimed breadth is one shallow task type deep rather than genuinely absent — and the efficiency methodology that's applied across all 250+ core languages is validated almost entirely in English.

// takeaways

What to remember

  1. MMTEB expands MTEB from 58 datasets / 112 languages to over 400 datasets (500+ tasks) across 10 task categories and 250+ languages — but "250+ languages" (the headline figure) and "1000+"/"1,051" (the Related Work / appendix figure) aren't a double-count: roughly 800 of the ~1,051 distinct languages are reachable only through bitext-mining tasks (Bitext Mining alone lists 1,043 languages across just 21 tasks, e.g. BibleNLPBitextMining's dozens of Bible-translation-derived codes), so most of that breadth is one shallow task type deep, not inflated.
  2. A 560M-parameter multilingual-e5-large-instruct (XLM-R-based) beats billion-parameter Mistral-based embedders (GritLM-7B, e5-mistral-7b-instruct) on MTEB(Multilingual) and especially MTEB(Indic) — but GritLM-7B actually ranks first on MTEB(Europe), so "the best publicly available model" depends on which regional slice of MMTEB is being asked.
  3. The efficiency contributions are real and quantitatively validated (16.11× clustering speedup at Spearman 0.9634; MTEB(eng, v2) reproduces v1's ranking at Spearman 0.90 using ~2% of the documents in 3.11 vs. ~48 GPU-hours) — but that validation is almost entirely English-only, even though the same downsampling thresholds are then applied uniformly across 250+ languages.
