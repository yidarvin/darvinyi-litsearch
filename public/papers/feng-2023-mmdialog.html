MMDialog: A Large-scale Multi-turn Dialogue Dataset Towards Multi-modal Open-domain Conversation

MMDialog crowdsources nothing: it mines 1.08 million real multi-turn dialogues and 1.53 million images from social media across 4,184 hashtag topics — 88x more dialogues than PhotoChat, the prior human-human dataset it benchmarks itself against. On top of the dataset the authors define two response-prediction tasks (generation and retrieval) and a CLIP-based MM-Relevance metric built to score multi-modal responses whose text/image ordering doesn't line up neatly with the ground truth — the metric LoCoMo (2024) later reuses directly for its own multi-modal dialogue-generation task.

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

By late 2022, multi-modal dialogue datasets fell into two flawed families. The first is crowd-sourced image-grounding: Visual Dialog (Das et al., 2017), IGC (Mostafazadeh et al., 2017), and Image-Chat (Shuster et al., 2020) all sit annotators in front of a shared image and have them talk about it. That structurally means "the topics of human utterances in a dialogue session are often triggered and grounded by these images," which the authors argue is "inconsistent with our daily communications, where the utterances are not always image-related." The second family derives images artificially rather than capturing them from a real multi-modal exchange: OpenViDial 1.0/2.0 (Meng et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2021) extract dialogue and visual context straight out of movies and TV series, and Lee et al. (2021) build a dataset by replacing selected utterances with retrieved relevant images — in both cases the images are bolted onto a conversation after the fact, not exchanged as part of it.

A few datasets get closer — PhotoChat (Zang et al., 2021) is the first human-human dialogue dataset with genuine photo-sharing acts, and MMChat (Zheng et al., 2022) pulls from real Chinese social-media conversations — but both "are still limited by their small scale or lack of domain diversity." Table 1's comparison against PhotoChat makes the scale gap concrete: PhotoChat has 12.29K dialogues, 10.92K images, and 89 topics; MMDialog has 1.08M dialogues, 1.53M images, and 4,184 topics. The paper states this makes MMDialog "the largest multi-modal conversation dataset by the number of dialogues by 88x" and, to the authors' knowledge, "the first million-scale open-domain multi-modal dialogue corpora."

// how it works

The method

MMDialog is a data-collection paper, a task-definition paper, and a metric paper rolled into one: it (1) scrapes and filters the dataset, (2) formalizes two response-prediction tasks on top of it, and (3) proposes a new metric because the standard single-modality metrics can't actually score either task correctly.

Collecting the dataset

The data comes from "one of the most influential online social platforms" (unnamed in the paper), accessed via its academic API, in three phases. First, the authors manually screened 4,184 popular hashtags (e.g. "#travel," "#friends," "#golf"), each required to have at least 1,000 dialogues, to seed open-domain topic coverage. Second, for each hashtag they crawled every turn containing it and kept only the turns with at least one attached image — called anchors — then recursively followed reply chains from each anchor up to the root and down to every leaf to reconstruct full multi-turn conversations. Third, a filtering pass removed: (a) toxic dialogues with explicit offensive words; (b) turns containing GIFs or video (left as future work); (c) irregular characters, URLs, and @-mentions; while (d) converting emojis and hashtags into natural-language text, (e) discarding dialogues with two or more consecutive self-replies, (f) dropping incomplete/missing images, and (g) keeping only dialogues with at least 3 turns.

The result is 1,079,117 unique dialogues and 1,531,341 images, averaging 2.59 images and 4.56 turns per dialogue with 15.90 tokens per turn on average (vs. PhotoChat's 0.89 images, 12.71 turns, and 6.33 tokens per turn) — images can appear at any turn, not just the first. The authors split this into 1,059,117 training / 10,000 validation / 10,000 test dialogues (4,825,053 / 45,382 / 45,801 turns respectively), and for the retrieval task additionally sample 999 negative text candidates and 999 negative image candidates per validation/test dialogue.

Two tasks, plus a shared sub-task

Given context U and ground-truth response R, both possibly containing a mix of textual utterances and images, the paper defines: Task-1, Multi-modal Response Generation — learn P(R|U;θ) and directly synthesize a response R̃ made of text, images, or both; and Task-2, Multi-modal Response Retrieval — given a candidate set of ground-truth plus negative text elements (Cᵗ) and a separate candidate set of ground-truth plus negative image elements (Cᵛ), extract elements step by step, auto-regressively, to assemble a full retrieved response. Both tasks share a third sub-problem, Response Modal Intent Prediction: at each step j, predict whether the next response element is text (0), image (1), or that the response is complete (2) — because in MMDialog, unlike in a fixed template, text and images can appear in any order.

Why the standard metrics don't work here, and MM-Relevance

BLEU/ROUGE assume a single modality and a metric like FID's zero-value convention for "no corresponding element" doesn't transfer cleanly across the text/image boundary; more fundamentally, a generated or retrieved response's sequence of text-then-image-then-text may simply be ordered differently than the ground truth's, so directly diffing content at each position conflates "got the content wrong" with "got the modality order wrong." MM-Relevance addresses this by aligning the ground-truth sequence R = {rlm} and the predicted sequence R̃ = {r̃jm} from the left, encoding each element with the matching pre-trained CLIP encoder (text encoder for text, image encoder for images), and scoring corresponding positions by dot product up to whichever sequence is shorter:

MMRel(R, R̃) = Σi=1min(L,J) (ei)ᵀ · ẽi // L = ground-truth length, J = predicted length; e / ẽ are CLIP-encoded vectors of the aligned i-th elements
PMM = MMRel / J    RMM = MMRel / L    F1MM = 2·PMM·RMMPMM + RMM // F1_MM is reported as "MM-Relevance" — dividing by J (soft precision) and L (soft recall) penalizes responses that are too long or too short, not just wrong

The intent-prediction sub-task is scored the same aligned-F1 way, but on a 0/1 match indicator between predicted and ground-truth modality labels at each position rather than a CLIP dot product.

Baselines

For generation, the paper adapts Divter (Sun et al., 2022): a textual response generator G — DialoGPT-medium (345M) fine-tuned, with context images first converted to text descriptions via OFA-huge so the whole context becomes one token sequence prefixed with [UTT]/[DST] markers — followed by a description-to-image translator F, DALL-E mega (fine-tuned one epoch, learning rate 1e-8, batch size 64, 256×256 output), which renders any generated [DST]-prefixed description into an image. For retrieval, the paper builds DE++, inspired by Zang et al. (2021)'s dual-encoder and Parekh et al. (2021): a frozen CLIP (openai/clip-vit-base-patch32) encodes text and images, an intent module Rα (a 4-layer, 8-head, 512-hidden Transformer over concatenated context embeddings plus a learnable [CLS] token) predicts whether to stop or which modality comes next, and a ranking module Rβ scores each candidate by dot product between context and candidate representations, selecting the top-scoring one per step. Retrieval training used learning rate 5e-7 and batch size 512; both baselines trained on 8× NVIDIA A100 80GB GPUs.

// what they found

The results

On the generation task (Table 3), Divter scores 71.77 intent F1, 20.53 ± 0.50 Inception Score on the images it generates, 9.44 BLEU-1 / 7.45 BLEU-2 / 11.19 ROUGE-L on the text it generates, and 61.85 MM-Relevance overall. The paper reads the low textual scores as evidence of task difficulty rather than a broken baseline: "the state-of-the-art model Divter achieves relatively low textual response generation performance … which validates the difficulty of multi-modal response generation tasks." It also notes the model does comparatively better at image generation (IS 20.53) than at text generation, and that a 71.77 intent F1 shows "considerable ability to determine whether to generate text or images during the conversation."

On the retrieval task (Table 4), DE++ scores 82.69 intent F1, image retrieval Recall@1/5/10 of 18.23 / 26.99 / 31.73, textual response retrieval Recall@1/5/10 of 23.07 / 39.21 / 47.05, and 68.91 MM-Relevance. DE++'s intent F1 (82.69) beats Divter's (71.77), and its MM-Relevance (68.91) beats Divter's (61.85) — the paper attributes part of that MM-Relevance gap to DE++'s stronger intent prediction: "we observe that the alignment of the modality would considerably improve the CLIP matching scores."

// does it hold up?

The eval read

MMDialog's core contribution — real, unscripted multi-modal dialogue at social-media scale — is a genuine step up in provenance over crowd-sourced or movie-extracted alternatives, and the paper is unusually candid that its own baselines are weak on the harder task ("relatively low textual response generation performance … validates the difficulty"). But the evaluation section has three specific gaps worth naming.

Exactly one baseline per task, with no non-neural floor. Divter is the sole generation baseline and DE++ is the sole retrieval baseline — there is no ablation of Divter's or DE++'s own components (e.g., a version without the OFA-huge image-to-description step, or a version of DE++ with a non-CLIP encoder), and no naive reference point (a random-candidate retrieval baseline, or a most-common-response generation baseline) against which 18.23% image R@1 or a BLEU-1 of 9.44 could be sanity-checked. The paper's numbers are internally comparable (DE++ beats Divter on intent F1 and MM-Relevance) but there's no external floor establishing what "bad" looks like on this specific dataset.

MM-Relevance's human-correlation check exists but is thin, and the correlations it reports are modest. §8.4 does validate the metric: 200 randomly sampled test-set contexts were rated by three English-speaking volunteers on a 1–5 Likert scale (Fleiss' κ 0.52 generation / 0.47 retrieval — "relatively high agreement"), and Table 6 reports MM-Relevance beating BLEU-1, BLEU-2, ROUGE-L, and CLIP-Sim on both tasks. But the winning numbers are Pearson 0.4043 (generation) and 0.2863 (retrieval) — the metric explains at best a minority of the variance in human judgment, and the retrieval-side correlation (where DE++ posts its headline 68.91 MM-Relevance) is the weaker of the two. The validation is also a single 200-context sample from one test set with three volunteer raters, not an independent or larger-scale check. And the paper's own explanation for DE++'s MM-Relevance edge over Divter — better intent prediction improving "the CLIP matching scores" — is itself a sign the metric partly rewards correct modality-ordering rather than pure content quality, a property the correlation study doesn't isolate.

The qualitative evidence is a single dialogue. Figure 3's Pink Floyd/prog-rock case study is the only worked example in the paper, and there is no broken-down performance analysis across the 4,184 hashtag topics the dataset claims to span — so it's untested whether the reported numbers hold roughly steady across, say, "#travel" versus "#music," or whether certain topic clusters (visual, object-centric ones like albums and landmarks) are systematically easier for CLIP-based retrieval than others. The source platform itself is also never named (just "one of the most influential online social platforms"), which limits how a reader can independently audit topic or demographic skew in the crawl.

baselines

One model per task (Divter for generation, DE++ for retrieval), no ablations of either model's own components, and no non-neural or random reference point to calibrate the absolute numbers against.

contamination

Not pretraining leakage — this is a freshly scraped, filtered social-media corpus. The open question is provenance transparency: the source platform is never named, so topic/demographic skew introduced by the crawl or by the keyword-based toxicity filter can't be independently audited.

what's missed

MM-Relevance is validated (§8.4, Table 6: 200 sampled test contexts, 3 raters, Fleiss' κ 0.52/0.47) but the correlations are modest — Pearson 0.4043 generation / 0.2863 retrieval — so it beats BLEU/ROUGE/CLIP-Sim while still leaving most of human judgment unexplained, worse on the retrieval side where DE++'s headline 68.91 comes from; the paper's own account of DE++'s MM-Relevance edge (better intent prediction, not necessarily better content) reinforces that the metric partly rewards ordering over content.

would raise confidence

A larger and more diverse human-correlation sample than 200 contexts / 3 volunteer raters, with a stated breakdown of how those 200 contexts split across the generative and retrieval labelings (the paper never says); at least one non-CLIP or random-candidate baseline per task; a per-hashtag-topic breakdown of the automatic metrics to check the 4,184-topic claim translates into roughly uniform difficulty.

// bottom line

MMDialog's real contribution is scale and provenance: a million-dialogue, real-human, image-anywhere corpus that genuinely dwarfs PhotoChat's 12.29K. The two baselines it ships are honestly framed as weak starting points rather than solved tasks, and MM-Relevance is a sensible answer to a real scoring problem, backed by an actual (if thin) human-correlation study — but that study's own numbers (Pearson 0.4043 generation, 0.2863 retrieval, from 200 contexts and 3 raters) show the metric still explains a minority of human judgment, so the single-example case study, single-baseline-per-task design, and modestly-validated headline metric together mean the reported numbers should be read as "this is hard, here's a first stake in the ground" rather than a calibrated measurement of how good multi-modal dialogue generation currently is.

// takeaways

What to remember

  1. Scale and provenance, not modeling, is the contribution. 1,079,117 real dialogues and 1,531,341 images across 4,184 hashtag topics, scraped and filtered from real social-media conversations rather than crowd-sourced around a shown image — 88x more dialogues than PhotoChat (12.29K), the prior human-human dataset it benchmarks itself against.
  2. Two tasks, one shared sub-problem. Multi-modal Response Generation (Divter: DialoGPT-medium + DALL-E mega) and Multi-modal Response Retrieval (DE++: CLIP encoders + a Transformer ranker) both also require Response Modal Intent Prediction — deciding whether the next response element is text, image, or "done" — because MMDialog lets images land at any turn.
  3. MM-Relevance is the paper's most durable artifact. A CLIP-based, position-aligned precision/recall/F1 score designed to compare two multi-modal sequences even when their text/image ordering doesn't match — DE++ scores 68.91 vs. Divter's 61.85, with the paper attributing part of that gap to DE++'s better intent prediction rather than pure content quality.
  4. The baselines are candidly weak, and the metric that judges them is validated but only modestly correlated with humans. Divter's own textual generation numbers (BLEU-1 9.44, ROUGE-L 11.19) are described by the authors themselves as low; MM-Relevance, introduced in this same paper, is checked against 200 human-rated test contexts (§8.4) and beats BLEU/ROUGE/CLIP-Sim there, but its Pearson correlation with human ratings is 0.4043 (generation) / 0.2863 (retrieval) — still a minority of the variance in human judgment — and it's exactly this metric that LoCoMo (2024) later reuses directly for its own multi-modal dialogue-generation task.
