ARB: Advanced Reasoning Benchmark for Large Language Models

A benchmark built deliberately out of graduate-level exam and problem-book material — math, physics, MCAT, and law — so it can't be solved by pattern-matching a high-school test bank. GPT-4, ChatGPT, GPT-3.5, and Claude all do noticeably better on the multiple-choice sections than on the numerical or symbolic ones, but even the multiple-choice scores are far from saturated on Law regardless of model — GPT-4 included, at roughly 63% — and the paper's second contribution — a GPT-4 rubric grader for open-ended proofs — tracks human scores well enough to be interesting, not well enough to replace them.

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

By mid-2023, the standard yardsticks for LLM reasoning — GSM8K, MATH, MMLU, BIG-bench, HELM — were starting to lose their discriminating power: models were racking up increasingly high scores on them without anything close to expert-level performance in the domains those benchmarks claim to test. The paper frames this as a recurring pattern going back to GLUE-then-SuperGLUE: "the race between language models and benchmarks has increasingly favored the former." Two things push a benchmark toward saturation and shallow signal in particular: it draws on material that's already public and easily scraped (raising contamination risk), and it leans on multiple-choice framing, which is comparatively easy to game and doesn't test a model's ability to actually produce an answer.

ARB's response is to source problems from places that are both harder and less thoroughly scraped than the usual benchmark pipeline: contest-mathematics books, a Harvard PhD qualifying-exam set, a "Major American Universities PhD Qualifying Questions" physics series, a practice bar exam, and MCAT practice-test books. It also deliberately shifts the mix of answer formats: rather than the multiple-choice questions that "dominated earlier benchmarks," a large share of ARB's math and physics problems require a short numerical/symbolic answer or a full open-ended proof — formats the paper argues are both harder for a model to fake and closer to what expert reasoning actually looks like. A second, related gap is evaluation itself: grading open-ended proofs and symbolic derivations at scale is expensive because it needs a human with domain training, so the paper also asks whether an LLM (specifically GPT-4) can be turned into a partial-credit grader against a rubric, as a cheaper proxy.

// how it works

The method

ARB spans four subject areas — mathematics, physics, law, and the MCAT (split into a reading-comprehension and a science portion) — with three question formats in descending order of how much of the dataset they make up: multiple choice (4-5 options, sourced from MCAT and bar-exam prep material, chosen partly because they're cheap to grade), short answer (a numerical value or, for a subset, a symbolic mathematical expression, sourced from problem books such as Putnam and Beyond and Souza & Silva's undergraduate mathematics text, plus five books from the "Major American Universities" PhD-qualifying-questions physics series), and open response (full natural-language proofs, sourced from the same math problem books plus a Harvard PhD qualifying exam, graded only by hand). The paper is explicit that it skipped algebraic short-answer questions where possible, because a grading scheme robust to every equivalent form of an algebraic expression "is not feasible" at dataset scale — an admission that shapes which problems could even be included.

Grading is format-specific. Multiple choice is exact-match against the parsed final letter. Numerical answers are parsed with SymPy and scored correct if the relative error against the ground truth is under 1%:

correct ⟺ |model_answer − ground_truth|ground_truth < 0.01 // numerical-answer scoring threshold, §4

Symbolic answers are normalized to a common variable-naming scheme and checked for equivalence up to permutation of variables with SymPy — a method the paper flags as "error-prone" and only usable for the subset of responses in clean function form; anything with set notation or more exotic structure needs a human. Proof-like answers are graded only by the authors, who have mathematical training, since natural-language proofs "cannot be evaluated automatically."

The paper's second contribution is a rubric-based evaluation pipeline: for symbolic and proof-like problems, GPT-4 is few-shot prompted (using hand-written example rubrics) to turn a reference solution into a 10-point rubric that breaks the problem into weighted sub-steps. GPT-4 is then separately prompted to grade a candidate solution step-by-step against that rubric and award partial credit. The authors validate this pipeline by hand-checking rubric quality (coverage and point-allocation, on a 1-5 Likert scale) and by having human annotators independently grade the same solutions against the same rubrics, then correlating the two sets of scores.

// what they found

The results

Four models were evaluated: gpt-4-0314 (8k context), gpt-3.5-turbo-0301, text-davinci-003, and claude-v1.3-100k, all with chain-of-thought prompting and a required ANSWER: delimiter for parsing. On the mechanically-scored subsets — Law, MCAT Reading, MCAT Science (all multiple choice), plus Physics Numerical and Math Numerical — GPT-4 leads every group in Figure 1: it's roughly 80-87% on the two MCAT splits and around 60-63% on Law, clearly ahead of the other three models, which cluster in the upper-30s-to-mid-40s on Law and mid-50s-to-high-70s on the MCAT splits. But all four models collapse on the numerical splits: even GPT-4 stays under roughly 20% on Physics Numerical and under 5% on Math Numerical, with the other three models scoring only a few percentage points on both. The paper's own read: "GPT-4 is the only model capable of reliably simplifying complex expressions, but even GPT-4 struggles to reliably perform arithmetic and symbolic manipulations over long contexts." Answer-format compliance also splits sharply by model — gpt-3.5-turbo fails to output a parsable multiple-choice answer around 25% of the time on the Law set (often refusing to commit to a choice), versus under 5% for every other model, with GPT-4 correctly parsed over 99% of the time.

On the symbolic subset — where answers are manually parsed rather than auto-graded — scores are low across the board and GPT-4 leads both columns:

An error-taxonomy pass — which subsamples the larger subject cells down to 20-40 problems each (GPT-4 only, categories non-exclusive so a single response can be tagged more than once) — finds "logical error or hallucination" as the single most common tag on three of the five math/physics rows — 88% on Math Numerical, 72% on Math Proof-like, 68% on Physics Symbolic — with "wrong approach" (the chain of thought never converges on a workable method) leading the other two: 80% on Physics Numerical, 50% on Math Symbolic. Arithmetic mistakes range widely by subject, from 4% (Math Symbolic) to 48% (Math Numerical). Grading Proof-like more leniently — awarding half credit when at least half of a multi-part problem's sub-results are correct — lifts GPT-4's Proof-like score to 16% (Table 3 marks the strict "correct answer" rate for this row n/a, since proof problems don't reduce to a single checkable value; the table's separate "correct reasoning" tag, at 5%, is a different, stricter judgment of the whole chain of thought, not a baseline for the 16% figure). Table 3's own "correct answer" column being marked n/a for this row is itself the point: the paper reports no strict correct-final-answer rate for Proof-like problems at all, so the 16% partial-credit score has no printed baseline to be measured against.

On the rubric-based grading experiment, GPT-4 rubrics score well on coverage of the reference solution's key steps (4.42/4.26/3.94 on a 1-5 Likert scale for Physics Symbolic/Math Symbolic/Proof-like) but somewhat worse on fairly allocating points across those steps for Physics and Math Symbolic (4.16 and 4.00, versus 4.42 and 4.26 on coverage) — though on Proof-like the two are essentially level (4.06 point-spread vs 3.94 coverage) — and even show a "hallucinated rubric" failure — GPT-4 awarding credit by referencing a rubric line that isn't actually in the rubric — 15% of the time on Math Symbolic (0% elsewhere). GPT-4 systematically over-scores relative to the human grader, and the gap widens as problems get less structured: +0.05 on Physics Symbolic, +0.24 on Math Symbolic, and +1.15 on Proof-like — the paper attributes the main failure mode to GPT-4 awarding partial credit to attempts that fall entirely outside the rubric, "where the human evaluation score is always zero."

// does it hold up?

The eval read

ARB is a small, hand-curated benchmark — 1,207 problems in total by our count from Table 1, but overwhelmingly weighted toward the cheapest-to-grade format: 627 of them (52%) are Law multiple choice, and Law + MCAT together (973 problems) make up about 81% of the dataset. The harder, headline-generating slice — the numerical, symbolic, and proof-like math/physics problems where the paper's low scores actually live — is only about 234 problems split across seven answer-type/subject cells, several with fewer than 20 items (Math Proof-like has 19, Physics Symbolic-with-image has 13). That's a defensible design for a benchmark meant to be hard rather than exhaustive, but it does mean the headline "well below 50%" numbers on the demanding subsets are drawn from small cells, and the error-taxonomy breakdown (Table 3) explicitly subsamples the larger cells down to 20-40 problems each on top of that — the paper is candid about this, noting the sample "is enough for a ballpark estimate... and is not worth increasing because attributing error types is inherently fuzzy," but it means Table 3's percentages should be read as rough proportions, not precise rates.

The rubric-based grading experiment is the more interesting methodological contribution, and it's also the one with the least specified evaluation protocol: Table 5's caption states its correlations are computed "over all problems" per column, which implies full-cell samples of n=18 (Physics Symbolic), 34 (Math Symbolic), and 19 (Proof-like) per Table 1 — a scope consistent with, though not directly proven by, Appendix Table 8's separate diagnostic, which independently reports n=18 and n=34 on the two symbolic cells. But the paper never restates a count next to Table 5 itself, so a reader still can't be fully certain how much sampling noise sits under a correlation of 0.78 or 0.91. It's also worth being precise about what that correlation validates: it's agreement between a human grader and GPT-4 grading GPT-4's own solutions, using rubrics that GPT-4 itself generated from the reference solution. Nothing in the paper checks whether the same rubric pipeline holds up when GPT-4 grades a different model's solutions, or whether a rubric written by a weaker rubric-generator (the paper notes GPT-3.5-turbo already writes much worse rubrics) degrades the correlation — so "rubric-based evaluation is a promising automated evaluation method," as the paper concludes, is really a claim about one grader validated on itself, not a general grading recipe. One appendix result is a useful counterpoint on the direction of the bias, though: tested on a narrower task (deciding whether two symbolic expressions are equivalent, not grading against a rubric), gpt-3.5-turbo shows zero false positives but misses most of the equivalences it should have credited — 6 of the 9 genuinely-equivalent Physics Symbolic answers and 8 of the 12 in Math Symbolic, for overall accuracies of 0.67 (n=18) and 0.76 (n=34) — Table 8 — a cheap automated grader that systematically under-credits, the opposite failure mode from GPT-4's rubric-based over-crediting above.

On contamination, the paper is unusually upfront rather than silent: it states plainly that "as with all other benchmarks that are not created anew and kept secret, it is possible there is data contamination," singles out the MCAT prep books as plausibly already inside some model's training data despite not being freely available, and gates dataset access behind an API rather than a public download specifically to slow future leakage — a choice made after explicitly weighing the alternative: Appendix C notes "there are several proposed strategies to mitigate this problem, including dataset poisoning and canary text methods," before concluding "restricting dataset accessibility to web crawlers seemed to be the only way to ensure integrity of our dataset for future uses." It also looks for memorization directly, and with more than one signal: beyond the single qualitative case in Appendix F (Table 12) — GPT-3.5-turbo reproducing a capacitance formula correctly despite visibly wrong reasoning, while GPT-4 stays faithful to its (wrong) reasoning chain and gets a worse final answer — §4.2 flags a more systematic pattern, noting that "many of the problems in Physics Symbolic have correct symbolic answers even when there are flaws in the chain of thought reasoning of GPT-4," which it attributes to "some kind of memorization." Table 3 itself is consistent with that: GPT-4's Physics Symbolic correct-answer rate (28%) is more than double its correct-reasoning rate (12%). The paper's own conclusion is still a hedge rather than a clearance, though: "the overall performance of all models we test is still somewhat low, hence not majorly affected by memorization of similar problems." That's a fair read for a mid-2023 snapshot, and a more substantive check than a single anecdote, but it still isn't a systematic contamination audit — no exact-match or n-gram search against training-adjacent corpora — so it should be read as "no major red flag found" rather than "cleared."

One genuinely floor-level result deserves a flag rather than a superlative: the Math/Physics Symbolic cells sit at 3-12% for the non-GPT-4 models (Table 2), and the Numerical cells (Figure 1) are lower still — roughly 0-5% for every non-GPT-4 model — which is close to what a model could hit by chance on an open-format numerical answer. At that level the benchmark is still doing its job — showing these tasks are essentially unsolved — but the exact ranking among the bottom three models on any single cell is not a meaningful signal.

baselines

Four contemporaneous 2023 models (GPT-4-0314, gpt-3.5-turbo-0301, text-davinci-003, claude-v1.3-100k), all zero-shot with chain-of-thought (few-shot prompting is used only in the rubric-generation step, not in solving the problems) — reasonable coverage for the moment it was written, but it's a snapshot: no open-weight model, no later reasoning-tuned model, and (per the paper itself) no multimodal evaluation of the image-bearing Physics/MCAT problems, which are explicitly held out "due to the lack of good multimodal models" at the time.

contamination

Addressed directly and honestly (see above) — MCAT prep-book contamination flagged as plausible, canary-text methods explicitly weighed and traded for API-gating, and two memorization signals examined (one anecdote, one Table-3-quantified pattern on Physics Symbolic) — but there's still no exact-match or n-gram audit against training corpora, so "no major effect observed" rests on qualitative checks, not a measurement.

what's missed

The rubric-grading validation is agreement-with-self (GPT-4 grading GPT-4, on GPT-4-generated rubrics) on a sample size the paper never restates next to Table 5 (implied at n=18/34/19 by Table 1's per-cell counts), not a test of whether the pipeline generalizes to grading other models or holds up with a cheaper rubric-generator. The hardest subsets (proof-like, symbolic-with-image) are also the smallest, so per-subject percentages there carry real sampling noise.

would raise confidence

Reporting the human-eval sample size for Table 4/5; testing the rubric pipeline on a second model's solutions and a second rubric-generator; a quantitative (not anecdotal) contamination check, e.g. exact/near-duplicate search against known pretraining-adjacent text for the problem-book sources.

// bottom line

ARB is a well-motivated, honestly-scoped difficulty benchmark — its multiple-choice/numerical split is exactly the axis that makes its point, and its contamination discussion is more candid than most 2023-era benchmarks. Its rubric-evaluation contribution is a promising direction whose validation is narrower than the "promising agreement" framing suggests: it's grader self-agreement on an unreported sample, not a validated general-purpose LLM grader.

// takeaways

What to remember

  1. ARB deliberately sources graduate-level, low-web-visibility material (PhD qualifying exams, problem books, MCAT and bar-exam prep) across math, physics, law and MCAT, and mixes multiple-choice with numerical/symbolic short-answer and open-ended proof formats specifically so a model can't coast on multiple-choice guessing or memorized test banks.
  2. The results show one dominant axis, not many: GPT-4, ChatGPT, GPT-3.5, and Claude-v1.3 all do reasonably well on multiple-choice Law/MCAT but collapse on numerical and symbolic math/physics — GPT-4 stays under roughly 20% on Physics Numerical and under 5% on Math Numerical even while leading every category.
  3. The paper's rubric-based GPT-4 self-evaluation method shows moderately high correlation with human graders (Pearson r of 0.78-0.91 across three problem types) but is validated only as GPT-4-grading-GPT-4 on GPT-4-generated rubrics, with the human-eval sample size never restated next to Table 5 itself — a genuinely useful signal, not yet a drop-in replacement for human grading.
