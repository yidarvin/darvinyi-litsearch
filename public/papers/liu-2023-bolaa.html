BOLAA: Benchmarking and Orchestrating LLM-augmented Autonomous Agents

Salesforce runs six agent architectures across fifteen open- and closed-source LLM backbones on WebShop and HotPotQA, then proposes BOLAA, a controller-orchestrated multi-agent scheme that comes out ahead on most -- but not all -- backbones.

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

By mid-2023, LLM-augmented Autonomous Agents (LAAs) -- BabyAGI, AutoGPT, ReAct, Langchain-style agents -- were multiplying faster than anyone was comparing them. Each new architecture paper (ReAct's fewshot reasoning-then-acting, ReWOO's decoupled planning) argued for its own design choice in isolation, usually paired with a single LLM backbone. Three questions were left open, and the authors name them directly: (1) which agent architecture is actually best -- zero-shot, fewshot, with or without an explicit self-think or planning step; (2) how does that answer change with the LLM backbone, since ReAct was tuned around PaLM, ReWOO around a distilled Alpaca, and MIND2Web only compared Flan-T5 against GPT-3.5/4; and (3), as tasks get more complex, is it better to keep asking one generalist agent to do everything, or to split the work across multiple specialist agents and coordinate them?

The paper's stated aim is to answer all three with one consistent experimental setup -- the same tasks, the same prompt budget per context length, the same metrics -- rather than stitching together numbers from architecture papers that were never run head to head.

// how it works

The method

The paper lays out five solo agent architectures (LAAs) that differ only in their prompt layer and control flow, then adds a sixth orchestration scheme, BOLAA, on top.

Two more solo architectures add an explicit planning stage before any environment interaction: PlanAct generates a fewshot plan up front, stores it in memory, and retrieves it while acting; PlanReAct is PlanAct plus the ReAct-style self-think step layered on top, so the agent both plans ahead and reasons at each intermediate step. In total that gives six data points to compare per backbone: ZS, ZST, ReAct, PlanAct, PlanReAct, and BOLAA.

BOLAA ("Benchmarking and Orchestrating LLM-Augmented Autonomous Agents" -- named to match the paper, by the authors' own admission) is the orchestration proposal. Instead of one LLM handling every action type, a pool of labor LAAs each specialize in one action type, and a separate controller module selects which labor agent to invoke, constructs its message, and parses its response into an executable environment action.

The comparison runs on two existing simulation environments rather than a newly released dataset. WebShop (Yao et al.) is an online shopping environment with 1.18M real products; the authors stratify by task complexity (the number of attribute requirements in the instruction, e.g. "quick release," "camera tripod," "easy carry" -- a price constraint like "price lower than 130.00 dollars" can appear in the instruction text but is not itself counted as an attribute) and sample 150 instructions per complexity level 1-6, for 900 total tasks. HotPotQA with the Wikipedia search API is the multi-hop QA environment; they sample 100 questions each at easy/medium/hard, for 300 total. Reward in WebShop is the attribute-overlap ratio between the purchased and ground-truth item; reward in HotPotQA is F1 against the ground-truth answer; WebShop additionally reports Recall (1 if the ground-truth item was ever retrieved, 0 otherwise). Fifteen backbones are tested: fastchat-t5-3b, vicuna-7b/13b/33b, Llama-2-7b/13b/70b-chat, mpt-7b/30b-instruct, xgen-8k-7b-instruct, longchat-7b/13b-16k, and OpenAI's text-davinci-003, gpt-3.5-turbo, and gpt-3.5-turbo-16k. Because HotPotQA's search/lookup/finish actions are hard to cleanly split across specialist agents, the authors explicitly leave BOLAA out of the HotPotQA comparison and reserve it for future work -- BOLAA is evaluated on WebShop only.

// what they found

The results

On WebShop reward (Table 1), BOLAA posts the single highest cell in the entire table -- 0.6567 with gpt-3.5-turbo, edging out the next-best gpt-3.5-turbo-16k+BOLAA (0.6541) and text-davinci-003+BOLAA (0.6341). Across the fifteen backbones, BOLAA is the row-winning architecture on eleven of them. It is not the best on the other four: ReAct beats it on vicuna-7b (0.1033 vs. 0.0604), PlanAct beats it on llama-2-13b (0.4892 vs. 0.3716), PlanReAct beats it on mpt-7b-instruct (0.1574 vs. 0.0632), and PlanReAct beats it again on longchat-13b-16k (0.4019 vs. 0.3205). The paper's own reading of this pattern is that pairing the right architecture to the right backbone matters as much as the architecture choice itself.

On WebShop Recall (Table 2) the pattern is similar: BOLAA wins eleven of fifteen backbones, but loses to ReAct on vicuna-7b (0.3600 vs. 0.3522), on mpt-7b-instruct (0.3644 vs. 0.3600), on gpt-3.5-turbo (0.3933 vs. 0.3929), and on gpt-3.5-turbo-16k (0.4011 vs. 0.3933) -- notably, the two strongest backbones by reward are exactly where BOLAA's recall edge disappears.

On HotPotQA reward (Table 3, no BOLAA row), ReAct is the row-winner on eleven of fifteen backbones, including the strongest open-source model, llama-2-70b (0.3558) and text-davinci-003 (0.4503, the table's overall maximum). But it does not win everywhere: plain zero-shot (ZS) wins on vicuna-7b (0.1339 vs. ReAct's 0.0318), on gpt-3.5-turbo (0.3340 vs. ReAct's 0.3226), and on gpt-3.5-turbo-16k (0.3027 vs. ReAct's 0.1859); PlanAct wins on fastchat-t5-3b (0.1155). The authors attribute ReAct's general edge here to fewshot examples mattering more for reasoning tasks, and to planning flows (PlanAct, PlanReAct) hurting more than helping because plans generated before the agent sees any context tend to hallucinate.

// does it hold up?

The eval read

The comparison design is genuinely useful -- fixing the environments and metrics and sweeping architecture × backbone is exactly the kind of study the field needed, and the paper is admirably candid about the exceptions to its own headline claims (it explicitly flags that Llama-2-13b prefers PlanAct and Longchat-13b-16k prefers PlanReAct, rather than papering over them). But the paper rounds off in its own favor twice: the Introduction's contributions bullet claims "BOLAA on the WebShop environment consistently yields the best performance compared with other LAA architectures" (p.2), and the Section 4.4 Recall discussion adds, unqualified this time, that "our proposed BOLAA generally performs the best on all LLMs" (p.8). The first at least scopes itself to WebShop reward; the second does not scope itself at all, and both round a result that is closer to most than all: 11/15 on reward, 11/15 on recall, with losses concentrated on exactly the backbones (vicuna-7b, mpt-7b-instruct) or exactly the metric (recall on both GPT-3.5 variants) where a reader would most want the caveat spelled out up front rather than buried three sections down.

The asymmetry in scope is the bigger issue: BOLAA is compared against five solo architectures on WebShop across fifteen backbones, but is never run on HotPotQA at all -- the authors say the environment's action types are "hard to separate," which is a real design constraint, but it also means every claim of BOLAA's superiority in this paper rests on a single environment. A reader can't tell whether orchestration wins because splitting search-vs-click genuinely helps web navigation, or because WebShop's action space happens to be unusually clean to split -- HotPotQA, math tool-use, or code-agent tasks might partition far less cleanly, and the paper offers no evidence either way.

The benchmark construction itself is also worth flagging as a limitation rather than a contribution: the 900 WebShop and 300 HotPotQA tasks are stratified subsamples of two existing public environments (WebShop, HotpotQA), not a new released artifact, and the complexity/difficulty buckets are the authors' own post-hoc labels rather than an externally validated difficulty scale. There's no held-out or contamination check for whether any of the fifteen backbones' pretraining data overlaps with WebShop product listings or HotPotQA passages -- a live concern given how heavily GPT-3.5/text-davinci-003 dominate the small-model backbones on both tables.

baselines

The five solo architectures (ZS, ZST, ReAct, PlanAct, PlanReAct) are a fair, comprehensive 2023 baseline set for solo agents -- but BOLAA, the paper's own contribution, is only ever measured against them on WebShop, never on HotPotQA.

contamination

Not discussed. WebShop products and HotPotQA/Wikipedia passages both plausibly overlap with the pretraining corpora of text-davinci-003, gpt-3.5-turbo, and Llama-2 -- no contamination check is reported for any backbone.

what's missed

Variance across runs (numbers appear to be single runs, not averages with error bars); whether BOLAA's win margin on WebShop actually stems from action-space splitting versus just having two LLM calls per step instead of one; and any test of orchestration on a second environment.

would raise confidence

Report BOLAA results on HotPotQA (or an environment with genuinely separable action types) rather than reserving it for "future work"; add error bars over repeated runs; and an ablation isolating whether BOLAA's gain comes from specialization per se or simply from extra inference compute (two LLM calls vs. one per step).

// bottom line

This is a solid, useful architecture × backbone sweep that the field lacked, and BOLAA is a reasonable, well-motivated orchestration idea -- but its "best on all LLMs" framing overstates a result that wins on most (not all) backbones on one (not two) environments, with no contamination check and no reported variance.

// takeaways

What to remember

  1. Six agent architectures (ZS, ZST, ReAct, PlanAct, PlanReAct, BOLAA) × fifteen LLM backbones × two environments (WebShop, HotPotQA) is a genuinely useful, controlled sweep -- and its unglamorous finding, that the best architecture depends on the backbone (Llama-2-13b prefers PlanAct, Longchat-13b-16k prefers PlanReAct), is arguably more valuable than the BOLAA headline.
  2. BOLAA, a controller that routes actions to specialist "labor" LAAs (e.g. a search agent and a click agent for WebShop), wins on 11/15 backbones for both reward and recall in WebShop -- a real but partial result, narrower than the "consistently" of the paper's own contributions bullet.
  3. BOLAA is never evaluated on HotPotQA at all, so its entire evidence base is one environment; whether multi-agent orchestration generalizes beyond a cleanly-splittable action space like WebShop's search/click remains untested.
