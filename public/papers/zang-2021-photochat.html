PhotoChat: A Human-Human Dialogue Dataset with Photo Sharing Behavior for Joint Image-Text Modeling

PhotoChat pairs 12,286 human-human dialogues with a single photo shared mid-conversation — visible to only one side until the conversation has run at least five turns — then defines two tasks on top of it: predicting whether a photo will be shared on the next turn, and retrieving which of 1,000 candidate photos the dialogue is about. The paper headlines 58.1% F1 for intent prediction (T5-base); the best retrieval baseline reaches only 10.4% recall@1 (SCAN).

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

By 2021, image-text modeling already had well-established datasets and tasks — but nearly all of them run in one direction: given an image, describe it (MSCOCO, Conceptual Captions), answer a question about it (VQA), or reason about it with a rationale (VCR). Even the image-grounded dialogue work closest to PhotoChat — IGA (4k dialogues, image visible from the start, "can only be used for evaluation" due to its small scale) and Image-Chat (202k dialogues) — was built by asking crowd workers to talk about an image both of them could already see. The authors argue this setup can't train or evaluate a system whose job is to decide when and which photo to suggest during an ordinary conversation, because in real messaging only one participant starts out holding the photo.

The paper frames photo-sharing as the reverse of captioning-style tasks: selecting an image from the understanding of text, rather than generating text from an image. It argues this reversal brings two distinct challenges. First, the dialogue "doesn't often explicitly mention the main visible content in the image" — the paper's own example (its Figure 1) has the sharer describing the event location ("court") and the pictured relative's occupation ("attorney") rather than the photo's actual main object, which the paper calls "lady." Second, the dialogue is "not guaranteed to be relevant to the image": real conversations open with greetings and unrelated chit-chat before drifting toward whatever the photo will turn out to depict, so a system has to first figure out which part of the dialogue is even about the photo. The paper states it is, "as far as we know, the first dataset that captures the photo sharing activities" — and separately, in contrasting itself with Image-Chat and IGA, calls itself "the first of its kind to the best of our knowledge."

// how it works

The dataset and the two tasks

Sourcing and filtering the photos. Images come from the Open Images Dataset V4 (OID), which the authors filter down using OID's own object-label annotations rather than sampling at random. Based on "our investigation of the image-grounded dialogues and daily experiences," they settle on four themes people plausibly share photos about — people, food, animals, and products (shopping) — and from OID's roughly 600 object labels hand-pick 89 that fall under those themes and are likely to appear in a casually-shared photo (labels like "girl," "bagel," and "camera" are kept; labels like "traffic light," "nail," and "reptile" are excluded). For the people category specifically, an extra geometric filter requires the annotated person object to be a true main subject: its center must not fall within 0.1 of the image width/height from the border, and its width or length must be at least 0.3× the image's — meant to exclude photos where a person only appears small or at the margin, rather than as the photo's main subject.

Collecting the conversations. For each selected image, two Amazon Mechanical Turk crowd workers are randomly paired and given an image description listing the image's object labels; when the image contains people, one of them is assigned a random name and relationship so the workers have a way to refer to them. Workers are told to "imagine talking with their friend." Only one side can see the photo at the start, and that worker is instructed to drive the conversation until it feels natural to share it — critically, the paper does not require strictly alternating turns, and the photo-holder "can't share the photo until the total number of the conversation turns reaches five," so every dialogue has at least five turns of context before any image information can appear. After the photo is shared, the pair may keep chatting until they choose to end and submit.

Verifying quality. A separate pool of "in-house professional crowd workers" — distinct from the Mechanical Turk pair who generated the conversation — then filters the collected dialogues. A dialogue is discarded if the connection between the image and the conversation is "in-evident before the photo sharing act," or if the text itself is "unnatural, contains inappropriate words, too many typos or broken English." Figure 2 shows the paper's own worked examples: two accepted dialogues where the pre-share turns plausibly foreshadow the photo (a Greece vacation story before a ruins photo; describing "preparing a pizza" before a pizza photo), against one rejected dialogue where the photo (a boy in a classroom) is never foreshadowed by anything said beforehand — the paper notes this kind of dialogue "can happen in a real conversation," but it's unusable for training a model to anticipate a share, so it's removed.

The result is 12,286 dialogues paired with 10,917 unique images (some training-set images recur across more than one dialogue), split into 10,286 train / 1,000 dev / 1,000 test dialogues while keeping roughly the same people/food/animal/product category mix across splits (Table 1 below). Counting every message as its own turn, dialogues average 12.7 turns and 6.3 tokens per turn; if consecutive same-speaker messages are merged into one turn (the more conventional way other dialogue datasets count), that becomes 9.5 turns averaging 8.5 tokens each. On average, people converse for 7 turns before the photo is shared.

Two tasks. Formally, each dialogue is a sequence of turns with one photo-sharing turn pk inserted at some position after turn th. Photo-sharing intent prediction asks, for every turn tj up to th, whether a photo will be shared immediately next — a binary classifier that should fire 1 only at j = h and 0 everywhere else, scored with F1, precision, and recall. Image retrieval asks a model to pick out the one correct photo pk from a candidate pool P given only the dialogue up to th; during training the pool is the in-batch images (global batch of 128, aggregated across TPU replicas), while at evaluation P is every image in the test set (1,000 candidates), scored with Recall@K for K = 1, 5, 10, plus their sum, "Sum(R@1,5,10)."

Baselines. For intent prediction, the authors fine-tune BERT-base, ALBERT-base, and both T5-base and T5-3B, concatenating all prior turns (joined by [SEP], with speaker id as BERT/ALBERT's segment id) and training with cross-entropy loss. For retrieval, the centerpiece is the authors' own dual encoder (DE): a pretrained ResNet encodes the resized (224×224) photo, a first BERT encodes the photo's object labels, and the two are concatenated into the image embedding; a second BERT separately encodes the dialogue context up to the share turn. Two fully-connected+normalization heads project both sides into a shared H=512 embedding space, and their dot product is the similarity score, trained with a bidirectional in-batch cross-entropy (softmax) loss (the paper also tries a bidirectional in-batch hinge-loss variant, but reports cross entropy worked better in preliminary experiments). DEimg and DElabel are ablations that keep only the pixel branch or only the label branch; DE* variants are first pretrained on MSCOCO before fine-tuning on PhotoChat. Alongside DE, the paper reports three external baselines under the same 1,000-candidate test protocol: BM25 (treating each image's object labels as its "document"), VSE++ (ResNet152 + GRU, hard-negative triplet loss), and SCAN (adds fine-grained cross-attention between image regions, via Faster R-CNN + ResNet-101 region features, and text tokens).

// what they found

The results

Intent prediction (Table 2, test set, all values in percent). T5-base is the model the paper reports as its headline: 58.1% F1, 58.2% precision, 57.9% recall — the exact numbers repeated in the abstract and introduction. BERT-base and ALBERT-base trail it (53.2 and 52.2 F1 respectively), and a separately-listed T5-3B row scores 58.9% F1 with a different precision/recall split (54.1% precision, 64.6% recall — higher recall, lower precision than T5-base). The task's negative turns outnumber positive (share) turns by roughly 7:1 in every split (68,795 negative vs. 10,286 positive turns in train, per Table 3), which the authors suggest is what drags precision down across all four models.

Image retrieval (Table 4, test set, all values in percent, out of 1,000 candidates). SCAN — the only baseline with full cross-attention between image regions and text tokens — comes out on top overall: 10.4% R@1, 27% R@5, 37.1% R@10 (Sum 74.5), which the paper calls "consistent with the prior work…, demonstrating the power of the bottom-up cross attention." Among models without cross-attention, the authors' own DE*(ResNet-152, Bert-tiny) trained with cross-entropy loss does best: 9.0% R@1, 26.4% R@5, 35.7% R@10 (Sum 71.1). That beats VSE++ (69.8 Sum) on R@5, R@10 and Sum, though VSE++'s own R@1 (10.2%) is still higher than this DE variant's (9.0%) — SCAN remains the only model to clear both VSE++ and every DE configuration on R@1.

What moved the dual-encoder's numbers. The paper's own ablations, all read off Table 4's Sum(R@1,5,10) column: swapping the image encoder from ResNet-50 to ResNet-152 (both label-free) raises Sum by 4.2 points (60.9→65.1); adding the object-label branch on top of ResNet-152 pixels adds another 1.3 points (65.1→66.4); pretraining that same configuration on MSCOCO before fine-tuning on PhotoChat adds 3.5 more (66.4→69.9); and, holding cross-entropy loss and ResNet-152 + MSCOCO-pretraining fixed, shrinking the label encoder from Bert-base to Bert-tiny adds a further 1.2 points (69.9→71.1) — the authors attribute this to labels being "a compact list of tokens," where a smaller encoder overfits less. The same Bert-tiny vs. Bert-base swap under hinge loss instead adds 2.4 points (61.0→63.4). Figure 5 (the paper's qualitative error analysis) shows what these misses look like in practice: for one query the model's top-ranked images are plausible but wrong — wine glasses and black tea instead of the ground-truth photo of a man holding a wine glass — and in another it fails to tell puffins from ducks and fails to infer the background from the keyword "atlantic," illustrating the paper's point that success here requires attending to specific event details, not just topical similarity.

// does it hold up?

The eval read

Both tasks are honestly framed as far from solved, and the paper is upfront about at least one baseline's practical limits: it notes SCAN's cross-attention "doesn't scale well to large-scale retrieval problems due to the high computational cost of cross attention" even as SCAN posts the best numbers in Table 4 — a real accuracy-vs-deployability tension the paper flags but doesn't try to resolve. Neither task result is accompanied by a variance or significance measure: each model in Tables 2 and 4 appears to reflect a single training run, so the smaller gaps in the tables (e.g., BM25's 6.6 R@1 vs. the dual-encoder variants clustered around 6.7–6.8) don't come with any indication of how much they'd move on a re-run.

The retrieval evaluation also has a train/test asymmetry worth flagging: training uses in-batch negatives from a global batch of 128 examples, while test-time Recall@K is computed against all 1,000 test images as candidates — an eight-fold jump in distractor-pool size between training and evaluation that the paper doesn't discuss the effect of. Separately, the paper notes "some images in the training set are used in multiple dialogues," which means a retrieval model can partly learn an image-specific prior untethered to any one dialogue's content; the paper doesn't report how many images repeat or whether dev/test images are held strictly disjoint from that reuse.

baselines

Reasonable and diverse for 2021 — BERT/ALBERT/T5 for classification, BM25/dual-encoder/VSE++/SCAN spanning statistical-to-cross-attention retrieval — but all pre-date CLIP-style contrastive vision-language pretraining, so the reported ceilings (10.4% R@1) reflect 2021-era representations, not what a modern joint image-text encoder would score on the same 1,000-candidate test set.

contamination

Not applicable in the usual pretraining-leakage sense — PhotoChat is a fresh, purpose-collected 2021 dataset with its own held-out test split — but the dual encoder's image branch leans on an ImageNet-pretrained ResNet and the retrieval task's candidate images are drawn from the public Open Images V4 corpus, so a system whose visual backbone was pretrained on Open Images itself could have prior exposure to the exact candidate photos (not their PhotoChat captions/dialogues), a caveat the paper doesn't discuss.

what's missed

No human performance ceiling or inter-annotator agreement number is reported for either task, so there's no baseline for how "hard" 58.1% F1 or 10.4% R@1 really is relative to what a person could do with the same dialogue context. The paper's own qualitative analysis (Figures 4 and 5 in the source paper) shows several "wrong" predictions on both tasks that look defensible on inspection — early false-positive share predictions the authors call plausible in real life, and top-ranked retrieval images that are topically right but factually wrong — which Recall@K and F1 can't distinguish from an unrelated miss.

would raise confidence

A reported rejection rate and agreement statistic from the verification step (how many generated dialogues were discarded and why, and how consistent the "in-house professional" reviewers were with each other) would make the claimed data quality checkable rather than asserted; a human baseline on both tasks would give the 58.1% F1 / 10.4% R@1 numbers a denominator to be read against.

// bottom line

PhotoChat's real contribution is the asymmetric collection protocol itself — one side holds the photo, the other doesn't, for at least five turns — which is what makes the dataset usable for photo-suggestion research at all, unlike the symmetric image-grounded chat corpora it positions itself against. The baselines it ships make clear both tasks are genuinely open (intent prediction tops out well under 60% F1; retrieval under 11% R@1), and the paper is candid about at least one accuracy/scalability tradeoff (SCAN) even as it reports SCAN's numbers as best-in-table.

// takeaways

What to remember

  1. PhotoChat is, per the paper, the first dataset built specifically around asymmetric photo-sharing behavior: 12,286 human-human dialogues, each paired with one of 10,917 Open Images V4 photos that only one side can see until at least five conversational turns have passed.
  2. The two tasks it defines sit at very different points on the difficulty scale: the paper's headline intent-prediction result (a binary "will they share next turn?" classifier) is 58.1% F1 with T5-base — Table 2 also lists a higher-scoring T5-3B row at 58.9% F1 — while dialogue-conditioned image retrieval out of 1,000 candidates tops out at just 10.4% recall@1 with SCAN, far from solved.
  3. Among models without cross-attention, the paper's own dual encoder — object labels plus ResNet-152 pixels, MSCOCO-pretrained, small (Bert-tiny) label encoder — is the strongest at 71.1 summed recall, beating VSE++ on Sum/R@5/R@10 though not on R@1; full cross-attention (SCAN) still wins outright despite the compute cost the paper itself says makes it impractical to scale.
