HEAD-QA: A Healthcare Dataset for Complex Reasoning

6,765 multiple-choice questions lifted straight from Spain's real healthcare-specialization licensing exams — medicine (MIR), nursing (EIR), pharmacology (FIR), psychology (PIR), biology (BIR), and chemistry (QIR) — released in the original Spanish plus a machine-translated English version, HEAD-QA-EN. The paper's own strongest baseline, a non-neural information-retrieval model, still falls short of the exam's actual pass mark in every one of the six subject areas.

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

By 2019, neural readers were closing in on human-level scores on the field's most popular reading- comprehension sets — SQuAD and bAbI among them — which several authors had already diagnosed as a sign that "surface-level knowledge suffices to answer" rather than genuine reasoning. The field's response was a wave of multi-choice datasets built to require more: ARC and its predecessor drew on grade-school science, RACE pulled from Chinese middle/high-school exams, and SWAG tested everyday commonsense. But as the authors put it, this kind of dataset "is scarce for complex domains like medicine" — the closest prior work was either textual-entailment challenges over medical text (Abacha and Dina, 2016) or document/snippet-level QA (BioASQ), and none of it demanded the kind of general reasoning over a genuinely expert domain that "takes humans years of training to answer correctly."

There is also a language gap layered on top of the domain gap: essentially all of this benchmark infrastructure — SQuAD-trained readers, ARC/RACE/SWAG themselves — is English-only, even though most of the state-of-the-art open-domain and multi-choice QA systems (DrQA, BiDAF-style readers, entailment rerankers) had only ever been evaluated in English. HEAD-QA is built to close both gaps at once: source real professional-licensing questions from a non-English healthcare system, and ship a parallel English translation so the same English-trained systems can be tested cross-lingually on the identical content.

// how it works

The method

Spain's Ministerio de Sanidad, Consumo y Bienestar Social runs annual examinations to fill specialization positions across the public healthcare system — applicants need a 4-to-6-year bachelor's degree, typically spend a year or more preparing, and are ranked against thousands of competitors for a limited number of openings (e.g. a cardiology slot at a specific hospital). HEAD-QA scrapes these exams, from 2013 onward, across six specialties: medicine (MIR), pharmacology (FIR), psychology (PIR), nursing (EIR), biology (BIR), and chemistry (QIR). Radiophysics exams are excluded (their equation-heavy PDFs are too hard to parse), and questions later invalidated by the exam board are removed. The 2013 and 2014 exams offered five answer options; every later year offers four.

The "Pool" column is the full corpus, used as one unsplit set in the paper's own unsupervised-setting experiments. The paper additionally defines an official Train / Dev / Test split — 2013+2014 exams for training, 2015 for development, and 2016 onward for test — deliberately not randomized: a random split would have discarded the fact that questions and per-area counts are already designed by healthcare experts to avoid bias, and it would have broken the ability to compare against official, aggregated per-exam-year human results. None of the systems in this paper are actually trained on this split (all are unsupervised or distantly-supervised, per the authors); the split exists so that future supervised systems have a fixed, standard test partition to report against.

Roughly 14% of the medicine questions reference an accompanying image (X-rays, scans, and similar) that supplies information the text alone doesn't — these are included in HEAD-QA's JSON records but are not exploited by any baseline this paper tests. Figure 1 shows one such example, image no. 21 of the MIR 2017 exam.

HEAD-QA-EN. Because the corpus is natively Spanish, the authors additionally release a full English translation produced with the Google Translate API. To check its quality, they sampled 60 random questions (with their answers) and had two fluent Spanish–English speakers score adequacy and one native English speaker score fluency, using the 5-point scales from Koehn and Monz (2006). Average adequacy came out to 4.35 and 4.71 out of 5 (i.e. "most of the meaning" preserved), and average fluency to 4 out of 5 ("good"). Disease names translated well; molecular formulae in chemistry questions were the weak point — partly a translation issue, partly a byproduct of extracting formulae from the original PDF exams in the first place.

Every retrieval-based method searches a downloaded Wikipedia dump (Spanish for HEAD-QA, English for HEAD-QA-EN) with headers/tables stripped out, following the DrQA recipe. Three control baselines anchor the low end: Random answer sampling; Blind-x, always picking option x (motivated by Poundstone's observation that exam-writers' correct answers cluster toward the middle options rather than being truly uniform); and Length, always picking the longest answer (the intuition being that a fully-correct option often needs more words than a plausible-but-wrong one). Above the controls sit the Spanish IR model — DrQA's Document Retriever, scoring "question + candidate answer" queries against the Wikipedia dump as TF-IDF-weighted bag-of-words-plus-bigram vectors and picking the answer whose query scores highest — and, run only on HEAD-QA-EN, its cross-lingual counterpart plus four neural systems retrofitted from span-extraction/entailment architectures: Multi-choice DrQA (an Attentive- Reader-style span extractor trained on SQuAD, whose extracted span is matched against each candidate answer by token overlap), Multi-choice BiDAF (Clark et al.'s BiDAF reader, trained on SQuAD then continued-trained on ARC), and Multi-choice DGEM / DecompAtt (Parikh's decomposable-attention model and Khot's SciTail entailment system, each scoring "question + option" as an entailment hypothesis against retrieved sentences). Whenever a system can select more than one answer, the paper's tie-break rule is to keep the longest one.

Two metrics are reported throughout: plain accuracy, and POINTS, the exams' own real scoring rule — +3 for a right answer, −1 for a wrong one. Because some exam years have five options and others four, the paper is explicit that accuracy and POINTS rankings need not agree: "a given healthcare area might have better accuracy than another one, but worse POINTS score."

// what they found

The results

Across the full, unsplit corpus (the "unsupervised setting"), the authors' Discussion states that "the evaluated models surpass all control methods" — true on the six-area average for every baseline except DecompAtt, which the tables show falling below the Length control: 27.5% vs. 28.1% accuracy (Table 5), 23.3 vs. 28.2 POINTS (Table 6), losing to Length in five of the six subject areas (all but BIR). The supervised split is worse still on this point: there BiDAF also lands under Length on both metrics (30.3% vs. 30.5%, Table 7; 48.0 vs. 50.2 POINTS, Table 8), and DGEM only edges past it (30.6% / 51.5). Either way, none of the baselines comes close to solving the task. The best model overall — by both accuracy and POINTS, in both the unsupervised and the test-split ("supervised") settings — is not a neural reader at all: it's the cross-lingual (English) IR baseline, run against HEAD-QA-EN.

That the English IR model outscores the Spanish one (34.6% vs. 32.9% accuracy; 87.6 vs. 71.7 POINTS) is, in the authors' reading, "another indicator that the translation is good enough to apply cross-lingual approaches" — while every neural reader, trained on English SQuAD/ARC/SciTail and applied to HEAD-QA-EN, scores below both IR baselines. On the officially-defined test split, the ranking holds: cross-lingual IR again leads at 37.2% accuracy / 111.8 POINTS, ahead of Spanish IR (35.2% / 93.2) and the BiDAF/DGEM readers (BiDAF 30.3% / 48.0 POINTS, DGEM 30.6% / 51.5 POINTS — POINTS swing from 14.5 to 104.0 depending on subject).

Medicine (MIR) is the hardest category almost everywhere in both tables — a pattern the authors tie directly to length: Medicine has both the single longest question in the corpus (308 tokens) and the highest average question length (55.29 tokens), and Nursing (EIR), the runner-up for average question length (29.03 tokens), is the second-hardest category. Biology and Pharmacology, with the shortest questions and answers, score the best.

The paper's sharpest illustration of scale, though, is against real exam-takers. Restricting to the 2016 exam, it compares its best model (cross-lingual IR) against the official pass mark and the average score of the 10 best human scorers, all on the POINTS scale:

// does it hold up?

The eval read

HEAD-QA is a genuinely hard, honestly sourced benchmark — real professional exams, a real scoring rule, and a headline result (the best model missing the pass mark everywhere) that needs no hedging. A few of its own design choices, though, are worth reading carefully rather than taking at face value.

The cross-lingual headline result has an unexamined confound. The paper reads English IR beating Spanish IR as evidence the translation is "good enough" for cross-lingual QA. But the two IR baselines don't just differ by translation quality — they retrieve from two different Wikipedia dumps. If English Wikipedia simply has denser or more detailed medical-topic coverage than Spanish Wikipedia (a very plausible and well-documented asymmetry), that alone could produce a higher score for the English pipeline regardless of how faithful the translation is. The 60-question adequacy/fluency check (§2, "English version") is a reasonable spot-check on translation quality, but it can't distinguish "the translation preserved meaning" from "the retrieval corpus on the English side is simply richer" — and the paper offers the retrieval-score gap itself as evidence for the former without ruling out the latter.

The "human performance" comparison is the ceiling and the floor, not the middle. Table 9 reports two numbers per subject: the pass mark (the minimum score to qualify) and the average of the 10 best-scoring humans on the 2016 exam. Both are useful anchors, but neither describes a typical applicant — thousands sit each exam and many fail it. The paper's claim that model performance is "well behind human performance" is true against these two anchors, but the reader has no way to tell whether the model would also lose to an average (non-elite) test-taker, since that number is never reported.

The baselines are architecturally narrow, and intentionally untrained on HEAD-QA. Every neural system tested (DrQA, BiDAF, DGEM, DecompAtt) is a span-extraction or entailment model pretrained on SQuAD, ARC, or SciTail and applied to HEAD-QA-EN zero-shot — reasonable given the paper explicitly frames all baselines, neural and non-neural alike, as unsupervised or distantly-supervised. But it also means the paper cannot separate "this architecture family is bad at graduate-level healthcare reasoning" from "zero-shot cross-domain, cross-lingual transfer from SQuAD/ARC/SciTail is what's failing here," even though the paper defines an official train/dev/test split for exactly this kind of follow-up work to resolve. (The paper's two non-neural TF-IDF IR baselines sidestep this confound entirely — and are also, per the results above, its two best systems.)

The length-based explanation for subject difficulty doesn't rule out the image confound. The paper attributes Medicine's (MIR) low scores to its longer questions and answers (Table 3) — a real and plausible factor. But roughly 14% of medicine questions also depend on an image (Figure 1 above), and no baseline in this paper ever reads that image. That means a meaningful slice of Medicine's questions are, for every tested system, missing information by construction — a second, unexamined reason MIR could score lowest that the paper's own "it's just length" story doesn't account for or rule out.

baselines

Era-appropriate for 2019 (DrQA/BiDAF/DGEM/entailment rerankers pretrained on SQuAD, ARC, and SciTail, applied zero-shot to the machine-translated English HEAD-QA-EN — no neural system is ever run on the Spanish original) but architecturally narrow, and none is actually trained on the official split the paper defines for that purpose.

contamination

Not a real risk for the 2019 baselines tested here, but the source exam PDFs live at a stable Spanish government URL — a later, web-pretrained model could plausibly have memorized them, a scenario the paper (writing pre-LLM-era) neither raises nor mitigates.

what's missed

A control run on the English side at all: BLIND1–4 and Length appear only in the Spanish block of Tables 5–8, while every neural system is English-only, so every control-vs-neural comparison in the paper — including the Discussion's summary that the models surpass all controls — silently crosses languages. A control isolating translation fidelity from Wikipedia-coverage differences between the two IR baselines; a non-elite (average test-taker) human score to complement the pass mark and top-10 average; and any accounting for the ~14% of medicine questions whose answer depends on an image no baseline reads.

would raise confidence

An IR ablation that swaps in the same Wikipedia language for both translation directions (or a corpus-matched control); a broader human-score distribution rather than only the ceiling and the pass mark; and at least one system fine-tuned on HEAD-QA's own train split.

// bottom line

HEAD-QA succeeds at what it set out to do: it is a real, professionally consequential, non-English benchmark that 2019-era QA systems clearly cannot solve, with a stark and well-supported headline number (missing the actual licensing pass mark in all six subjects). Its secondary claims deserve more scrutiny — the cross-lingual translation-quality story is confounded with Wikipedia coverage, the human baseline is two extremes rather than a distribution, and the paper's own explanation for why Medicine is hardest doesn't account for the image-dependent questions none of its systems can see.

// takeaways

What to remember

  1. Real professional exams, not adapted trivia. HEAD-QA's 6,765 questions (Biology, Nursing, Pharmacology, Medicine, Psychology, Chemistry — 2013 onward) are pulled directly from Spain's actual healthcare-specialization licensing exams, released in Spanish plus a Google-translated English version (HEAD-QA-EN), with an official, deliberately non-random train/dev/test split.
  2. The best 2019 model is not a neural reader — and it still misses the real pass mark. Cross-lingual information retrieval leads every baseline (34.6% average accuracy, unsupervised setting; 37.2% on the test split), outscoring four SQuAD/ARC/SciTail-pretrained neural readers. Yet on the 2016 exam, that same best model's POINTS score falls below the official pass mark in all six subject areas.
  3. Accuracy and the exam's real scoring rule (POINTS: +3 right, −1 wrong) can disagree in ranking — the paper flags this explicitly, since exam years vary between four and five answer options.
  4. The cross-lingual "translation is good enough" claim rests on an unexamined confound: the English IR baseline retrieves from a different Wikipedia than the Spanish one, so a coverage difference between the two encyclopedias — not just translation fidelity — could explain why English scores higher.
