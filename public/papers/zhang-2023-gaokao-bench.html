Evaluating the Performance of Large Language Models on GAOKAO Benchmark

GAOKAO-Bench scores LLMs on the actual Chinese college-entrance exam — 2,811 questions across 9 subjects and 2010–2022, objective items auto-graded and subjective items scored by two human teachers each — then converts everything to the exam's own 750-point-per-track scale.

Xiaotian Zhang, Chunyang Li, Yi Zong, Zhengyu Ying, Liang He, Xipeng Qiu, Tianxiang Sun, Peng Li, Shiqiao Meng, Yanjun Zheng, Jun Zhan, Zhangyue Yin, Xiannian Hu, Guofeng Quan — Fudan University & East China Normal University

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

By early 2023, benchmarks like MMLU, GLUE, SQuAD, and BIG-Bench had become standard for measuring general LLM capability, and Chinese-specific successors — C-Eval, AGIEval, CMMLU — had begun to fill the obvious gap of testing Chinese-language knowledge. The authors argue these all share a structural limit: they "only consist of objective questions and lack subjective questions that are more closely related to generative abilities." Multiple-choice accuracy is cheap to score but doesn't exercise the free-form writing, multi-step derivation, and open-ended reasoning that make LLMs interesting in the first place. They add a second, subtler complaint: because these benchmarks lack real-world test samples, "individuals often underestimate the complexity of these tasks and the abilities of the models" — synthetic or extracted question sets can drift from what a genuinely hard, high-stakes exam looks like.

The paper's fix is to stop building a new question format and instead borrow one that already exists at scale and already mixes both question types by design: the GAOKAO, China's national college-entrance examination. It is simultaneously a nationwide, high-stakes, expert-vetted test and a natural mixture of objective items (single-choice, multi-choice, cloze) and subjective items (open-ended essays, corrections, derivations) — precisely the combination the authors say prior LLM benchmarks were missing. Prior GAOKAO-derived benchmarks are described as narrower still: the paper characterizes them as mainly focused on English (Yuan and Liu, 2022), especially English reading comprehension (Zhang et al., 2022), rather than the whole-subject, whole-year exam GAOKAO-Bench covers.

// how it works

The method

GAOKAO-Bench collects every national GAOKAO exam paper from 2010 to 2022 across 9 subjects (Chinese, English, mathematics — scored separately as a sciences-track and a humanities-track paper — physics, chemistry, biology, politics, history, geography), converted from PDF into JSON by a combination of scripting and manual annotation, with mathematical formulas hand-converted to LaTeX. In total the dataset holds 2,811 questions: 1,781 objective (1,418 single-choice, 273 multi-question-choice, 64 multi-choice, 26 "five out of seven") and 1,030 subjective (786 open-ended, 218 cloze, 26 correction). Each subject's marks are weighted to match the GAOKAO's own historical proportions, so that a sciences-track paper and a humanities-track paper each total 750 points once every subject's objective and subjective marks are summed (Chinese 150, English 150, sciences-mathematics 150, humanities-mathematics 150, physics 110, chemistry 100, biology 90, politics 100, history 100, geography 100 — the sciences set and the humanities set each add to 750).

Models answer in a zero-shot setting, with prompts that specify the required output format as well as the task, because the authors treat "intrinsic knowledge level" and "ability to follow instructions" as equally important. Objective questions are graded automatically by regular-expression matching of the model's stated answer against the standard answer, following the grading approach OpenAI describes for GPT-4. Subjective questions are graded by two human teachers per question, with the average of the two taken as the final score — this is the expensive step, so it was run only on the five closed-source models (both GPT-4 checkpoints, GPT-3.5-turbo-0301, and both ERNIE-Bot checkpoints). Seven additional open-source models (LLaMA-7b, Vicuna-7b, Baichuan2-7b-Base/7b-Chat/13b-Chat, ChatGLM-6b, ChatGLM2-6b) were evaluated on objective questions only.

A subject i's objective and subjective scoring rates, Ri,obj and Ri,subj, are each converted into points by multiplying against that subject's mark totals and summed over the sciences set S or the humanities set H:

Stotal,S = Σi∈S (Ri,obj · Mi,obj + Ri,subj · Mi,subj) // and symmetrically for Stotal,H over the humanities subject set H — both totals cap at 750

Because human grading is expensive, the authors also test LLM-as-a-judge: teachers write detailed marking criteria that break each subjective answer into discrete scoring points, and GPT-4-turbo (GPT-4-1106-preview, temperature 0) grades against those criteria in a zero-shot prompt. They report Spearman ρ and Kendall's τ between the judge's question-level scores and the human teachers' scores, and separately ablate what happens without the marking criteria. A companion contamination check, GAOKAO-Bench-2023, adds the objective questions from the 2023 GAOKAO as a fresh supplement the authors plan to keep extending each year to mitigate dataset leakage, so scores on it can be compared against the 2010–2022 set.

// what they found

The results

Figure 1 gives the headline: converted total scores for the five closed-source models, split by track. GPT-4-0314 tops both tracks (485/750 humanities, 447/750 sciences), with GPT-4-0613 close behind (480/750, 434/750). ERNIE-Bot-0615 (397/750, 333/750) beats GPT-3.5-turbo-0301 (368/750, 300/750) on both tracks, and every model — without exception — scores higher on humanities than on sciences, which the authors attribute to sciences subjects demanding more logical reasoning and computation while humanities subjects reward breadth of knowledge.

Table 1's per-subject objective breakdown shows GPT-4 leading most columns, but not all of them: ERNIE-Bot-0615 posts the highest closed-source score in chemistry (66.1% vs. GPT-4-0314's 44.4%), politics (86.9% vs. GPT-4-0613's 72.5%), and history (79.1% vs. GPT-4-0314's 75.6%) — exactly the three subjects the text calls out. Among open-source models on objective questions only, scale and Chinese-alignment both help: Baichuan2-13b-Chat (43.9% overall) beats the smaller Baichuan2-7b-Chat (40.5%), and both beat the un-aligned Baichuan2-7b-Base (27.2%) by a wide margin, with LLaMA-7b (21.1%) and Vicuna-7b (21.0%) at the bottom of the field.

The LLM-as-judge experiment (Table 4) reports a consistently strong correlation between GPT-4-turbo's grades and the human teachers' averaged scores, with ERNIE-Bot-0615 the best-aligned model (ρ = 0.864, τ = 0.740) and ERNIE-Bot-turbo-0725 the weakest (ρ = 0.825, τ = 0.685). The judge's converted totals systematically overshoot the human ones on humanities (e.g. GPT-4-0314: 535 judged vs. 485 human, a gap the authors describe as "around 5% of the total score") while tracking sciences much more tightly (GPT-4-0314: 440 vs. 447, "less than 2%").

The marking-criteria ablation (Table 6) shows the criteria matter: without them, GPT-4-0613's correlation with human scores drops from ρ = 0.854/τ = 0.710 to ρ = 0.820/τ = 0.659, and GPT-3.5-turbo-0301 and ERNIE-Bot-turbo-0725 show the same pattern in both directions.

Figure 3 is offered as evidence that per-year difficulty is roughly stable over the decade, which underwrites the contamination check in Table 5: on GAOKAO-Bench-2023 (the freshly-released 2023 exam's objective questions), all six models checked score lower than on the 2010–2022 set — ChatGLM-6b drops 6.7 points (30.8%→24.1%), ChatGLM2-6b 5.8 points (42.7%→36.9%), both Baichuan2-chat variants 2.6 points each, GPT-4-0613 0.6 points (71.6%→71.0%), and GPT-4-0314 2.4 points (72.2%→69.8%) — which the authors read as ordinary "difficulty fluctuations."

// does it hold up?

The eval read

GAOKAO-Bench's core idea — grade LLMs on a real, high-stakes exam instead of a synthetic multiple-choice set — is a genuine improvement in ecological validity, and pairing cheap automatic grading of objective items with expensive-but-precise human grading of subjective items is the right trade-off given the paper's stated budget constraints. But three things in the paper's own numbers deserve more scrutiny than the text gives them.

The 12-model field is really two fields of unequal depth. Twelve models appear somewhere in the paper, but only the five closed-source ones (both GPT-4 checkpoints, GPT-3.5-turbo-0301, both ERNIE-Bot checkpoints) receive the human-graded subjective score that feeds the converted total in Figure 1 and Table 4 — the paper's actual headline metric. The seven open-source models (LLaMA-7b, Vicuna-7b, three Baichuan2 variants, two ChatGLM variants) are scored on objective questions alone (Table 1). Figure 1's claim that "LLMs have achieved competitive scores in the GAOKAO" is true only of that five-model subset; there is no converted total, and therefore no way to compare against the 750-point scale, for the open-source half of the field.

The leakage check can't distinguish a genuinely harder exam from mild contamination, and the paper never establishes that any model's training data predates it. All six models checked in Table 5 score lower on GAOKAO-Bench-2023 (the 2023 exam's objective questions, released as a fresh supplement intended to mitigate leakage) than on the 2010–2022 set, uniformly and in the same direction. The authors read this as "within the normal range of difficulty fluctuations," but a uniform one-directional drop is also exactly the signature a modest training-data leak on the older questions would produce — the check as designed can't tell the two apart, since it never asks whether the pre-2023 scores are inflated, only whether the 2023 scores are lower. The paper's own text never claims the 2023 questions postdate any tested model's training cutoff, and it's plausible some don't: ChatGLM2-6b and both Baichuan2-chat variants are 2023 releases whose training data could plausibly already include the June 2023 exam. The check is also incomplete on its own terms: Table 5 covers ChatGLM-6b, ChatGLM2-6b, both Baichuan2-chat variants, and both GPT-4 checkpoints, but not GPT-3.5-turbo-0301 or either ERNIE-Bot checkpoint — three of the five models in the paper's actual headline comparison never get a leakage check at all.

Table 8's own subtype counts don't reconcile with Table 3's aggregate, and the gap isolates to one subject. Table 3 states 786 open-ended questions; summing Table 8's per-subtype "open-ended question" rows gives 658 — a shortfall of exactly 128. Table 8's cloze subtotal (28+23+86+81=218) and correction subtotal (26) both reconcile exactly against Table 3, so the discrepancy sits entirely in the open-ended category. The likely explanation: Table 8 lists no History subjective row at all — it carries History MCQs (287, single choice) on the objective side, but nothing below the line for History — even though Table 7 credits History with 50 of its 100 total marks to a "Subjective Score." The paper never states this, so a reader has no way to learn how many History subjective questions exist or how each is individually weighted — the one subject whose subjective side Table 8 is silent on is also the one subject whose subjective questions are otherwise undocumented.

baselines

A reasonable 2023 snapshot (GPT-4 ×2, GPT-3.5-turbo, ERNIE-Bot ×2, seven open-source models), but the two tracks — human-graded and objective-only — cover different, non-overlapping subsets of it, so "the field" means something different in Figure 1 than in Table 1.

contamination

A real, well-motivated GAOKAO-Bench-2023 supplement, but it only tests 6 of 12 models (missing GPT-3.5-turbo-0301 and both ERNIE-Bot checkpoints), and a uniform negative delta is treated as evidence against leakage when it's equally consistent with a mild leakage effect on the older questions.

what's missed

No inter-rater figure between the two human teachers who score every subjective question — only judge-vs-averaged-human correlation is reported; no discussion of GPT-4-turbo (the judge) sharing a lab with 3 of the 5 models it grades; Table 8's open-ended subtotal undercounts Table 3's by exactly 128, tracing to a missing History row.

would raise confidence

Report human-human agreement directly; run both the subjective-scoring and the 2023-leakage check on every model in the objective-only set; reconcile or explain Table 8's shortfall; test the LLM-as-judge step with a judge from outside the OpenAI family.

// bottom line

GAOKAO-Bench's design is sound and its numbers, where checkable, are internally consistent — the ten subject totals sum to 750 on both tracks, the judge-vs-human correlations in Table 4 match the ablation numbers in Table 6 exactly, and the bold-marked column leaders in Tables 1–2 match the prose claims about who wins which subject. But the paper asks for more credit on generalization and contamination-resistance than its own tables fully support: the headline comparison and the leakage check each quietly cover a different, narrower slice of the twelve models than the abstract implies.

// takeaways

What to remember

  1. GAOKAO-Bench converts the actual Chinese college-entrance exam (2010–2022, 9 subjects, 2,811 questions — 1,781 objective, 1,030 subjective) into an LLM benchmark, auto-grading objective items by regex match and human-grading subjective ones (two teachers per question, averaged), then converting both into a 750-point-per-track score.
  2. Only the five closed-source models tested (GPT-4-0613/0314, GPT-3.5-turbo-0301, ERNIE-Bot-0615/turbo-0725) receive that full converted-total score; seven open-source models (LLaMA-7b, Vicuna-7b, Baichuan2 variants, ChatGLM variants) are scored on objective questions only. GPT-4-0314 leads both tracks (485/750 humanities, 447/750 sciences); ERNIE-Bot-0615 beats ChatGPT on both.
  3. A GPT-4-turbo judge given human-written marking criteria correlates with human scores at ρ up to 0.864 (ERNIE-Bot-0615) — but the paper's own leakage check (all six tested models score lower on a fresh 2023 exam) and its Table 8 question-count breakdown (658 counted open-ended questions against a stated 786) both leave loose threads the text doesn't resolve.
