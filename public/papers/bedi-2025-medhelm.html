MedHELM: Holistic Evaluation of Large Language Models for Medical Tasks

Stanford's HELM recipe applied to medicine: an 81-author, clinician-heavy consortium builds a clinician-validated taxonomy of 121 real clinical tasks, a 35-benchmark suite built to cover it end to end, and a clinician-checked LLM jury for grading the open-ended half — then ranks nine frontier models on all of it, not just licensing-exam questions.

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

LLMs are effectively saturated on medical licensing-exam questions — MedHELM's own introduction cites ~99% accuracy on MedQA-style benchmarks — which makes exam accuracy the wrong number to keep optimizing. The authors' prior systematic review (Bedi et al., JAMA 2025, one of this paper's own references) found that only about 5% of published healthcare LLM evaluations use real electronic health record (EHR) data, and roughly 64% test nothing beyond licensing exams and diagnostic tasks. That leaves most of what actually fills a clinician's day — writing a discharge summary, triaging a patient message, generating a prior-authorization letter, assigning billing codes — almost entirely unbenchmarked. Synthetic exam vignettes also don't carry the ambiguity, shorthand, and missing context that real clinical text does.

HealthBench, released the same month, narrowed part of this gap with 5,000 single-turn, direct-to-patient conversations graded against physician-authored rubrics. MedHELM's authors argue it still models the LLM as acting alone (no follow-up questions, no human oversight) rather than as an assistive copilot in an iterative workflow, and that it has no task taxonomy behind it — so there's no way to say which parts of clinical work it does and doesn't cover. MedHELM is built to answer exactly that second question: a taxonomy first, then a benchmark suite engineered to have complete coverage of it.

// how it works

The method

MedHELM follows HELM's recipe — define the task space, build a benchmark suite over it, define the metrics — but grounds all three steps in clinician input rather than dataset convenience.

1. A clinician-validated taxonomy

Starting from tasks identified in the authors' own JAMA systematic review, one clinician co-author reorganized them into 98 tasks across 21 subcategories and 5 top-level categories: Clinical Decision Support, Clinical Documentation, Patient Communication & Education, Medical Research Assistance, and Administration & Workflow. Twenty-nine practicing clinicians across 14 medical specialties then took a two-part, ~30-minute survey: sort each subcategory into its parent category (96.7% correct, which the authors read as evidence the structure mirrors how clinicians actually organize their work), and rate the taxonomy's comprehensiveness on a 1–5 scale (mean 4.21/5, with 107 free-text suggestions). That feedback expanded the taxonomy to its final form — 5 categories, 22 subcategories, 121 tasks.

2. A 35-benchmark suite with full coverage

Every one of the 22 subcategories gets at least one benchmark, assembled via a three-tier curation strategy: 17 existing benchmarks pulled from public or gated sources (e.g. HeadQA, Medbullets, ACI-Bench, PubMedQA), 5 reformulated from previously-unevaluated medical datasets by adding standardized prompts, contexts and metrics (e.g. MIMIC-RRS radiology-report summarization), and 13 new benchmarks — 12 of them EHR-based — built to fill the chronically under-benchmarked Administration & Workflow category (referral triage, billing-code assignment, scheduling), curated with Stanford Health Care. Thirteen of the 35 benchmarks are open-ended (free-text generation, e.g. clinical note or patient-message drafting); 22 are closed-ended (exact-match or classification). By design, access is uneven: 14 public, 7 gated (require approval), 14 private — the paper is explicit that 14 datasets are withheld specifically for privacy/regulatory compliance and to keep them out of future LLM training data.

3. Grading: exact-match for closed-ended, a validated LLM jury for open-ended

Closed-ended benchmarks are scored by exact-match accuracy or micro-F1 (e.g. ICD-10 code assignment). Open-ended benchmarks are scored by a three-model jury — GPT-4o, Claude 3.7 Sonnet, and Llama 3.3 70B, chosen for architectural diversity — each rating a response 1–5 on accuracy, completeness, and clarity (for NoteExtract, which has no gold-standard response, "completeness" is swapped for "structure"). A response's final jury score is the mean of all nine ratings (3 judges × 3 axes). To check whether this jury can be trusted as a clinician stand-in, the authors collected independent ratings from 20 clinicians on a 56-instance subset — 31 from ACI-Bench, 25 from MEDIQA-QA — and compared LLM-jury scores to clinician scores via intraclass correlation, ICC(3,k), after z-scoring each rater's composite score to remove individual scale bias.

The results

Nine frontier models were evaluated at temperature 0 under uniform prompting: DeepSeek R1, o3-mini (2025-01-31), Claude 3.7 Sonnet (20250219), Claude 3.5 Sonnet (20241022), GPT-4o (2024-05-13), GPT-4o mini (2024-07-18), Gemini 2.0 Flash, Gemini 1.5 Pro (001), and Llama 3.3 Instruct (70B). DeepSeek R1 wins the most often (66% win-rate) with a low win-rate SD (0.10); o3-mini trails narrowly on win-rate (64%) but posts the single highest macro-average (0.77), driven by strength in Clinical Decision Support. The two Claude Sonnet models land within a point of each other (63–64% win-rate) and tie exactly on macro-average (0.73). GPT-4o reaches 57%; Gemini 2.0 Flash and GPT-4o mini fall to 42% and 39%; open-source Llama 3.3 70B Instruct wins 30% of comparisons; Gemini 1.5 Pro is last at 24% — but with the lowest win-rate SD of any model (0.08), meaning it loses about as reliably as DeepSeek R1 wins.

Performance by category (the paper's Figure 4, not reproduced here) shows the same nine models scoring highest on the two free-text categories — Clinical Note Generation (0.74–0.85 across models) and Patient Communication & Education (0.76–0.89) — moderately on Medical Research Assistance (0.65–0.75) and Clinical Decision Support (0.61–0.76), and worst on Administration & Workflow (0.53–0.63), which is tied with Medical Research Assistance for the narrowest band of the five (both span 0.10). DeepSeek R1 tops Patient Communication & Education outright (0.89) and ties o3-mini for the best Clinical Note Generation score (0.85); o3-mini leads Clinical Decision Support (0.76) and Medical Research Assistance (0.75). Llama 3.3, despite a respectable 0.81 in Patient Communication & Education, posts the single lowest Administration & Workflow score of any model (0.53) — a pattern the paper attributes to administrative workflows using data types that are unlikely to appear in pretraining, rather than a general capability gap.

Is the LLM jury trustworthy?

Pooled across both validation benchmarks, the LLM jury reaches ICC=0.474 against clinician ratings — above the clinicians' own inter-rater agreement (ICC=0.426) and above both automated baselines, ROUGE-L (0.361) and BERTScore-F (0.441). That pooled result is the headline number in the abstract. But the two benchmarks disagree with each other and with the pooled figure:

On ACI-Bench the jury (0.305) trails both ROUGE-L (0.445) and clinician-clinician agreement (0.458) — the two things it's supposed to beat. On MEDIQA-QA the jury (0.625) beats ROUGE-L and the clinician baseline but is itself beaten by BERTScore-F (0.668). The jury only wins outright — beating all three comparators at once — in the pooled "Combined" row (n=56); the paper doesn't say how that row is computed from the two per-benchmark rows, whose own instance counts (ACI-Bench n=31, MEDIQA-QA n=25) are fairly small on their own.

Cost-performance

Using per-token pricing as of 2025-05-12 and an upper-bound token estimate (maximum output tokens — necessary because o3-mini's reasoning tokens aren't otherwise observable), Table 3 puts total per-model cost — benchmark runs plus jury grading, all 35 benchmarks — at $804.74–$1,806.17. Running the full 9-model leaderboard once is estimated at $11,739.07. The cheapest models, GPT-4o mini ($804.74) and Gemini 2.0 Flash ($814.77), are also among the weakest performers (39%, 42%) — but not the weakest: the two lowest win-rates belong to costlier, mid-priced models, Llama 3.3 (0.30 at $939.58) and Gemini 1.5 Pro (0.24 at $1,131.06), each winning less while costing more than either cheap model. DeepSeek R1 ($1,806.17) and o3-mini ($1,722.04) cost the most and win the most. Claude 3.5 Sonnet ($1,570.88) and Claude 3.7 Sonnet ($1,536.64) sit in between, winning 63–64% of comparisons at noticeably lower cost than the two reasoning models.

// does it hold up?

The eval read

MedHELM's strongest contribution is the discipline of the taxonomy-first approach: 121 tasks, validated for structure (96.7% clinician agreement) and comprehensiveness (4.21/5), then engineered into full benchmark coverage rather than backfilled from whatever datasets happened to be lying around. That part holds up well. Two other claims deserve closer reading before they're taken at face value.

The abstract's 40%-lower-cost claim doesn't reconcile with the paper's own cost table. The abstract states Claude 3.5 Sonnet "achieved comparable results at 40% lower estimated computational cost" than the reasoning models. But Table 3 puts Claude 3.5 Sonnet's total cost at $1,570.88 against DeepSeek R1's $1,806.17 — a 13.0% reduction, not 40% — and against o3-mini's $1,722.04, an 8.8% reduction. Nowhere else in the paper is a 40% figure computed or explained; the body text (Section 2.3.3) only says Claude offers "a good cost-performance balance... at reduced costs," without a specific percentage. The qualitative claim (Claude is cheaper for similar performance) is true; the specific number in the abstract isn't supported by the paper's own Table 3.

The "LLM-jury beats clinician-clinician agreement" claim is true only in the pooled figure, not on either individual validation benchmark. As shown above, the jury underperforms both ROUGE-L and the clinician-clinician baseline on ACI-Bench, and underperforms BERTScore-F on MEDIQA-QA. The paper's own limitations paragraph acknowledges the jury "was validated on only two benchmarks" and that expanding clinician annotation "would strengthen the clinician–LLM agreement estimates" — but it doesn't flag that even on those two benchmarks, the pooled headline number doesn't hold up per-benchmark. This matters because the jury grades 13 open-ended benchmarks in the leaderboard; its validity on the other 11 is simply untested, and the two it was tested on give mixed signal.

The jury judges three of the nine models it's also ranking. GPT-4o, Claude 3.7 Sonnet, and Llama 3.3 70B are simultaneously jury members (Section 4.3.3) and three of the nine models being scored in Table 1. Self- or family-preference bias in LLM-as-judge setups is well-documented in the literature the paper itself cites (Verga et al.'s "Replacing Judges with Juries"), but MedHELM doesn't report any check for whether its jury systematically favors outputs from its own member models. Section 4.3.3 says the jury "was chosen to represent diverse model architectures and training approaches, minimizing systematic bias from any single provider" — that's a design rationale, not a measured self-preference check, and the paper never reports whether GPT-4o, Claude 3.7 Sonnet, or Llama 3.3 actually score their own outputs any differently than the other eight models' outputs.

baselines

Nine current frontier models across reasoning/non-reasoning and closed/open weights is a reasonable spread, but the jury's only baselines (ROUGE-L, BERTScore-F, clinician-clinician agreement) are checked on 2 of 13 open-ended benchmarks, and the jury doesn't clearly beat them on either one individually.

contamination

No decontamination check or contamination analysis is reported for the 14 public benchmarks. The paper's only contamination-facing measure is prospective, not diagnostic: 14 of the 35 datasets are withheld from public release specifically to keep them out of future LLM training data (p.3, fn.1) — but that covers none of the 14 public benchmarks already in the wild, several of which (HeadQA, Medbullets, PubMedQA) are established datasets plausibly present in frontier pretraining corpora.

what's missed

Whether the jury favors its own member models' outputs; whether an equal-weighted macro-average across 35 wildly different-sized, different-difficulty benchmarks is the right way to summarize "holistic" performance; run-to-run variance at temperature 0 isn't reported (the paper does report cross-model minimum-detectable-effect analysis, Appendix E, MDE range 0.007–0.092, which is a real strength on the significance side).

would raise confidence

Clinician validation extended to more than 2 of the 13 open-ended benchmarks (the paper says this itself); a jury-composition ablation that excludes judges from also being ranked; an explicit contamination check on the 14 public datasets; a corrected or removed 40% cost figure.

// bottom line

The taxonomy and the 35-benchmark suite are a genuine, well-documented step past exam-only medical LLM evaluation — that infrastructure is the paper's lasting contribution. The second headline claim, that the LLM jury is a validated stand-in for clinician judgment, is weaker than the abstract implies: it's tested on 2 of 13 open-ended benchmarks, and on both of those the jury's ICC doesn't beat every comparator individually — only the pooled average does. Treat the leaderboard's open-ended scores (11 of 13 benchmarks are unvalidated against clinicians) with that caveat, and read the abstract's 40%-cost figure as an error rather than a number to cite.

// takeaways

What to remember

  1. MedHELM builds a clinician-validated taxonomy (5 categories, 22 subcategories, 121 tasks, 29 clinicians, 96.7% category-assignment agreement) and a 35-benchmark suite engineered for complete coverage of it — 17 existing, 5 reformulated, 13 new (12 EHR-based); 14 public, 7 gated, 14 private.
  2. Reasoning models lead on aggregate (DeepSeek R1: 66% win-rate; o3-mini: 64%, highest macro-average at 0.77), with Claude 3.5 Sonnet close behind (63%) at meaningfully — but not the abstract's stated 40% — lower cost (about 13% below DeepSeek R1 by the paper's own Table 3).
  3. All models score highest on Clinical Note Generation and Patient Communication & Education, worst on Administration & Workflow (0.53–0.63) — the category the authors specifically built new EHR-based benchmarks to cover, and where every model still struggles most.
  4. The LLM-jury grading method (ICC=0.474 pooled, vs. 0.426 clinician-clinician agreement) is validated on only 2 of the suite's 13 open-ended benchmarks, and even there doesn't beat every baseline on either benchmark individually — a caveat worth carrying into any use of the leaderboard's open-ended scores.
