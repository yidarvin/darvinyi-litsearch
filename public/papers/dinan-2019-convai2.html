The Second Conversational Intelligence Challenge (ConvAI2)

A NeurIPS 2018 competition report: 23 teams' open-domain chatbots, built on Persona-Chat, ranked by perplexity/F1/Hits@1 on a hidden test set and then re-ranked by Mechanical Turk humans — whose own numbers show the two leaderboards disagreeing, and a fixed nonsense reply beating every competitor's F1 score.

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

By 2018, "progress in the field is significantly hampered by the absence of established benchmark tasks for non-goal-oriented dialogue systems (chatbots) and solid evaluation criteria for automatic assessment of dialogue quality," as the authors put it. Open-domain chit-chat had no shared arena: task-oriented dialogue had DSTC's five iterations of state-tracking challenges, and single-turn QA had quiz-bowl-style contests, but chatbots that just try to have an engaging conversation had nothing comparable. The organizers had already tried once — the first ConvAI competition, run at NeurIPS 2017, asked bots to discuss specific Wikipedia snippets with volunteers, and it "was not engaging to all human participants"; no training data was provided up front, participants had to collect it themselves as the competition went, and the best bot's live-evaluated dialogue quality (2.746) landed well under the human ceiling (3.8).

ConvAI2 is the second attempt, and it changes three things at once: it swaps encyclopedic Wikipedia chat for grounded, persona-based chit-chat about people's interests (building on the Persona-Chat dataset); it ships a full training/validation set from day one instead of collecting data live; and it evaluates competitors three ways — automatic metrics, Amazon Mechanical Turk, and "wild" volunteer chat — rather than one. The paper is a competition report, not a method paper: its contribution is the benchmark infrastructure (a hidden test set, a paraphrased "revised" test set to blunt lexical-overlap gaming, and open evaluation code) plus the outcome data from actually running 23 teams through it, which is what makes its account of where the automatic and human evaluations agreed and disagreed worth reading closely.

// how it works

The competition design

The task itself is inherited from Persona-Chat (Zhang et al., 2018): two crowdworkers are each handed a short persona — five-plus profile sentences like "I like to ski" or "I have four children" — and told to chat naturally and get to know each other, without knowing their partner's persona. ConvAI2 built a full benchmark around that task. The public training data comprises 131,438 examples across 17,878 dialogues drawn from 1,155 personas; a validation split holds out 100 never-seen personas (7,801 examples, 1,000 dialogues); and, crucially, the organizers crowdsourced an entirely new hidden test set — 100 further personas never shown to competitors, yielding 6,634 examples across 1,015 dialogues — so the automatic leaderboard couldn't be gamed by memorizing the public test split. They additionally crowdsourced paraphrased ("revised") versions of the same personas — e.g. "I just got my nails done" rewritten as "I love to pamper myself on a regular basis" — specifically to check whether models were succeeding by exploiting trivial word overlap between a persona sentence and the model's own utterance rather than genuinely using the persona.

Submissions were scored automatically on a held-out test set with three metrics, each usable only for certain model types:

F1 = 2 · precision · recallprecision + recall // precision = fraction of predicted-response words in the gold response; recall = the reverse. Computable for any model (retrieval or generative).

Perplexity (mean log-probability of the gold response, lower is better) is computable only for probabilistic generative models. Hits@1/20 (accuracy of picking the gold next utterance out of 1 correct + 19 random distractors, higher is better) requires a model that can score candidate utterances. Teams were ranked by the best (minimum-numbered) rank they achieved across whichever of the three metrics applied to their model. The top 7 teams by this rule advanced to a Mechanical Turk round: for each evaluation a fresh Turker was paired with the model, given a persona, told to chat 4–6 turns, then asked "How much did you enjoy talking to this user?" on a 1–4 scale and asked to pick the model's real persona out of two options (persona detection). A hundred such conversations were crowdsourced per model, and a Bayesian calibration method (from prior work on reducing annotator bias) was applied before the final ranking — though the paper reports this "did not affect the ordering of the scores." A third channel, "wild" live chat with unpaid volunteers over Facebook Messenger and Telegram, was run in parallel, but the authors ultimately discarded it from the competition ranking entirely: too many wild conversations were "senseless, offensive, or simply not in line with the instructions," with no systematic way to filter them out.

// what they found

Two leaderboards, two winners

23 teams submitted to the automatic-metrics round. Hugging Face won outright on every metric it reported — perplexity 16.28, Hits@1 80.7/20, F1 19.5 — beating not just the other entrants but the paper's own three baselines (a KV Profile Memory retrieval model at Hits@1 55.2, an LSTM Seq2Seq+Attention generative model at perplexity 29.8/F1 16.18, and a plain LSTM language model at perplexity 46.0). The top 7 by minimum-rank — Hugging Face, ADAPT Centre, Happy Minions, High Five, Mohd Shadab Alam, Lost in Conversation, Little Baby — advanced to the human round, though High Five opted out, leaving six finalists plus the KV Profile Memory baseline.

But the automatic leaderboard's winner is not the paper's overall winner. In the Mechanical Turk round, Lost in Conversation took the grand prize with an engagingness score of 3.11/4 (human-human pairs scored 3.48), while Hugging Face — the automatic-metrics sweep winner — placed second at 2.68. On persona detection, though, Lost in Conversation placed only fourth of six (0.90): Hugging Face topped that metric too (0.98), with Mohd Shadab Alam and ADAPT Centre tied above Lost in Conversation at 0.93 apiece — persona detection and engagingness don't track each other at all, since ADAPT Centre finishes dead last on engagingness (1.60) while tying for second on persona detection (0.93). Little Baby and the KV Profile Memory baseline tied exactly on engagingness, both at 2.44, and it's not an isolated case: on the metric that decided the competition, only Lost in Conversation (3.11) and Hugging Face (2.68) beat the baseline outright, while Mohd Shadab Alam (2.33), Happy Minions (1.92), and ADAPT Centre (1.60) all scored below it. The authors describe checking whether the Hugging Face/Lost-in-Conversation reversal was Turker noise: two of the authors themselves blind-rescored a sample of the same conversation logs, and "each annotator agreed with the Turkers regarding which model was better" (Turkers 2.80 vs. 3.29, Annotator 1 2.47 vs. 2.78, Annotator 2 2.00 vs. 2.71) — so the reversal held up under a second, blind — though in-house — read.

The F1 metric fares worse under the paper's own stress test. During the competition, the organizers discovered that always replying with the fixed nonsense string "i am you to do and your is like" — assembled just by picking frequent training-set words — scores F1 = 19.6 on the test set and 20.5 on validation, which is higher than Hugging Face's own winning F1 of 19.5 (test) and 19.1 (validation). A meaningless, context-free reply outscored the best F1 of every real competitor in the competition.

A parallel finding concerns dialogue behavior the automatic metrics never measured. Reading the blind annotators' comments, the authors noticed a recurring complaint that Hugging Face's model "asked too many questions" — so they counted wh-question openers and question marks across the 100 Mechanical Turk logs per system. Hugging Face was the clear outlier: it opened a response with "who," "what," "when," "where," "why," or "how" 107 times versus humans' 12 times in the same number of conversations. The authors' "tentative conclusion" is that over-asking questions — a purely conversation-level, multi-turn phenomenon — measurably hurt Hugging Face's human engagingness score even though it had won every single-utterance automatic metric.

// does it hold up?

The eval read

As a benchmark-design exercise, ConvAI2 does several things a 2019 chit-chat paper often didn't: it uses a genuinely unseen hidden test set rather than the original released Persona-Chat test split, and it adds a paraphrased "revised" persona set specifically to probe whether models were winning on lexical overlap rather than persona use — a deliberate anti-shortcut control, and Figure 1's own data shows it worked (it caught the KV Profile Memory baseline relying on word overlap while the top neural systems mostly didn't). But the paper's most valuable contribution to a benchmarks reader isn't the leaderboard itself — it's the honest, self-administered stress-testing of that leaderboard, and what it exposes is substantial.

First, the automatic winner and the human-judged winner are different systems, and the gap isn't small: Hugging Face swept perplexity, Hits@1, and F1, yet placed second in the round that actually decided the competition, with a Mechanical Turk engagingness score (2.68) meaningfully below Lost in Conversation's (3.11). Second, F1 — one of only three automatic metrics used, and the only one computable for every model — is directly falsified within the paper's own text: a fixed, meaningless nine-word reply beats every real competitor's F1 score. Third, on the metric used to crown a winner, only two of the six human-evaluated finalists actually beat the retrieval baseline outright: Little Baby ties it exactly (2.44), and Mohd Shadab Alam, Happy Minions, and ADAPT Centre all score below it — which, given only 100 rated conversations per model, is a reminder that these are noisy point estimates without reported confidence intervals, not settled rankings. Fourth, the "wild" volunteer channel — arguably the evaluation closest to real deployment conditions — was dropped from the competition outcome altogether once it turned out to be dominated by spam and off-instruction chat, so the grand prize ultimately rests entirely on a curated, paid, single-session Mechanical Turk setup that mirrors the training-data collection process rather than open-ended use.

baselines

The three provided baselines (KV Profile Memory retrieval; LSTM Seq2Seq+Attention and plain LSTM language-model generative baselines, from the original Persona-Chat paper) are fair starting points but are 2018-era, pre-Transformer architectures — the paper itself notes the competition "was run before the BERT model... was released," so every winning entry had an architectural generation's head start on the baselines by design, which is expected for a competition report but worth flagging for any later comparison against these numbers.

contamination

Not classical train/test leakage — the hidden test set was freshly crowdsourced and the revised paraphrase set specifically defends against shortcut overlap. The real comparability gap is training-data heterogeneity: competitors were free to add any public dataset, and per the paper's own model summaries, Lost in Conversation trained on Persona-Chat plus DailyDialog plus Reddit comments while Hugging Face pretrained on the Billion Words corpus plus CoNLL 2012 — meaning the two headline systems aren't isolating "which architecture is better" so much as "which architecture-plus-data recipe is better," and the paper doesn't attempt to disentangle the two.

what's missed

All three automatic metrics are single-utterance measures; none of perplexity, Hits@1, or F1 can see the multi-turn phenomenon (over-questioning) that the authors themselves identify as depressing Hugging Face's human score. The authors say as much in their own conclusion: future systems "must go beyond single word metrics like perplexity" to measure repetition, consistency, and dialogue-act balance across a conversation — this paper documents the gap but doesn't close it.

would raise confidence

Reported variance/confidence intervals on the 100-conversation-per-model human-eval scores (Figure 4's error bars are visibly wide pre-calibration); a training-data-controlled bake-off isolating architecture from pretraining corpus; and — as the authors themselves propose — an automatic proxy for question/answer balance or multi-turn consistency (they point to dialogue NLI as one candidate direction) so future competitions don't have to fall back on ad hoc post-hoc counting of question marks to explain a leaderboard reversal.

// bottom line

ConvAI2's real value isn't "Hugging Face" or "Lost in Conversation" as leaderboard entries — it's that the organizers ran an automatic-metrics round over all 23 systems, then narrowed to the six finalists that cleared that cut for two further human protocols (Mechanical Turk and "wild" volunteer chat), and then reported, in their own words, where the results disagreed: a swept automatic-metrics winner that lost the human round, an F1 score beaten by a nonsense string, and a question-asking behavior that no automatic metric caught but clearly cost points with human raters. For a benchmarks survey, this paper is less a chatbot leaderboard than an early, unusually candid case study in why single-turn automatic dialogue metrics and human judgment diverge.

// what to remember

What to remember

  1. ConvAI2 built a proper benchmark on top of Persona-Chat — a competitor-unseen hidden test set (100 personas, 1,015 dialogues) plus a paraphrased "revised" persona set specifically designed to catch models winning on lexical overlap rather than persona use.
  2. The automatic-metrics winner (Hugging Face: best perplexity 16.28, best Hits@1 80.7, best F1 19.5) was not the competition's overall winner. Lost in Conversation won the Mechanical Turk grand prize (engagingness 3.11 vs. Hugging Face's 2.68), and blind re-annotation confirmed the reversal wasn't rater bias.
  3. The paper falsifies its own F1 metric in passing: a fixed nonsense reply ("i am you to do and your is like") scores F1 19.6/20.5 (test/val) — higher than the best real competitor's 19.5/19.1 — and separately shows (via a post-hoc question-counting analysis) that none of its three automatic metrics could see the multi-turn, over-questioning behavior that hurt Hugging Face's human score.
