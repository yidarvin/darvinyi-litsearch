nuScenes: A multimodal dataset for autonomous driving

The first dataset to carry the full autonomous-vehicle sensor suite -- 6 cameras, 5 radars, 1 lidar, all 360° -- across 1,000 twenty-second scenes: 1.4M 3D boxes over 23 classes, plus two new composite metrics (NDS for detection, sAMOTA for tracking) built to score box quality directly instead of thresholding IOU.

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

By 2019, image-based benchmarks -- CamVid, Cityscapes, Mapillary Vistas, BDD100K, ApolloScape -- had driven most of the progress in detection, tracking and segmentation for driving scenes. But "most autonomous vehicles carry a combination of cameras and range sensors such as lidar and radar," and none of the existing multimodal datasets carried the full rig. KITTI, "the pioneering multimodal dataset," provided dense lidar and front-facing stereo images but only 200k 3D boxes over 22 scenes, with objects annotated only in the frontal view. H3D added full 360° annotation (1.1M boxes over 160 scenes) but still no radar. The paper is direct about the gap it's filling: "we are not aware of any autonomous driving datasets that provide radar data." Radar matters because it fails differently than cameras and lidar -- longer range (200-300m vs. lidar's 50-150m) and direct Doppler velocity, at the cost of sparser, less precise returns -- and the paper cites work suggesting multimodal sensor configurations "provide redundancy in the face of sabotage, failure, adverse conditions and blind spots," not just complementary accuracy.

Two more gaps sit underneath the sensor argument. First, generalization: existing AV datasets and vehicles were tied to particular operational design domains, and the paper argues there's a need to "study how detection methods generalize to different countries, lighting (daytime vs. nighttime), driving directions, road markings, vegetation, precipitation and previously unseen object types" -- conditions absent from KITTI (Karlsruhe only, no night, no rain per Table 1). Second, semantic maps: "with the notable exception of [Lyft L5, Argoverse]" most AV datasets shipped no map priors at all, despite maps being an established prior for scene understanding (expecting cars on roads, not sidewalks). Finally, the standard AP-with-IOU-matching metric itself was a poor fit: small objects like pedestrians and bicycles can be localized almost perfectly and still register 0 IOU, which the paper argues "makes it hard to compare the performance of vision-only methods" against lidar-based ones -- motivating a metric redesign as a second contribution, not just a bigger dataset.

// how it works

The method

nuScenes is built from 84 logs (15h of driving, 242km at an average of 16km/h) collected with two identical Renault Zoe electric cars in Boston (Seaport, South Boston) and Singapore (One North, Holland Village, Queenstown) -- two cities chosen for dense traffic, right- vs. left-hand driving, and varied vegetation/building/road-marking styles. From that raw data the authors manually selected 1,000 "interesting" 20-second scenes -- intersections, construction sites, rare classes like ambulances and animals, jaywalkers, lane changes -- each given an expert-written textual caption (e.g. "Wait at intersection, peds on sidewalk, bicycle crossing, jaywalker, turn right, parked cars, rain"). The full sensor rig sits on each car: 6 cameras (RGB, 12Hz, 1600×900), 1 spinning lidar (32 beams, 20Hz, ≤70m range, ±2cm accuracy), 5 radars (77GHz FMCW, 13Hz, ≤250m range), and GPS/IMU/AHRS. Camera exposure is triggered off the lidar sweep crossing each camera's field of view for cross-modality alignment, and ego-vehicle localization -- built from a Monte Carlo scheme over lidar and odometry against an offline HD map -- reaches "≤10cm" error, deliberately avoiding GPS, which the paper notes is unreliable in dense urban canyons.

Within each scene, keyframes (image + lidar + radar) are sampled at 2Hz and every one of 23 object classes is annotated with a semantic category, attributes (visibility, activity, pose), and a 3D cuboid parameterized as x, y, z, width, length, height and yaw. Objects are tracked continuously between keyframes as long as at least one lidar or radar point covers them. Alongside the raw data, nuScenes ships a vector-format semantic map with 11 layers (road dividers, lanes, pedestrian crossings, stop lines, traffic lights, etc.) covering the operating areas, plus baseline routes an idealized AV would follow.

The paper's second contribution is a pair of custom metric families. For detection, matching is thresholded by 2D center distance on the ground plane rather than IOU -- decoupling detection from object size and orientation -- averaged over four thresholds D = {0.5, 1, 2, 4} meters to get mAP. Alongside mAP, five "True Positive" (TP) metrics are computed at a fixed 2m match: Average Translation Error (ATE, meters), Average Scale Error (ASE, 1 − 3D IOU after alignment), Average Orientation Error (AOE, radians), Average Velocity Error (AVE, m/s) and Average Attribute Error (AAE, 1 − accuracy). These five collapse into the nuScenes Detection Score:

NDS = 110 [ 5·mAP + ΣmTP∈TP (1 − min(1, mTP)) ] // half the score is detection (mAP); the other half is the five mean TP-metric complements, each capped at 1

For tracking, the paper adopts and extends Weng & Kitani's AMOTA/AMOTP (average MOTA/MOTP across all recall thresholds), introducing a recall-adjusted sMOTAr that guarantees values span [0,1] even when nuScenes' difficulty drives raw MOTA to zero, averaged over 40 recall points into sAMOTA -- "the main metric for the tracking task." Two further novel metrics, Track Initialization Duration (TID) and Longest Gap Duration (LGD), penalize slow-starting or intermittently-lost tracks specifically because "many short-term track fragmentations may be more acceptable than missing an object for several seconds" in an AV context. Detection operates on sensor data in [t−0.5, t]; tracking operates on [0, t] -- deliberately different temporal windows.

// what they found

The results

The release totals 1,000 scenes (5.5 hours), 1.4M camera images, 400k lidar sweeps, 1.3M radar sweeps, 40k annotated keyframes and 1.4M 3D boxes across 23 classes and 8 attributes (the detection benchmark scores only 10 of those 23 — “The 10 classes are a subset of all 23 classes annotated in nuScenes” — and tracking scores 7) -- "7x as many annotations and 100x as many images as the pioneering KITTI dataset" per the abstract (the 3D-box ratio checks out exactly: 1.4M / 200k = 7.0x; the image ratio is closer to 93x than a literal 100x). Table 1's own dataset comparison bolds nuScenes as the single best entry among range-data datasets for RGB images (1.4M), lidar pointclouds (400k), radar pointclouds (1.3M, the only nonzero entry in that column among all 16 compared datasets), map layers (11) and classes (23); it ties Waymo Open for most scenes (1k each); and Waymo Open alone is bolded ahead of nuScenes on total 3D boxes (12M‡ vs. nuScenes' 1.4M), a gap the paper attributes explicitly to "the higher annotation frequency (10Hz vs. 2Hz)" rather than more raw driving. Within the released data, locations split Boston 55% / SG-OneNorth 21.5% / SG-Queenstown 13.5% / SG-HollandVillage 10%, with 19.4% of data in rain and 11.6% at night, and a per-keyframe average of 7 pedestrians and 20 vehicles. Class frequency is heavily skewed -- a 1:10,000 ratio between the least- and most-common classes, "1:36 in KITTI" by the paper's own comparison.

Three baselines are trained by the authors: a lidar-only PointPillars detector (accumulating multiple lidar sweeps and adding a velocity regression head), an image-only re-implementation of Orthographic Feature Transform (OFT) across all 6 cameras with NMS fusion, and an SSD+3D single-stage image baseline. Table 4 (test set) also reports the top external submissions to the 2019 nuScenes detection challenge: Megvii (lidar, class-balanced sparse 3D convolutions) posts NDS 63.3% / mAP 52.8%, best in six of the table's seven columns (MonoDIS edges it on mAAE, 0.13 vs. 0.14), while MonoDIS is the best image-only submission at NDS 38.4% / mAP 30.4%, "significantly outperforming" the paper's own OFT baseline (NDS 21.2% / mAP 12.6%) and even some lidar methods. PointPillars (NDS 45.3%, mAP 30.5%) and MonoDIS (NDS 38.4%, mAP 30.4%) reach nearly identical mAP, but PointPillars' NDS is 6.9 points higher -- the paper traces this to lower translation error (ATE 0.52m vs. 0.74m) and much lower velocity error (AVE 0.32 m/s vs. 1.55 m/s), calling the close mAP itself "notable" as evidence of recent progress in monocular 3D estimation. Per class (Table 7-SM), PointPillars leads on the two most common classes -- car (68.4% vs. 47.8% AP) and pedestrian (59.7% vs. 37.0% AP) -- while MonoDIS, helped by importance sampling during training, leads on the thin-structure/small classes: bicycle (24.5% vs. 1.1% AP) and traffic cone (48.7% vs. 30.8% AP).

Two ablations back the paper's design choices. Accumulating more lidar sweeps into PointPillars improves both detection and velocity estimation with diminishing returns: NDS rises from 31.8% (1 sweep) to 42.9% (5 sweeps) to 44.8% (10 sweeps, KITTI-pretrained) while mAVE drops from 1.21 to 0.34 to 0.30 m/s; pretraining source barely moves the needle at 10 sweeps (KITTI 44.8%, ImageNet 44.9%, no pretraining 44.2% NDS) -- "the final performance of the network only marginally varied between different pretrainings." Separately, training PointPillars, OFT and SSD+3D on varying fractions of the data shows method ranking itself changes with scale: PointPillars tracks SSD+3D at KITTI-comparable data volumes but pulls ahead as more data is added, which the paper reads as evidence that "the full potential of complex algorithms can only be verified with a bigger and more diverse training set." Switching the matching function from IOU to center distance (CD) also flips rankings on small classes: under IOU, pedestrians and bicycles "fail to achieve above 0 AP" for several methods, while CD matching "declares MonoDIS a clear winner" and reorders the lidar-vs-camera comparison on the bicycle class specifically. Weather and location subsets (Table 6, val set) show the vision-only baselines degrading far more at night (OFT −55%, MonoDIS −58% relative mAP) than the lidar baseline (PointPillars −36%), while Singapore data costs 1-8% relative mAP versus the full val set across all three methods.

For tracking, val-set numbers reported in the text put Megvii, PointPillars and MonoDIS at sAMOTA 17.9% / 3.5% / 4.5% and AMOTP 1.50m / 1.69m / 1.79m respectively -- a separate run from the test-set leaderboard numbers in Table 8, where the top external tracking submission (StanfordIPRL-TRI) reaches sAMOTA 55.0%, "significantly outperforming the strongest baseline (+40% sAMOTA)."

// does it hold up?

The eval read

The paper's own baselines are modest and honestly framed: PointPillars, OFT and SSD+3D are described plainly as the authors' training runs, not tuned to win. The headline numbers -- Megvii's 63.3% NDS, MonoDIS's 38.4% -- come from external submissions to the nuScenes detection-challenge leaderboard, and the paper is transparent that these are leaderboard entries rather than controlled ablations. But nothing in the paper discusses what protections, if any, existed against a leaderboard participant tuning repeatedly against the held-out test labels -- no submission-frequency cap, no description of a private vs. public test split, is mentioned anywhere in the text (checked: the words "leaderboard," "submission" and "test set" appear only in results framing, never in a methodology/safeguards discussion). That's a gap worth flagging for a paper whose most-quoted number (63.3% NDS) is precisely the one furthest from the authors' own controlled experiments.

Read Table 1's own bolding carefully before repeating the "most annotations" framing that citing papers often borrow from this one. nuScenes is the column-leader for RGB images, lidar pointclouds, radar pointclouds, map layers and classes -- but not for total 3D boxes or annotated frames read as an unqualified maximum: Waymo Open's 12M boxes are bolded ahead of nuScenes' 1.4M, and the paper says so itself, attributing the entire gap to annotation frequency (10Hz vs. nuScenes' 2Hz) rather than more collected driving. The "Ann. frames" column in Table 1 is in fact bolded for four different rows (nuScenes' 40k, Lyft L5's 46k, Waymo Open's 200k, and A*3D's 39k) rather than a single winner, which on its own undercuts any claim that nuScenes leads that column outright. None of this makes nuScenes a weaker dataset -- radar and 360° coverage really were unique at release, per the paper's own literature scan -- but the specific superlatives worth repeating are narrower than "biggest AV dataset."

The class-imbalance problem the paper surfaces (1:10,000 vs. KITTI's 1:36) is acknowledged, not solved: the text explicitly hands it off ("encourages the community to explore this long tail problem in more depth"), and the paper's own numbers show why -- PointPillars' bicycle AP is 1.1% even under the fairer center-distance matching, recovering only to 24.5% for the best baseline (MonoDIS, and only because it applied importance sampling during training). The center-distance-vs-IOU ablation (Figure 7) is genuinely useful methodologically, but it demonstrates that a metric fix changes which model wins, not that small-object detection is solved.

Two smaller points. First, the automated privacy pipeline (Faster R-CNN with a Cityscapes-trained ResNet-101 backbone for plates, MTCNN for faces) is tuned to "achieve an extremely high recall" with heuristic filtering to raise precision -- but no numeric precision or recall figures for the blurring step itself are reported anywhere in the paper, so readers can't independently judge how much identifiable information might remain in 1.4M images. Second, the dataset ships under CC BY-NC-SA 4.0, a genuine practical constraint (not a rigor issue) that shaped the ecosystem around it -- the paper notes Lyft L5 adopted the nuScenes database schema specifically to interoperate, rather than nuScenes data itself being redistributable for commercial benchmarking.

The weather and lighting analysis has two confounds, and the paper names both. Table 6 is the page's evidence that nuScenes tests generalization to conditions KITTI never covered, and rain is where it looks weakest — a small average change, better for one detector than worse. The paper's own explanation is that the label doesn't mean what a reader assumes: nuScenes “annotates any scene with raindrops on the windshield as rainy, regardless of whether there is ongoing rainfall.” That is a windshield-condition label, not a rainfall label, so a near-null rain result is close to uninformative about precipitation robustness — which is precisely the generalization the dataset's motivation invokes. The night result has the mirror problem in the other direction: the drops are large (36% for the lidar method, 55% and 58% for the vision ones), and the page reads that as a lighting effect, but the paper adds that “night scenes have very few objects and it is harder to annotate objects with bad visibility.” Some of that gap is annotation quality and class distribution, not perception under low light, and nothing in the table separates them.

One further caveat rides on the same table: its own caption states that the MonoDIS results “are not directly comparable to other sections of this work, as a ResNet34 backbone and a different training protocol are used.” The 58% figure — the largest degradation in the paper and the most quotable number in it — comes from that row.

baselines

Own baselines (PointPillars, OFT, SSD+3D) are modest and clearly labeled; the standout numbers (Megvii 63.3% NDS, MonoDIS 38.4%) are external leaderboard entries whose tuning process against the test set is not documented in the paper.

contamination

No train/test leakage issue found, but the "most annotations" superlative needs Table 1's own bolding pattern read carefully -- Waymo Open, not nuScenes, is bolded on total 3D boxes, and the "Ann. frames" column bolds four different datasets, not one.

what's missed

NDS's fixed 50/50 mAP-vs-TP-metric weighting can mask which failure mode (localization vs. velocity vs. attribute) actually drove a headline score change, as in the PointPillars/MonoDIS comparison the paper itself draws.

would raise confidence

Reported precision/recall for the automated face/plate-blurring pipeline (currently qualitative only), and a stated policy on test-leaderboard submission limits or a private test split.

// bottom line

nuScenes earned its 8,635 citations by being first with a genuinely complete AV sensor rig (360° cameras + radar + lidar) and by forcing the field to adopt center-distance-based, non-IOU detection and tracking metrics. But several of the dataset's most-repeated superlatives -- "most annotations," "100x the images" -- are softer once you read the paper's own hedges and Table 1's actual per-column bolding, and the paper is silent on how its leaderboard numbers were protected from test-set tuning.

// takeaways

What to remember

  1. nuScenes was the first AV dataset to combine a full 360° six-camera, five-radar, one-lidar rig with 3D box annotation -- 1,000 twenty-second scenes, 1.4M boxes across 23 classes, 7x KITTI's box count exactly, though the "100x the images" and "most annotations" framings are softer once you read Table 1's own per-column bolding and the paper's Waymo Open caveat.
  2. It introduced NDS and sAMOTA, composite detection/tracking scores built around center-distance matching (not IOU) specifically so small objects like pedestrians and bicycles stop scoring 0 AP by construction -- a genuine metric contribution the field adopted well beyond this one dataset.
  3. The paper's own ablations show scale and metric choice change conclusions, not just headline numbers: PointPillars only overtakes SSD+3D past KITTI-scale training volumes, and switching from IOU to center-distance matching flips which detector "wins" on bicycles -- while the underlying 1:10,000 class imbalance remains an acknowledged, unsolved problem even under the fairer metric.
