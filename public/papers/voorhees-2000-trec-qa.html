Building a Question Answering Test Collection

NIST's report on the TREC-8 Question Answering track — the first large-scale, domain-independent QA evaluation — turns 200 fact questions and three independent human assessors into the founding methodology for QA benchmarking: mean reciprocal rank scoring, a documented ~6% assessor disagreement rate, and the finding that comparative system rankings stay stable (Kendall's tau spanning .9146-.9976) even though the assessors themselves rarely agree on every string.

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

By 1999, TREC had spent seven years perfecting a recipe for evaluating document retrieval: a fixed document collection, a set of information-need queries, and human relevance judgments turned into a reusable test collection that any future system could be scored against without re-running the original participants. Question answering had none of this. QA systems were built one-off, evaluated by whatever the individual research group thought "correct" meant, on whatever documents or knowledge base they happened to have — Winograd's SHRDLU worked in a toy block world, Woods's LUNAR answered geologists' questions about moon rocks, MURAX pulled from an on-line encyclopedia for closed-class questions, and information-extraction systems like FASTUS and PLUM populated database-like templates from narrower domains. None of these efforts shared a document collection, a question set, or a notion of a correct answer, so no result was comparable to any other.

NIST's TREC-8 QA track set out to fix this by importing the document-retrieval evaluation recipe wholesale — a shared corpus, a shared question set, human assessors judging correctness — and treating the exercise as a genuine open research question rather than an assumption: does the relevance-judging methodology that works for ranked document lists actually transfer to a task whose unit of judgment is a short answer string instead of a whole document? Document retrieval's test collections work because a document either was or wasn't in the judged set — a returned document's identity is unambiguous. An answer string has no such identifier: two runs almost never return the literal same string, so it isn't obvious the same "did the judges see this" logic even applies. That's the paper's real subject: not just building one QA benchmark, but interrogating whether the benchmark-building methodology itself is sound for QA, and, if it works for ranking systems, whether it also yields something reusable for scoring systems nobody has judged yet.

// how it works

The method

The task given to the track's 20 participating teams (45 submitted runs) was deliberately narrow: retrieve answers, not documents, but only for closed-class questions — ones with a single definite answer typified by a noun phrase, in the tradition of the MURAX system, rather than open-ended or procedural questions. The document collection was the existing TREC-8 ad hoc collection: roughly 528,000 newswire and newspaper articles from the LA Times, the Financial Times, FBIS, and the Federal Register — an unconstrained domain, since the questions could be about anything the news covered, but a constrained answer type, since IE-style systems could reasonably expect the correct response to be an entity: a person, a date, a quantity, a place.

The 200 test questions (Figure 1 shows ten real examples — "How many calories are there in a Big Mac?", "Where is the Taj Mahal?", "Who was the 16th President of the United States?") were pooled from four sources — TREC participants, NIST staff, the NIST assessors themselves, and logs from the FAQ Finder system — then filtered by NIST staff to remove list-answer questions, obvious back-formations of a single document sentence, and anything ambiguous. Even after that filtering, once judging began the assessors determined that 2 of the 200 questions had no answer at all in the collection, so all official scores are computed over the remaining 198 questions. The final mix was 127 questions from participants/NIST staff, 49 from the assessors, and 24 from the FAQ Finder logs — a deliberate blend of technically-informed and naive-user question styles.

For each question, a system returned a ranked list of five [document-id, answer-string] pairs, with the answer string capped at either 50 or 250 bytes depending on the run type — the paper treats these as two separate tasks throughout, since a 250-byte budget (roughly a sentence or two) admits far more of the surrounding context than a 50-byte snippet does. A human assessor read each candidate string and made a binary call: would a user who trusted the system completely get the correct answer from this string alone, given the context of the document it came from? The score for a question was the reciprocal of the rank of the first correct string (0 if none of the five was correct), and a run's score was the mean of those reciprocal ranks — mean reciprocal rank, MRR:

MRR = 1|Q| Σq∈Q 1rank of first correct answer for q // rank contributes 0 if none of the top-5 answers for q was judged correct; higher MRR is better

The paper's central methodological move was to judge every question with three independent assessors rather than one, specifically so the effect of disagreement could be measured directly rather than assumed away. Assessors received roughly two hours of hands-on training on four practice questions and explicit written guidance (e.g., document context matters for time-sensitive answers like "who is the prime minister of Japan"; a list of same-category entities with no indication of which one is the answer counts as incorrect; units and punctuation must match, so "500" does not substitute for "$500"). Each of the 41 valid runs (four were excluded as erroneous submissions) was judged against the full pool of distinct answer-string/document pairs it and every other run returned for that question — an answer pool that averaged 191.6 pairs and 55.3 distinct documents per question. Where the three assessors' judgments disagreed for a given pair, an adjudicator resolved the case — not by voting, but by determining whether the disagreement reflected a genuine difference of opinion (in which case majority ruled) or a misapplication of the guidelines (in which case the adjudicator could overrule the majority). This adjudicated judgment set is what produced the official Table 1 scores.

// what they found

The results

The best 50-byte run (textract9908, Cymfony, Inc.) scored an MRR of .660 with 54 of 198 questions unanswered; the best 250-byte run (SMUNLP2, Southern Methodist University) scored .646 with 44 unanswered. The single highest score of all 41 runs was, notably, a 50-byte run — the paper's own framing of "most accurate systems found a correct response for more than 2/3 of the questions" refers to this system's roughly 73% found-rate (144/198). But length still mattered on average: for every organization that submitted runs at both byte limits, its 250-byte run scored a higher MRR than its matched 50-byte run — SMU rose from .555 to .646, both AT&T pairs and both IBM pairs rose similarly, and NTT Data, GE/UPenn, University of Massachusetts, and Sheffield all showed the same pattern. The paper reads this as evidence that 250 bytes (about a sentence) is roomy enough for ordinary best-matching-passage retrieval to work, while 50 bytes forces systems into the harder job of classifying the expected answer type (person, date, quantity, ...) and pulling out just the matching entity near the question's key terms — the general architecture most of the stronger systems converged on.

The paper's real finding, though, is about the judging process itself, not the leaderboard. On average 6% of judged answer strings had disagreement among the three assessors — small in raw percentage because most strings are obviously wrong and everyone agrees on those, but concentrated in the genuinely ambiguous cases. Measuring agreement the way the document-relevance literature does (overlap = |intersection of correct-string sets| / |union|), the mean overlap across all three judges, over the 193 questions with at least one correct string found, was .641 — moderate agreement, driven by real ambiguity in how much of a name is required ("Aung San Suu Kyi" vs. "Suu Kyi" vs. just "Kyi"), how granular a date or location answer needs to be (some assessors accepted "1789" for when the Bastille fell, everyone accepted "July 14, 1789", nobody accepted "USA" for where Harry Truman was born, but some accepted just "Missouri"), and even what counts as the "real" entity versus a replica (one of three assessors accepted "Atlantic City, NJ" — home of a Taj Mahal casino — for "Where is the Taj Mahal?").

Despite that disagreement, the ranking of systems proved stable. The paper re-scored all 41 runs against 100,000 randomly sampled single-assessor ("1-judge") qrels and computed Kendall's tau correlations between the resulting system rankings: the mean correlation among pairs of 1-judge rankings was .9632 (equivalent to about 15 pairwise adjacent swaps out of a possible 820 for 41 systems), and the mean correlation of each 1-judge ranking against the official adjudicated ranking was .9563. The majority-vote and union qrels correlated with the adjudicated ranking at .9683 and .9780 respectively; the strict intersection qrels (only count a string when all three assessors agree) correlated at .9146 — tying the single worst 1-judge-vs-adjudicated correlation observed across the 100,000-sample resampling, not falling outside it. Where multi-judge aggregation actually did diverge from single-judge sampling was in the runs' raw MRR scores, not these rank correlations: the paper reports that majority and adjudicated MRR scores always landed between the min and max of a run's 100,000 1-judge scores, while union and intersection MRR scores frequently fell outside that band. The conclusion: any two runs whose MRR scores differ by less than .014 — the largest per-run standard deviation observed across the 100,000 1-judge qrels — must be treated as equivalently accurate, but which system beats which barely changes with the judgment set used — comparative QA evaluation is about as stable as comparative document-retrieval evaluation already known to be.

Where the news is worse is reusability. Because the unit of judgment is an entire answer string rather than a document identifier, there is no cheap way to tell whether a brand-new run's unjudged strings would have been marked correct — the University of Ottawa's two runs, misnumbered and excluded from judging by mistake, illustrate the problem directly. The paper's own attempted fix — an answer key of 338 hand-derived Perl string-matching patterns across the 198 questions (1.71 patterns/question on average, ranging from a single pattern for 128 questions up to 13 patterns for "What does the Peugeot company manufacture?") — re-scored the 41 judged runs at a system-ranking Kendall's tau of .96 against the adjudicated ranking, comparable to the disagreement level between two human assessors. But tested honestly on the two never-judged Ottawa runs (Table 3), the pattern method and a post-hoc human judge nearly agreed at 50 bytes (MRR .292 vs. .291, tied at 117 not-found) but diverged at 250 bytes, where the patterns were more lenient (MRR .467 vs. .432; 78 vs. 87 not-found) — exactly the direction the authors predicted, since the patterns were deliberately built to over-accept rather than under-accept.

// does it hold up?

The eval read

Read in 2026, this paper is worth separating from every later QA/NLP benchmark it inspired, because its actual claims are narrower — and more careful — than the phrase "TREC QA track" tends to suggest in citation. It is not a claim that QA is solved, or even that this specific evaluation recipe scales; it is a claim about the stability of one particular 198-question, 41-run, pooled-answer evaluation, and the authors are explicit that the reusability problem is not solved by their own proposed fix.

baselines

There are no "baselines" in the modern sense — the 41 runs are the field's actual state of the art in 1999, from 20 different labs (Cymfony, SMU, AT&T, IBM, Xerox, MITRE, NTT Data, University of Massachusetts, and others), so the comparison is contemporaneous and fair by construction. The one place a comparison could mislead is between the 50-byte and 250-byte groups: they are different tasks (more context budget), and the paper is careful to compare same-organization pairs across the split rather than raw MRR across it — a discipline worth preserving when citing this paper's numbers today.

contamination

Not a concept the paper addresses or could have addressed — the term didn't exist in this sense in 2000, and the newswire corpus and hand-authored questions were purpose-built for a single competition with no possibility of prior model exposure. Its actual analog of a "contamination" concern is answer-key construction bias: the pattern-matching answer key was derived from the responses of the very runs it was then used to re-score (Table 1's 41 runs), which the authors flag themselves as "essentially testing on the training data" — and it's precisely why they go to the trouble of testing the patterns on the two never-judged Ottawa runs instead of stopping at the in-sample .96 correlation.

what's missed

Two things a 2026 reader should not import from this paper: first, MRR itself only rewards the position of the first correct answer among five slots and says nothing about the quality or confidence-calibration of the ranking beyond that — a system that guesses right on attempt 5 scores the same .2 whether attempts 1-4 were near-misses or nonsense. Second, and more consequential for how the paper is often cited, the .641 mean overlap and .9146-.9976 range of Kendall's tau are properties of this specific 198-question set, three specific NIST-trained assessors, and a pooling depth of 45 runs; the paper does not claim (and a careful reader shouldn't assume) that assessor agreement or ranking stability generalize in the same numeric range to a benchmark an order of magnitude larger, with crowd-sourced rather than NIST-trained judges, or with today's open-ended generative answers rather than 1999's closed-class entity answers.

would raise confidence

The paper is already unusually rigorous for its era in the checks it runs on itself — three independent assessors instead of one, an explicit adjudication protocol, a 100,000-sample resampling study of judgment variance, and an honest out-of-sample test of its own proposed reusability fix on the Ottawa runs rather than only reporting the flattering in-sample number. What would strengthen it further, by the standards the authors themselves apply elsewhere in the paper, is a breakdown of the .641 overlap and 6% disagreement rate by question source (participant-authored vs. assessor-authored vs. FAQ-Finder-authored) — the paper notes the three sources represent different levels of "naturalness" but never checks whether disagreement or scoring stability differs across them, which would have told a very direct story about whether professionally-curated benchmark questions behave differently from genuine user questions.

// bottom line

This is a methodology paper disguised as a benchmark report, and it should be read that way: the 41-run leaderboard is almost incidental to the finding that mattered for the next two decades of QA evaluation — that human assessors will disagree on individual answer strings (here, about 6% of the time, .641 overlap) but that this disagreement barely perturbs which systems come out ahead of which (Kendall's tau .9146-.9976), so QA can be evaluated the same way document retrieval already was. The one problem this paper explicitly does not solve — a reusable test collection that scores new, unjudged runs without re-running the whole assessment pipeline — is exactly the gap that pooled, string-matching, and eventually LLM-graded QA benchmarks spent the following 25 years trying to close.

// takeaways

What to remember

  1. The TREC-8 QA track (200 closed-class fact questions over 198 scorable, a 528K-document newswire/newspaper collection, 45 runs from 20 organizations) was the first large-scale, domain-independent evaluation of question-answering systems, scored by mean reciprocal rank over a ranked list of five [document-id, answer-string] pairs.
  2. Three independent assessors judged every answer string, deliberately so disagreement could be measured rather than assumed: about 6% of judgments were contested, and the mean three-judge overlap on correct-answer sets (over the 193 questions with at least one correct string found) was .641 — yet Kendall's tau between system rankings under different judgment sets spanned .9146-.9976, confirming that comparative QA evaluation survives assessor disagreement about as well as document-retrieval evaluation already did.
  3. Building a truly reusable QA test collection — one that scores a new, never-judged run without a full re-judging pass — remains unsolved in this paper: its own pattern-matching answer key (338 patterns over 198 questions) scored a system-ranking correlation of .96 in-sample, but on the two genuinely unjudged University of Ottawa runs it proved measurably more lenient than a human judge at the 250-byte length (MRR .467 vs. .432), the exact failure mode the authors predicted from the patterns' deliberately over-inclusive design.
