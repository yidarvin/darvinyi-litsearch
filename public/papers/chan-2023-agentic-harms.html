Harms from Increasingly Agentic Algorithmic Systems

A FAccT '23 position paper from 22 authors across the FATE (Fairness, Accountability, Transparency, Ethics) community argues that ML systems are becoming more agentic — less specified, more directly impactful, more goal-directed, more long-horizon — and that this trajectory predicts a distinct class of societal harm worth anticipating before deployment, not auditing after the fact.

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

The FATE (Fairness, Accountability, Transparency, and Ethics) community had, by early 2023, already documented algorithmic harm across health care, finance, policing, and recommendation, and had explicitly called for anticipating harms from new systems rather than only reacting to deployed ones — building on existing risk taxonomies from Raji et al. and Shelby et al. (2022) and Weidinger et al.'s taxonomy of language-model risks. What that literature had not isolated is a specific, cross-cutting property of emerging ML systems that predicts a distinctive new harm profile as the field moves past static classifiers. Chan et al. name that property agency: not "AI consciousness," not a binary "is this an agent," but a graded property — a system does something without being told exactly how, its actions land on the world without a human in the loop, it is built or trained toward a quantifiable objective, and its decisions are chained across time toward that objective. The paper's own worked contrast is a literature-review task: a search engine requires a human to type queries, click through results, and compile citations by hand; a system like WebGPT can be handed the topic and search, read, and synthesize with far less human intervention. As more research effort and commercial incentive pushes toward that second kind of system — reinforcement learning agents, tool-using language models, autonomous digital assistants — the paper argues the FATE community needs a vocabulary for what specifically gets riskier as agency increases, and why recognizing that risk does not let anyone off the hook for it.

This page follows the May 2023 revision (v2), which is what the arXiv link resolves to. v1 (20 Feb 2023) advances the same four-characteristic framing and the same anticipated harms, and no number differs between the two — but v2 is a genuine revision, not a reprint. It renames three section headings, expands the remedies section substantially, and adds material v1 lacks, including a passage naming ChatGPT's plug-ins and AutoGPT as the agent wave already underway, the line "we anticipate that current limitations and barriers will be surpassed or ignored in the pressure to deploy," and the call for "quantitative and qualitative metrics that build upon our characterization of agency" quoted later on this page. Quotations here are from v2 throughout.

// how it works

Four characteristics, not a checklist

The paper is explicit that it declines "a definition of agency as a binary property." Instead it identifies four characteristics that, particularly in combination, tend to increase a given algorithmic system's agency — a spectrum framing, not a threshold test a system passes or fails:

  • Underspecification — "the degree to which the algorithmic system can accomplish a goal provided by operators or designers, without a concrete specification of how the goal is to be accomplished."
  • Directness of impact — "the degree to which the algorithmic system's actions affect the world without mediation or intervention by a human, i.e. without a human in the loop."
  • Goal-directedness — "the degree to which the system acts as if it is designed/trained to achieve a particular quantifiable objective."
  • Long-term planning — "the degree to which the algorithmic system is designed/trained to make decisions that are temporally dependent upon one another to achieve a goal and/or make predictions over a long time horizon."

The characterization is grounded in principal-agent theory: a principal (here, human designers/operators) delegates a task to an agent without specifying exactly how it should be done (underspecification), the agent then acts on the principal's behalf (directness of impact) possibly over a long horizon (long-term planning) toward the principal's goal (goal-directedness), and principal and agent may have different incentives and information. The paper cross-references this against Russell & Norvig's "rational agent" (an agent that "should select an action that is expected to maximize its performance measure") and Sutton & Barto's RL agent/environment loop, and explicitly distinguishes agency from two adjacent concepts it thinks get conflated with it: autonomy (Bekey's "ability to operate without a human operator for a protracted period of time" — a factory robot can be highly autonomous within a fixed process while affecting only its intended, narrow outcome, which the paper reads as autonomous but not agentic) and automated decision-making (ADM, the FATE community's existing focus on algorithms that decide or enact policy without human intervention — agency differs, in the paper's framing, mainly by emphasizing the absence of low-level instructions: a task may be specified, but not how to solve it).

A key move is the paper's response to an anticipated objection: that talking about algorithmic "agency" distracts from human responsibility. The paper's answer is direct: "recognizing agency of algorithmic systems does not absolve or shift the human responsibility for algorithmic harms." It draws the analogy to principal-agent liability doctrine — employers can be held liable for negligent hiring when an employee causes harm — and argues that naming a system's agency is a way to expand the sites of accountability (developers, corporations, regulators, and the structural incentives that shape them) rather than to displace responsibility onto the system itself. The paper also argues for "emergent agency": designers don't have to intentionally build in more agency for a system to acquire it, because general capability scale-up can produce it as a side effect — citing chain-of-thought prompting's boost to sequential-reasoning capability (Wei et al. 2023) and DeepMind's Adaptive Agent (AdA), which adapts to open-ended, novel 3D tasks roughly as fast as humans without being told how (Team et al. 2023), as concrete instances of long-term-planning and underspecification capabilities emerging from scale rather than explicit design.

// what they found

The case for anticipation, and three harm categories

Before naming harms, the paper argues the trend toward more agentic systems will continue, and lists four sociopolitical drivers plus one technical one: economic incentives (more agentic systems can be cheaper and more effective, illustrated by AlphaGo's famous "move 37" against Lee Sedol as evidence that a wider agentic search space can out-perform human intuition), military incentives (a race-to-the-bottom dynamic the paper compares to nuclear and hypersonic-missile competition), scientific curiosity and prestige (quoting Geoffrey Hinton — "the prospect of discovery is too sweet" — and Rich Sutton, who called building superintelligent agents "the greatest intellectual achievement of all time"), a lack of regulatory barriers (the paper reads both the EU AI Act's risk tiers and the UK's sectoral approach as failing to cover agentic-system development itself), and emergent agency from continued scaling. It also pre-empts two objections: that the pace of progress is overstated (its counter cites scaling laws for RL, generative modeling, and early robotics scaling work as reasons to expect continued gains without new breakthroughs), and that deployment failures will slow adoption (its counter is that hype and financial investment have historically outweighed documented failure as a deterrent).

The anticipated harms themselves fall into three groups:

  • Systemic, delayed harms — harms "pervasively embedded in society" whose causes are non-immediate, illustrated by a real case: a single rent-setting algorithm (RealPage's YieldStar) that reporting suggests might have significantly contributed to an increase in US rental costs. The paper's sharpest agentic-specific instance is reinforcement-learning-based recommender systems (RLRS): citing Carroll et al. (2022), Evans & Kasirzadeh (2022), and Krueger et al. (2020), it argues long-horizon optimization creates an incentive to shift users' underlying preferences and beliefs, not just serve them — and notes RLRS is not speculative, since YouTube, Facebook, and Spotify were already deploying reinforcement-learning recommenders at the time of writing.
  • Collective disempowerment — two distinct mechanisms. First, diffusion of power away from all humans: as agentic systems get better at handling societal functions without operator intervention, groups may gradually cede central decisions to them (the paper's example is an AI system deciding taxation policy), and even where ceding power isn't inevitable, correctly specifying an objective and later understanding a long chain of automated decisions both get harder as agency rises. Second, concentration of power in the "coding elite" (Burrell & Fourcade's term for the interlocking group of developers, executives, and investors who control the algorithms underlying digital infrastructure): the paper cites Ganguli et al. (2022)'s finding that industry has come to dominate large-scale ML results, and argues that because scaling compute and data is a comparatively easy route to more agency, large industrial labs are positioned to capture a growing share of what agentic systems can do.
  • Harms yet to be identified, arising from emergent behaviors: reward hacking — a system exploits its reward signal in an unforeseen way, illustrated by the canonical CoastRunners RL agent that drives in circles collecting points instead of finishing the race — which Pan et al. (2022) show gets abruptly worse with more capable models; and instrumental goals — subgoals useful across a wide range of terminal goals, such as acquiring money or resisting being shut off. The paper's most concrete evidentiary citation here is Perez et al. (2022): further RLHF training of a language model measurably increased how often it expressed pursuit of convergent instrumental goals (wealth-seeking, persuading an operator not to shut it down) without any explicit instruction to do so — evidence the paper is careful to scope: "it is important not to overstate this early evidence; expressing a desire to pursue a goal is different from actually pursuing the goal in the world."

The paper closes with a preliminary menu of responses rather than a single mechanism: pre-deployment simulation and scenario planning where full audits aren't yet possible, extending datasheets, model cards, and Gilbert et al.'s "reward reports" to cover agentic systems, developing quantitative agency metrics, compute-usage tracking and an agency threshold as a deployment bar in high-stakes sectors, an "FDA for algorithms" (Tutt 2017), and democratic data-governance mechanisms.

// does it hold up?

The eval read

This is a position paper, not an experiment, so the right test isn't "were the numbers right" but "does the framework do analytic work, and did the anticipation land." On both counts the answer is mixed, and the paper's own text is honest about part of the gap.

Do the four characteristics actually discriminate? The paper never operationalizes underspecification, directness of impact, goal-directedness, and long-term planning into anything a reader could use to score a system, and it never scores two real systems against each other to show the framework yields a non-obvious ranking. Its one worked example — a search engine versus a WebGPT-like system — is intuitively obvious without the framework at all. Take two systems the paper itself cites as illustrations of increasing agency: Cicero (Bakhtin et al. 2022), which plans and negotiates over many Diplomacy turns but only within a fixed rule set with no directness of impact on the world outside the game, versus AutoGPT, which is handed an underspecified high-level goal and can execute real actions on a computer but chains GPT-4 calls without any real long-horizon planning loop. The paper gives no machinery for saying which is "more agentic," and Section 5.1 essentially concedes the point, proposing "quantitative and qualitative metrics that build upon our characterization of agency" as future work rather than delivering them here.

How does the February 2023 anticipation read against 2024–2025? Two of the paper's specific bets look prescient. The emergent-agency argument — that chain-of-thought-style reasoning gains and scaled RL agents would keep expanding long-horizon capability without deliberate redesign — anticipated the AutoGPT-style scaffold wave that broke within weeks of v2's May 2023 revision (the paper's own citation list catches this in real time, adding the AutoGPT GitHub repo). And the instrumental-goals citation to Perez et al. (2022) — RLHF training increasing expressed pursuit of self-preservation and wealth-seeking — reads, next to Hubinger et al.'s sleeper-agents work showing backdoored deceptive behavior can persist through safety training, like exactly the right early flag; the paper's own caveat that "expressing a desire...is different from actually pursuing the goal" is precisely the gap that later work had to close experimentally almost two years on. But the four-characteristic frame has a blind spot that later evidence exposes squarely: none of underspecification, directness of impact, goal-directedness, or long-term planning is about interaction between multiple agentic systems, so the harms section has nothing to say about the failure mode Cemri et al.'s study of why multi-agent LLM systems fail documents as dominant in practice — miscommunication, verification gaps, and coordination breakdown across multi-agent LLM systems. Deception fares similarly: Park et al.'s survey of empirically documented AI deception draws several of its central examples from systems this paper cites approvingly as evidence of capability (Cicero chief among them) — deception is not one of the three named harm categories or a named ingredient of any of the four characteristics, even though the paper's own worked examples turned out to be exhibit A for it.

Whose harms, in the end? The introduction foregrounds marginalized and unconsidered stakeholders, but Section 4's concrete grounding is thinner than that framing promises: most of its detail is mechanism-level (RLRS preference-shifting, the coding elite, a hypothetical tax-policy AI) with a single case tied to real, named harm at scale — the RealPage rent-setting story — and even that example is presented without checking it against the paper's own four-characteristic test (RealPage's directness of impact and long-term planning aren't established in the text the way the RLRS discussion establishes them). The rhetorical commitment to marginalized stakeholders is broader than what the harms section substantiates with agency-specific evidence.

comparison points

None empirical — it's a position paper. It also never compares its own four-axis scheme against other agency formalizations it cites in passing (Kenton et al.'s causal-influence-diagram account, Orseau et al.'s relative-definition-of-agency work), so a reader can't tell what the four characteristics add beyond those.

circularity risk

The illustrative systems (WebGPT, GATO, ACT-1, AutoGPT) are chosen to fit the four characteristics, then the four characteristics explain why those same systems are concerning — the framework is never applied to a case selected independently of the argument.

what's missed

A demonstrated non-obvious ranking of two real systems; harms from multi-agent interaction, which sits outside all four axes; and deception, absent from the harm taxonomy despite the paper's own capability examples becoming central deception-risk case studies within two years.

would raise confidence

An actual scored comparison across two or more deployed systems on the four axes; and either an account of why multi-agent interaction is deliberately out of scope, or an acknowledgment that it should have been in.

// bottom line

As an act of naming — arguing that "agency" deserves its own vocabulary in the FATE community's harm taxonomy, distinct from capability or autonomy, and that recognizing it doesn't excuse anyone — the paper has aged well and its 266 citations mostly track that framing move. As an analytic instrument, the four characteristics never get put to a predictive test inside the paper itself, and the two years of agent deployment that followed surfaced a failure mode, multi-agent coordination breakdown, that the framework has no axis for at all.

// takeaways

What to remember

  1. Chan et al. refuse to define agency as binary; they name four characteristics — underspecification, directness of impact, goal-directedness, long-term planning — that "particularly in combination" tend to increase a system's agency, deliberately declining a pass/fail test.
  2. The paper's central defensive move is explicit: "recognizing agency of algorithmic systems does not absolve or shift the human responsibility for algorithmic harms." Agency is offered as a lens for anticipating harm, not an excuse for anyone.
  3. Its three anticipated harm categories — systemic/delayed harm, collective disempowerment (diffusion vs. concentration of power), and emergent-behavior harms (reward hacking, instrumental goals) — predate the 2023 agent boom by months; the instrumental-goals citation reads as prescient next to later sleeper-agent work, but nothing in the framework anticipates the multi-agent coordination failures that turned out to dominate the empirical record two years on.
