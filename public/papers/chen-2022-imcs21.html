A Benchmark for Automatic Medical Consultation System: Frameworks, Tasks and Datasets

IMCS-21: a 4,116-dialogue, 164,731-utterance Chinese pediatric-telemedicine corpus with token/utterance/dialogue-level annotations, packaged into five independent tasks — named entity recognition, dialogue act classification, symptom label inference, medical report generation, and diagnosis-oriented dialogue policy — each scored against its own baseline models on a shared train/dev/test split.

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

By 2022 there were already several Chinese medical-dialogue corpora, but the authors group them into two unsatisfying categories. The first is raw, unannotated conversation logs — MedDialog (3.4M Chinese conversations) and KaMed (60K+ sessions) — which support only end-to-end response generation, and whose chatbot outputs "lack interpretability and controllability" for a healthcare setting. The second category is a scatter of smaller annotated sets, most built for a single modelling task apiece: MSL for slot-filling, CMDD/SAT/MIE for medical information extraction, MZ/DX/RD-SD for symptom-driven diagnosis policy, Chunyu for end-to-end diagnosis-oriented generation — and MedDG, which the paper itself describes as an outlier: more than 17K conversations spanning two established tasks (next-sentence entity prediction and dialogue response generation). The authors frame their critique as a hedge, not a universal: "the majority" of these datasets provide only "one specific medical label," which they argue is "too coarse to accurately describe the patient's state and intent," and are "typically on the order of hundreds of dialogues" — a description that, by the paper's own numbers, MedDG doesn't fit.

The paper's diagnosis is that automatic medical consultation has never had a single framework that (a) defines the full set of sub-problems a real consultation touches — understanding what was said and deciding what to do next — and (b) backs that framework with one benchmark corpus, richly enough labelled that all of those sub-problems can be trained and evaluated on the same data. IMCS-21 is built to close both gaps at once: one corpus, five tasks, shared splits.

// how it works

The method

The paper splits automatic medical consultation into two frameworks. The Dialogue Understanding framework is static: it extracts structured information from an already-recorded conversation, at three levels of granularity. Token-level output is Named Entity Recognition (NER) — locating symptom (SX), drug name (DN), drug category (DC), examination (EX) and operation (OP) mentions with BIO tagging (11 tags total). Utterance-level output is Dialogue Act Classification (DAC) — tagging each turn with one of 16 dialogue acts built from a request/inform split crossed with eight information categories (basic info, symptom, etiology, existing exam/treatment, medical advice, drug recommendation, precautions, diagnosis) plus an "other" catch-all. Dialogue-level output is Symptom Label Inference (SLI) — identifying which of 331 SNOMED-CT-normalized symptom names were discussed and whether the patient is Positive (POS), Negative (NEG), or of unclear status (Not Sure, NS) for each — and Medical Report Generation (MRG), producing a six-part structured report (chief complaint, present disease, auxiliary examination, past medical history, diagnosis, suggestions) that summarizes the whole conversation. The Task-oriented Interaction framework is dynamic: its single task, Diagnosis-oriented Dialogue Policy (DDP), decides which symptom to ask about next and when to commit to a diagnosis, using the same structured symptom features SLI is trained to predict as its training signal — SLI and DDP are explicitly coupled rather than independent.

The corpus itself, IMCS-21, is built from Muzhi (muzhi.baidu.com), a Chinese online pediatric health community, covering 10 pediatric diseases: bronchitis, fever, diarrhea, upper respiratory infection, dyspepsia, cold, cough, jaundice, constipation and bronchopneumonia. After filtering incomplete or too-short samples, the authors annotate 4,116 medical consultation records (164,731 utterances total) with a web-based multi-turn labeling tool, using undergraduate and postgraduate medical-school annotators; two annotations are collected per dialogue and a third annotator resolves disagreements. Inter-annotator Cohen's kappa is 83.11% for named entities, 76.41% for dialogue acts, and 92.71% for symptom labels. The standard split — used identically across all five tasks — is 2,472 training / 833 dev / 811 test dialogues, at the dialogue level (so no dialogue's turns are split across sets).

The results

Corpus analysis first: symptom entities dominate the annotated content, at 58.3% of all tagged entities, followed by examination (16.9%), drug name (11.5%), drug category (9.3%) and operation (4.0%). Among dialogue acts, the two most frequent categories are I-SX (patient telling the doctor a symptom, 20.0% of tagged acts) and R-SX (doctor asking about a symptom, 13.6%) — consistent with the authors' reading that "doctor-patient conversations mainly discuss the patient's symptoms." The positional heat-map of dialogue acts across a conversation's five quintiles shows requests about basic info/symptoms concentrated early and acts about drugs, treatments and precautions concentrated later — the consultation has a discernible shape.

Per task, the headline benchmark numbers (all values are percentages, per the paper's own tables):

NER (Table 4, token-level P/R/F1 across 7 Chinese NER baselines): all seven land around F1 90%. ERNIE-Health reaches the highest precision (89.71) and F1 (91.24) among the baselines, while LEBERT reports the highest recall (92.91). The domain-adapted models (MC-BERT, ERNIE-Health) show only what the authors call "a weak advantage" over generic-domain LMs (BERT-CRF, ERNIE, FLAT) on this task.

DAC (Table 5, P/R/F1/Accuracy across 8 baselines): pre-trained models clearly beat the four non-pretrained baselines (TextCNN/TextRNN/TextRCNN/DPCNN, accuracy 78.5–79.4) — BERT, ERNIE, MC-BERT and ERNIE-Health all land in the 81.5–82.4 accuracy range, with ERNIE-Health highest at 82.37% accuracy (F1 76.71). As in NER, the gain from biomedical-specific pretraining over generic pretraining is small.

SLI splits into two cognate sub-tasks with separate metrics (Tables 6–7). For symptom recognition (finding which symptoms were mentioned, ignoring their POS/NEG/NS label): SLI-EXP (explicit symptoms in the self-report) reaches example-level subset accuracy 73.24–75.34 and label-level F1 up to 90.54 (MC-BERT-MLC); SLI-IMP (implicit symptoms scattered through the full dialogue) is markedly harder at the example level — subset accuracy only 34.16–37.48 — while label-level F1 still reaches 89.6–91.62, since with dozens of possible symptoms per dialogue, getting every single one exactly right (subset accuracy) is a much stricter bar than getting most of them right (label-level F1). The multi-task BERT-MTL/MC-BERT-MTL variants, which additionally exploit the NER BIO labels during training, edge out the plain MLC variants on SLI-IMP recognition. For symptom inference in SLI-IMP (predicting the POS/NEG/NS label once a symptom is correctly identified, Table 7): every model scores far higher on POS (79.64–81.25 F1) than on NEG (41.30–53.87 F1) or NS (58.15–60.20 F1); the MTL variants' overall macro F1 (64.27–64.57) beats the plain MLC variants (60.08–62.31), driven almost entirely by better NEG/NS detection.

MRG (Table 8, ROUGE-1/2/L, Concept-F1, and regex-based diagnostic accuracy RD-Acc, across 6 generation baselines): pretrained generative models improve ROUGE scores over the non-pretrained Seq2seq/PG/Transformer baselines, but the paper notes the R-2 gain over Pointer-Generator specifically is "quite limited." Bio-ProphetNet (ProphetNet further pretrained on MedDialog) posts the best R-1 (61.83), R-2 (47.12) and Concept-F1 (50.12) of the six baselines; T5 actually posts the highest R-L, at 57.63 versus Bio-ProphetNet's 57.48. ProphetNet (without the extra MedDialog pretraining) posts the best RD-Acc at 61.90, narrowly ahead of Bio-ProphetNet's 61.15. T5 shows a high ROUGE score but, in the authors' words, "performs mediocrely" on RD-Acc (49.32) — a case where the summarization-style metric and the diagnostic-content metric diverge for the same model.

DDP (Table 9, three metrics — symptom recall SX-Rec, diagnostic accuracy DX-Acc, average number of turns — across 5 dynamic baselines, 4 of them RL and one, GAMP, GAN-based): HRL is best on both SX-Rec (0.295) and DX-Acc (0.556) among the five interactive agents, at an average 6.99 turns. GAMP is fastest (1.78 turns) and mid-pack on accuracy (0.500), but second-weakest on recall (0.067) — DQN is worse still, at 0.047. The static UB-SVM reference point — an SVM classifier given every gold symptom (explicit and implicit) up front, described by the authors as "an invalid static agent" — reaches 0.706 DX-Acc, meaning even a perfect symptom-collector tops out well short of 1.0 on this 10-disease classification problem, and every dynamic agent is still 15–30 points of DX-Acc below that ceiling.

// does it hold up?

The eval read

As a benchmark-construction paper this is careful and unusually well-instrumented (kappa scores reported, per-metric baselines matched to each task's actual difficulty), but the released tables have real rough edges, and the headline aggregates hide task-specific weaknesses worth naming explicitly.

Start with an internal inconsistency in the paper's own flagship table. Table 4 prints ERNIE-Health's NER recall as 2.82, alongside a precision of 89.71 and an F1 of 91.24 — but F1 is the harmonic mean of precision and recall, and a recall of 2.82 with a precision of 89.71 would force F1 down near 5, not up to 91.24. The paper's own prose ("ERNIE-Health has the highest Precision and F1 score, while LEBERT performs best on Recall") implies ERNIE-Health's real recall sits competitively among the other six baselines (which range 90.84–92.91), so 2.82 reads as a transcription error in the peer-reviewed table that survived to publication — worth flagging to anyone citing this table's recall column directly rather than treating F1/precision as the reliable entries.

Second, the paper's narrative conclusion for MRG doesn't fully match its own Table 8: the text states "Overall, ProphetNet…has the best performance," but Bio-ProphetNet actually leads on three of the five reported metrics (R-1, R-2, Concept-F1); T5 leads R-L (57.63 vs. Bio-ProphetNet's 57.48), and plain ProphetNet leads RD-Acc, ahead of Bio-ProphetNet by only 0.75 points. Table 8 itself compounds the confusion here: it bolds Bio-ProphetNet's 57.48 as the R-L maximum even though T5's 57.63 is higher — a second, independently verifiable table error, in the same family as the Table 4 recall cell above. Bio-ProphetNet is also the one baseline given extra domain pretraining (on MedDialog), so a reader relying on the prose summary — or on the table's own (wrong) bolding — rather than re-checking the column would come away with the wrong sense of which model the extra medical pretraining actually helped.

On baselines: the Chinese-NER/NLU model line-up (Lattice LSTM, BERT, ERNIE, FLAT, LEBERT, MC-BERT, ERNIE-Health) is contemporary for 2022 and appropriately spans generic vs. domain-adapted pretraining — but the paper's own consistent finding, repeated across NER, DAC and SLI, is that domain-specific biomedical pretraining brings only a "weak advantage" over generic-domain Chinese LMs. That's a genuinely useful negative result, but it also means none of the reported gaps between baselines are large enough to rule out that differences are noise from a single train/dev/test split rather than a real ranking — no variance across seeds or splits is reported for any task.

On what the aggregates miss: SLI-IMP's headline label-level F1 (~90%) is a "symptom recognition" number that ignores POS/NEG/NS correctness entirely; the separately-reported "symptom inference" F1 (Table 7) shows the real per-label story — POS detection is comparatively easy (79.6–81.25 F1) while NEG (41.30–53.87) and NS (58.15–60.20) are much harder, and clinically the ability to correctly register that a patient does not have a symptom, or that it's unclear, matters for differential diagnosis at least as much as confirming positives. A reader who only sees the aggregated SLI numbers in the abstract or a leaderboard would not see this split.

On the DDP setup specifically: the patient simulator P is a lookup function over gold-labeled symptom data (returning UNK for anything not mentioned in the dialogue), not a language-based patient — the paper is explicit that a natural-language simulator is future work, so DX-Acc/SX-Rec here measure policy-learning over structured symptom slots, not a full doctor-patient interaction loop. And because the task only spans 10 pediatric diseases, the 0.706 SVM upper bound and the 0.408–0.556 range for the RL/GAN agents shouldn't be read as a general estimate of automatic-diagnosis difficulty; it's specific to this narrow, already-curated 10-way classification problem.

One more provenance note for anyone chasing citations from this article: a few in-text model attributions in the "Experimental settings" paragraphs point to bibliography entries that don't obviously match. "MC-BERT (Zhang et al., 2022)" resolves, via the paper's own reference list, to "CBLUE: A Chinese Biomedical Language Understanding Evaluation Benchmark" (Zhang, N. et al., 2022) — a benchmark-suite paper, not an obvious source for the whole-entity/whole-span-masking pretraining described in the text — and "T5 (Xue et al., 2021)" resolves to the mT5 paper rather than the original T5 paper. Neither affects the reported numbers, but it's a reminder to verify a baseline's actual source paper rather than trusting the in-text citation key.

baselines

Reasonable and task-matched (7 Chinese NER models, 8 text-classifiers, 2 encoders for SLI, 6 generation models, 5 RL/GAN diagnosis agents), but no variance is reported anywhere, and the domain-vs-generic pretraining comparison the paper itself highlights shows only small, single-run gaps.

contamination

Not really a contamination risk in the LLM-benchmark sense — the corpus is newly collected from Muzhi and released with its own gold test labels — but that also means anyone benchmarking against this test set going forward is training against a fully public, labeled test partition with no held-out leaderboard mechanism.

what's missed

SLI's aggregated recognition F1 hides the POS-vs-NEG/NS gap (Table 7); MRG's ROUGE gains don't track its diagnostic-content metric (RD-Acc) model-for-model; DDP's accuracy numbers are bounded by a 10-disease, structured-symptom setup, not open-ended diagnosis.

would raise confidence

A corrected/erratum'd Table 4 recall column, seed-level variance on at least one task per baseline family, and — since this 2022 benchmark is now roughly four years and several LLM generations old — a follow-up comparison against instruction-tuned Chinese medical LLMs on the same fixed splits.

// bottom line

IMCS-21 is a genuinely useful, well-annotated Chinese medical-dialogue benchmark that is honest about where its own baselines fall short (it says so in its own prose for DDP and for the domain-pretraining comparison) — but a couple of its own tables and narrative claims don't fully agree with each other, and the aggregated per-task metrics smooth over real, clinically-relevant gaps (POS vs. NEG/NS symptom inference, ROUGE vs. diagnostic accuracy) that only show up if you read the sub-tables, not just the abstract.

// takeaways

What to remember

  1. IMCS-21 replaces the field's prior pattern of one small, single-label dataset per sub-task with a single 4,116-dialogue, 164,731-utterance Chinese pediatric-consultation corpus carrying token/utterance/dialogue-level annotations that support five independent, shared-split tasks: NER, DAC, SLI (with EXP/IMP sub-tasks), MRG and DDP.
  2. The benchmark results split sharply by task type: static language-understanding tasks (NER F1 ≈90%, DAC accuracy ≈82%) are already well-handled by off-the-shelf pretrained Chinese LMs, but tasks closer to real clinical use either look strong only on an aggregated metric (SLI's ~90% label-F1 masks a POS-vs-NEG/NS split of 80% vs. 41–60% F1; MRG's ROUGE scores don't track its own diagnostic-accuracy metric) or remain clearly unsolved (DDP's best RL agent reaches 0.556 diagnostic accuracy against a 0.706 upper bound from a perfect symptom-collector).
  3. On the three tasks where the comparison is actually run — NER, DAC and SLI symptom recognition — Chinese-biomedical-specific pretraining (MC-BERT, ERNIE-Health) shows only a "weak advantage" over generic-domain Chinese pretraining by the authors' own account, and on SLI-IMP symptom inference MC-BERT is outright worse than plain BERT (macro F1 60.08 vs. 62.31 for the MLC variants, 64.27 vs. 64.57 for MTL). MC-BERT and ERNIE-Health were never run on MRG or DDP at all, and on MRG the one domain-pretrained model that was tested, Bio-ProphetNet, is the strongest baseline on three of five metrics — so domain pretraining's payoff on this corpus is task- and model-dependent, not a uniform negative result, though no run-to-run variance is reported anywhere to confirm even the "weak advantage" gaps are real rather than noise.
