CoAuthor: Designing a Human-AI Collaborative Writing Dataset for Exploring Language Model Capabilities

63 writers, four GPT-3 instances, 1,445 writing sessions, every keystroke and suggestion logged — CoAuthor argues that interaction traces, not aggregate scores, are how you find out what a language model actually does in someone's hands.

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

By early 2022, the standard way to ask "what can this language model do" was to run it against a benchmark: GLUE, MMLU, a fixed test set with a fixed metric, one number out. CoAuthor's authors argue this approach has a structural blind spot for models like GPT-3 that are meant to be used interactively. A benchmark score assumes full automation — the model produces an answer and the answer is graded — but a writing assistant doesn't work alone. It proposes; a person accepts, rejects, edits, or ignores; the two go back and forth. None of that process shows up in a static score, and the paper argues it's exactly the process that determines whether the model is actually useful.

The two existing alternatives each capture only part of the picture. Contextual inquiry — inviting a handful of writers to use the model and interviewing them afterward, as Calderwood et al. did with four professional novelists writing with GPT-2 — gets rich, felt insight into how one person experienced one model, but it's unclear how far those four interviews generalize to other writers, other prompts, or the next version of GPT. Tinkering in the OpenAI Playground has the same problem in the other direction: fast, but idiosyncratic and impossible to systematize. Prior interaction-logging work (Roemmele & Gordon on an RNN storyteller; Buschek et al. on GPT-2 email suggestions) covered more ground but was tied to one narrow task, one adapted model, and a restricted turn-taking interface — the findings don't transfer to a different writing task or a different model.

CoAuthor's response is to treat the interaction log itself as the research artifact: capture every keystroke, every suggestion GPT-3 offers, and every accept/reject/edit decision, across enough writers, prompts, and decoding settings that the resulting dataset can support repeated, independent analyses — the way a benchmark dataset is reused across papers, but built to preserve process rather than collapse it into a single result.

// how it works

The method

The paper states four design desiderata for an interaction dataset meant to reveal an LM's generative capabilities: it should cover diverse contexts (tasks, prompts, writers), support subjective interpretation rather than impose one metric, capture process, not just results, and be reusable and expandable. CoAuthor is built to satisfy all four at once.

63 crowd workers were recruited on Amazon Mechanical Turk (from 201 who took a qualification round, 100 qualified, 63 completed the main study; 62 of the 63 were native English speakers) to write with GPT-3 across two writing tasks — creative writing (10 story prompts pulled from the r/WritingPrompts subreddit) and argumentative writing (10 essay prompts from The New York Times) — for at least ten minutes per session, up to five sessions per prompt. GPT-3 (the davinci engine, unmodified — no fine-tuning) generated suggestions under four fixed decoding configurations spanning temperature 0.2–0.9 and frequency penalty 0–1: one "low-randomness" and one "high-randomness" instance per writing type, four instances in total (creative: T 0.3/FP 0 vs. T 0.75/FP 1; argumentative: T 0.2/FP 0.5 vs. T 0.9/FP 0.5).

The interface (Figure 3) is a text editor: writers type freely, and pressing Tab requests five GPT-3 suggestions (each parsed down to a single sentence with Stanza for readability); a suggestion is accepted if the writer selects it from the popup, and dismissed if they keep typing, click away, or hit Escape. Every action — text-insert, text-delete, cursor-forward/backward, suggestion-get/open/select/close, and more — is logged as a timestamped event with a snapshot of the editor state (Table 1), then compressed into coarser, non-overlapping event blocks (insert, delete, cursor, query, choose, dismiss, reopen, navigate — Table 2) that later analyses are built on. The result is 1,445 such sessions, plus a post-session survey on ownership, satisfaction, and perceived collaboration quality, and a public replay interface (coauthor.stanford.edu) that lets a researcher watch any session unfold keystroke by keystroke.

To turn raw event logs into the paper's later analyses, the authors define two session-level scores directly from event-block counts. Let H = human-only writing (insert) and M = machine-accepted writing (choose); let I = interaction blocks (insert, choose, reopen, navigate) and A = solo-writing blocks (dismiss, insert, delete) — note insert deliberately belongs to both I and A in the paper's own definitions, since inserting text is simultaneously "writing" and (when it follows a suggestion) part of an interaction.

equality = 1 − |Σ[e∈H] − Σ[e∈M]|Σ[e∈H] + Σ[e∈M] // 1 = perfectly balanced turns between writer and GPT-3, 0 = one party did all the writing
mutuality = Σ[e∈I]Σ[e∈I] + Σ[e∈A] // 1 = writer interacted with GPT-3 the whole session, 0 = writer never interacted

These two scores, adapted from Storch's (2002) notions of equality and mutuality in human collaborative writing, are the paper's own instrument for turning an interaction log into a comparable per-session number — they are computed over the full event stream, not just the final text, so text later deleted or never accepted still counts toward them.

// what they found

The results

Table 3 gives the dataset's own vital statistics: 830 stories from 58 writers (creative) and 615 essays from 49 writers (argumentative), 1,445 sessions total. On average a session runs 11.2 minutes, produces 418 words, involves 11.8 suggestion requests, has a 72.3% acceptance rate, and ends with 72.6% of the final text written by the human rather than GPT-3 — creative sessions ran slightly longer and had a higher acceptance rate (75.7% vs. 67.6%) than argumentative ones.

The paper then uses the dataset to probe three kinds of capability, each demonstrating a different way to read an interaction log rather than score it.

Language capabilities

Comparing sentences in the final texts by author, GPT-3's sentences had fewer spelling/grammar errors than the writers' own (0.033 vs. 0.037 errors per word, both ± 0.001), and sentences produced jointly (writer text plus an accepted GPT-3 suggestion) had the fewest of all (0.032 ± 0.001) — matching Dou et al.'s (2021) independent finding that GPT-3 outwrites humans on surface grammaticality. Vocabulary diversity (distinct-2, the fraction of unique bigrams) showed the opposite shape: writer-only (0.884 ± 0.001) and GPT-3-only (0.876 ± 0.001) sentences were similar, but jointly-written sentences were noticeably more diverse (0.923 ± 0.001) — and GPT-3's own diversity drops even further, to 0.868 ± 0.001, when distinct-2 is computed over all suggestions GPT-3 generated rather than just the accepted ones, further confirming that GPT-3 output alone is less diverse than human writing; the fact that accepted suggestions (0.876) score higher than the full suggestion pool (0.868) suggests writers were selecting the more distinctive completions, not merely being handed them.

Ideation capabilities

To test whether GPT-3 contributes genuinely new ideas rather than just extending what's already there, the authors flag accepted suggestions that introduce a new named entity (via Stanza NER, checked against everything written so far) and track whether the writer reuses it later. In creative writing, 13% of accepted suggestions introduced a new named entity, and 20% of those were reused downstream by the writer (Figure 7); in argumentative writing the rates were lower — 7% introduced a new entity, 14% of those reused. High-randomness GPT-3 instances introduced new entities more often than low-randomness ones (creative: 15±1% vs. 10±0%; argumentative: 9±1% vs. 6±0%), while the reuse rate once introduced was roughly randomness-independent in both writing types.

Collaboration capabilities

Equality and mutuality (defined above) varied far more across writers than across prompts: Figure 8 shows individual writers ranging from equality ≈0.07 to ≈0.53 and mutuality ≈0.13 to ≈0.80, while the same scores barely move across the ten prompts within a writing type (Figure 9). Randomness had a small, type-dependent effect: in argumentative writing, high-randomness sessions scored higher on both equality (0.29±0.01 vs. 0.22±0.01) and mutuality (0.47±0.01 vs. 0.38±0.01), with 9 of 10 prompts (90%) favoring high randomness on both scores. In creative writing the aggregate difference was smaller and, per the paper, not statistically significant — equality was marginally higher under high randomness (0.30±0.01 vs. 0.29±0.01) while mutuality was marginally lower (0.48±0.01 vs. 0.49±0.01); broken out by prompt, low randomness actually won on equality in 5 of 10 prompts and on mutuality in 8 of 10.

Finally, the paper shows CoAuthor can support hypothesis generation for two informal definitions of "good collaboration." For productivity, the amount of text a session produces correlates with the number of suggestion queries (r = 0.42) and the number of accepted suggestions (r = 0.48) more strongly than with time spent writing (r = 0.29). For ownership, the fraction of the final text a writer personally wrote correlates 0.3 with their self-reported ownership (both writing types), but only 0.1 (creative) / 0.0 (argumentative) with their satisfaction — the same predictor, a much weaker relationship to the outcome. The amount of editing a writer did (delete and cursor event blocks) barely correlates with either: r = 0.1 with ownership, r = 0.0 (creative) / −0.1 (argumentative) with satisfaction. The paper is explicit that these are correlational hypotheses from "simplified definitions," not causal claims — validating them requires, as it puts it, "further experiments with interventions."

// does it hold up?

The eval read

CoAuthor is not trying to be a leaderboard, and it says so explicitly — Section 2.4 argues that the standard NLP-benchmark assumption of full automation misses interactive settings entirely. That framing is honest, but it also means the paper's evidentiary standard has to be judged on its own terms: does the dataset actually support the capability claims made from it, and how far do those claims generalize?

Every headline number comes from a single model family observed once. All 1,445 sessions used the same GPT-3 davinci engine with no fine-tuning, varied only along two decoding knobs (temperature, frequency penalty) collapsed into a binary "low/high randomness" split. The grammaticality, diversity, ideation, and collaboration findings are therefore facts about one 2021-era model under one prompting regime, not general facts about "language models." The paper doesn't claim otherwise, but a reader skimming the abstract easily could — "GPT-3's capabilities in assisting creative and argumentative writing" reads more general than "one non-adapted engine at four fixed sampling settings."

The 63-writer, two-genre design is also a real scope limit the paper is candid about but doesn't quantify: 62 of 63 writers were native English speakers, all recruited from Mechanical Turk (a specific, non-representative population of paid crowd workers), writing on 20 total prompts (10 creative, 10 argumentative) drawn from just two sources — a subreddit and a newspaper column. The equality/mutuality result that between-writer variance swamps between-prompt variance (Figure 8 vs. Figure 9) is itself evidence that 10 prompts per genre may be too few to see prompt-level effects clearly, and 63 writers is a small base for the kind of individual-difference claims the collaboration analysis leans on.

The accept/reject signal is a weaker proxy for suggestion quality than the analysis sometimes treats it as. A writer might accept a mediocre suggestion because they're rushing to hit the ten-minute minimum, or reject a genuinely good one because it doesn't fit a plot they'd already committed to — the paper's own event log can't distinguish "accepted because good" from "accepted because convenient." The ideation analysis partially sidesteps this by tracking downstream reuse of named entities rather than acceptance alone, which is a stronger signal — but the core "acceptance rate" and "written-by-writers %" statistics in Table 3 carry this ambiguity without a caveat.

On the positive side, the paper is unusually careful about what it does not claim: Section 5.2 flags its own productivity and ownership findings as correlational hypotheses requiring intervention studies to validate, and the equality/mutuality formulas are given in full, in the open, rather than gestured at — a reader can recompute both from the released event logs. That transparency is worth more here than an extra ablation would be, because the paper's real contribution is the dataset and the replay tool, not a single number to be reproduced.

baselines

There isn't a baseline comparison in the usual sense — the paper studies one model against itself (writer-alone vs. GPT-3-alone vs. jointly-written text), not GPT-3 against a competing model or an older LM generation.

contamination

Not applicable in the training-data sense; the relevant analogue is generalization, and the dataset is a one-time 2021 snapshot of one engine — it cannot be "refreshed" the way a benchmark test set can be re-scored against a newer model without a new human study.

what's missed

No inter-writer variance is reported on the acceptance-rate or written-by-writers statistics in Table 3 (only session-averaged means), and accept/reject is conflated with quality without a caveat despite the paper's own framing implying otherwise.

would raise confidence

A second model family run through the same interface and event schema, and a released per-writer breakdown of acceptance rate alongside the equality/mutuality scores already provided.

// bottom line

CoAuthor is a well-instrumented, honestly scoped snapshot of how 63 people actually used one 2021 GPT-3 instance to write — strong as a replayable, reusable interaction dataset and as an argument for why HCI evaluation of LMs needs this kind of artifact, but its capability claims are tied tightly to that one model, that one interface, and that one writer population, and the paper is careful to say so.

// takeaways

What to remember

  1. CoAuthor logs every keystroke and every GPT-3 suggestion (accepted or not) across 1,445 writing sessions from 63 writers, then argues that this kind of full interaction trace — not a benchmark score — is what actually reveals a language model's context-dependent capabilities.
  2. The dataset supports three concrete capability readings: GPT-3 sentences are more grammatical but less diverse than writers' own (0.033 vs. 0.037 errors/word; jointly-written text is both most grammatical and most diverse at 0.923 distinct-2); 13%/7% of accepted suggestions (creative/argumentative) introduce new named entities, of which 20%/14% get reused downstream; and collaboration equality/mutuality vary far more by writer than by prompt.
  3. Every finding comes from one non-fine-tuned GPT-3 engine at four fixed decoding settings, observed once, on 20 prompts, with 63 mostly-native-English Mechanical Turk writers — the paper is explicit that its productivity and ownership correlations are hypotheses for future intervention studies, not settled causal claims.
