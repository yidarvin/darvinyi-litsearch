MedicalAgentsBench for Complex Medical Reasoning: Comparing Internalized Reasoning Models versus Externalized Agent-based Frameworks

Every model class had already conquered ordinary medical QA — GPT-4o alone clears 88.2% on MedQA and 90.9% on MMLU. MedicalAgentsBench instead builds an 862-question hard set, screened for contamination and reviewed by four clinicians, specifically to find where ten base models and nine agent frameworks still disagree. The finding: internalized "thinking" models and externalized multi-agent scaffolding are not rivals but a stack — the paper's best score, o3-mini layered with the MDAgents framework at 35.1%, needs both.

// the gap

What problem is this solving?

Medical-QA benchmarks have been solved from the top down. On the standard, full versions of MedQA, MMLU, MMLU-Pro, MedExQA and friends, a model like GPT-4o already scores 88.2%, 90.9%, 68.6% and 84.8% respectively — numbers high enough that two very different systems can post nearly identical averages while failing on completely different questions. That ceiling hides the question the field actually needs answered: as LLMs get two distinct upgrades — reasoning models such as o1-mini, o3-mini and DeepSeek-R1 that are trained (via RL, distillation, or long chain-of-thought supervision) to work through multi-step inference inside a single forward pass, and agent frameworks such as MedAgents and MDAgents that externalize the same work into multiple LLM calls that discuss, critique and vote — do these two upgrades compete for the same gains, or do they stack?

No existing medical benchmark could answer that cleanly. Popular datasets (MedQA, PubMedQA, MedMCQA, MMLU, MMLU-Pro, MedBullets, MedExQA, MedXpertQA) are each useful individually, but none is curated specifically to separate strong models from each other, and prior agent-framework papers evaluated their own methods with "inconsistent and subjective sampling practices," making cross-paper comparison unreliable. Almost none of them report cost or latency alongside accuracy, even though agent frameworks multiply the number of LLM calls per question and reasoning models multiply the number of tokens generated per call — both are central to whether a "better" method is actually worth deploying. MedicalAgentsBench is built to close all three gaps at once: a genuinely hard, contamination-screened question set, a standardized evaluation protocol applied identically to every method, and joint accuracy/cost/latency accounting.

// how it works

The method

The benchmark starts from eight established medical QA datasets: MedQA (1,273 USMLE-style questions), PubMedQA (500 yes/no/maybe questions over PubMed abstracts), MedMCQA (2,816 Indian medical-entrance-exam questions), MedBullets (308 USMLE Step 2/3 case questions), MedExQA (935 questions across five underrepresented specialties — audiology, nutrition, occupational therapy, physical therapy, speech-language pathology), MedXpertQA (2,450 expert-level questions, split into a Reasoning subset and an Understanding subset), and the medical subjects of MMLU (1,089 questions) and MMLU-Pro (818 questions, health category only). Ten base models — seven "standard" LLMs (GPT-4o, GPT-4o-mini, DeepSeek-V3, Llama-3.3-70B, QwQ-32B, Claude-3.5-Sonnet, Claude-3.5-Haiku) and three internalized reasoning models (o1-mini, o3-mini, DeepSeek-R1) — are run zero-shot across the full pool of questions, and any question fewer than 50% of those ten models answer correctly is marked a "hard candidate."

From the hard candidates, the authors sample a fixed, balanced hard set: 100 questions each from MedQA, PubMedQA, MedMCQA, MMLU-Pro and MedExQA, 100 each from MedXpertQA's Reasoning and Understanding subsets, 89 from MedBullets (its full hard-candidate pool) and 73 from MMLU — 862 questions in total, averaging 147.4 tokens. Two more filters run on top of the difficulty cut. First, data-contamination screening via MELD (Memorization Effects Levenshtein Detector): each question is split in half, the model is asked to complete the missing half from only the first, and the Levenshtein similarity between its completion and the true continuation estimates memorization (high similarity is suspicious; the authors note the method has "high precision but unknown recall" — a clean score doesn't guarantee an uncontaminated question). Second, human validation: four medical students in their clinical years independently rate a stratified sample of 180 questions (20 per dataset, split Full/Hard) on a five-level reasoning-depth rubric, reaching an overall inter-annotator Fleiss' κ of 0.62, and the same annotators flag outright bad items — 21 questions were dropped for wrong marked answers, missing clinical context, or clinical irrelevance.

On top of the hard set, the authors evaluate nine reasoning/agentic methods — Chain-of-Thought and Self-Consistency (traditional prompting); Multi-Persona, Self-Refine and MedPrompt (advanced single-model prompting); MedAgents and MDAgents (discussion-based multi-agent frameworks, both reimplemented in-house "to ensure accurate parsing of multiple-choice responses"); and AFlow and SPO (agentic workflow-optimization methods that search over agent structures). Because MELD showed OpenAI's models have the lowest measured memorization risk, the primary method comparison (their Table 4) restricts the nine methods to four base models — GPT-4o-mini, GPT-4o, o1-mini and o3-mini — with the two search-based optimization methods (AFlow, SPO) run only on the two non-reasoning GPT-4o variants, since search is expensive. Every agentic method uses a standardized protocol (a fixed two-inference-round pattern for methods like MedAgents, three fixed agent roles for MDAgents) so that differences in the results reflect the method, not an implementation quirk. Cost is tracked throughout: published per-token pricing for GPT-4o/Claude, Together AI's hosting rates for the open-source models, for a total experimental spend of $226.17.

// what they found

The results

The difficulty filter does exactly what it is supposed to do. On the Full test sets, GPT-4o scores 88.2% on MedQA and 84.8% on MedExQA; on the curated Hard subset, the same model drops to 32.2% and 18.1% on the same two datasets. Across the ten base models on the paper's overview chart, DeepSeek-R1 posts the highest average Pass@1 (74.5%), ahead of o3-mini (72.3%), o1-mini (68.9%) and GPT-4o (68.6%), with QwQ-32B lowest (54.9%; Claude-3.5-Haiku, at 56.6%, is second-lowest) — but that ranking barely survives contact with the Hard subset, where DeepSeek-R1 tops five of the nine datasets (MedMCQA 30.9%, MMLU 43.7%, MMLU-Pro 36.8%, MedExQA 26.0%, MedXpertQA-U 26.3%) while o3-mini instead leads on MedQA (52.9%) and MedBullets (50.3%) — the Hard subset separates models the Full sets could not.

The central result is that internalized reasoning and externalized agent scaffolding help independently, and their gains compound rather than substitute for each other. Agent scaffolding lifts weak base models substantially: GPT-4o + AFlow reaches a 29.8% average across the nine Hard datasets, well above bare GPT-4o's own Hard-set average (roughly 18%). But a reasoning model alone already gets further for less: bare o3-mini averages 27.8% zero-shot — beating most agent-augmented non-reasoning configurations outright. Stacking the two pushes higher still: o3-mini + Multi-Persona reaches 34.5% (Table 4), and the paper's best score in the whole study is o3-mini + MDAgents at 35.1% — a reasoning model wearing a discussion-based agent framework on top. Not every agent method helps every base model, though: on GPT-4o-mini, MedAgents (18.3% average) actually scores below the far cheaper SPO (20.1%), which the authors read as heavier scaffolding not automatically buying more accuracy on a weak model.

The Pareto view of cost against accuracy sharpens the same story: externalizing reasoning (adding an agent framework to a fixed base model) always costs more but usually earns more accuracy, while internalizing reasoning (swapping a costly agent stack for a bare reasoning model) can raise accuracy and cut cost at once, since multi-step inference is compiled into the model's weights instead of paid for again on every API call. The frontier the authors trace runs from GPT-4o-mini + SPO at the cheap end, up through bare o3-mini, and out to o3-mini + MDAgents at the expensive, most-accurate end — with GPT-4o-plus-agent configurations sitting well inside the frontier, since o3-mini alone matches or beats them at an order of magnitude lower cost.

// does it hold up?

The eval read

The construction is genuinely careful — a real difficulty filter validated against ten independent models, an explicit contamination check, and human clinician review are all rarer in medical-LLM papers than they should be. But the paper's headline claim is narrower than its framing suggests. The "internalizing and externalizing compound" story is built entirely from OpenAI's o1-mini/o3-mini and GPT-4o/GPT-4o-mini — the four base models the authors' own MELD contamination analysis judged least likely to have memorized the questions. That is a defensible reason to restrict the method comparison, but it also means the model that actually leads the Hard subset on five of nine datasets, DeepSeek-R1, is never given a single agent framework to run with. Whether reasoning-plus-agents compounds on the strongest model in the benchmark — not just the safest one to test — is left untested.

There is also a small but real gap between the paper's running prose and its own Table 4 — and it recurs three times in the same sentence. The Results section states "o3-mini + MDAgents (35.1%)"; the o3-mini block of Table 4 lists that same configuration's average as 35.2%. It then credits "o3-mini + MultiPersona (34.1%)" as the next step down, but Table 4's MultiPersona row averages to 34.5%. And it credits "o3-mini + CoT (32.7%)" as the step after that, but re-summing Table 4's own nine per-dataset cells puts o3-mini + Chain-of-Thought at 33.0% — the 32.7% figure belongs to o3-mini + Self-Refine instead. None of the three slips changes which configuration wins or their relative order (Table 4's own Average column still descends MDAgents > MultiPersona > MedPrompt > CoT > Self-Refine), but a reader who re-derives the numbers (as this page did, by summing Table 4's per-dataset cells directly) will hit three genuine table-vs-prose mismatches in a single results paragraph, not just rounding noise.

The human-validation story is also less uniform than the topline κ=0.62 suggests. MedBullets — one of nine datasets in the reasoning-depth study — comes in at κ=0.11, essentially chance-level agreement, far below every other dataset (the next-lowest is MedXpertQA-U at 0.42). That is also the one dataset the paper describes as already "operat[ing] at relatively high reasoning levels" pre-filtering, so the dataset where the annotators agreed least is exactly the one where the paper is leaning hardest on an assumption rather than a validated measurement.

baselines

Ten base models are benchmarked head-to-head on raw accuracy, which is broad and fair. But the paper's central "reasoning models and agents compound" claim is demonstrated only on four contamination-cleared OpenAI models — DeepSeek-R1, the model that otherwise wins the most Hard-set datasets, is never paired with any of the nine agent frameworks.

contamination

Real MELD screening with a transparent limitation (the authors call out "high precision but unknown recall"). Open-source models (DeepSeek-R1, QwQ-32B, Llama-3.3-70B) show markedly higher memorization signal than OpenAI models even on the Hard subset — which is precisely why they're excluded from the method comparison, but also means their strong Hard-set numbers carry more residual contamination risk than the paper's headline configuration.

what's missed

Training-time cost of building a reasoning model (RL, distillation, CoT supervision) is explicitly and honestly flagged as unmeasured — the "internalizing reasoning is cheaper" claim covers inference only. And no agent framework is ever tested on the benchmark's own best-scoring model.

would raise confidence

Running at least one agent framework (MDAgents or MultiPersona) on DeepSeek-R1 despite the contamination caveat, so the compounding claim is tested on the strongest model rather than only the safest one; a second contamination detector alongside MELD to bound its "unknown recall"; and reconciling Table 4's own cells against the running prose's rounded numbers (35.1 vs. 35.2%, MultiPersona's 34.1 vs. 34.5%, and the CoT/Self-Refine 32.7% swap).

// bottom line

MedicalAgentsBench is a well-engineered hard set — it does what it claims: reopen a ceiling that ordinary medical QA had closed, screen for contamination, and validate reasoning depth with real clinicians. Its central finding, that internalized reasoning and externalized agent scaffolding compound rather than compete, is convincingly shown on OpenAI's models. It just isn't shown on the model the benchmark itself says is strongest, which means the paper's most quoted number — 35.1% — rests on a narrower evidentiary base than the abstract implies.

// takeaways

What to remember

  1. Medical QA ceilings are real and large, and re-opening them is mostly a filtering problem, not a modeling one: the same GPT-4o that scores 88.2% on full MedQA drops to 32.2% on MedicalAgentsBench's 862-question hard subset of the exact same source datasets.
  2. Internalized reasoning models and externalized agent frameworks are not competing strategies — they compound. Bare o3-mini (27.8%) already beats most agent-augmented non-reasoning setups, and the paper's best score, o3-mini + MDAgents (35.1%), needs both routes at once.
  3. Accuracy-per-dollar depends on which lever you pull: cheap workflow optimization (GPT-4o-mini + SPO, 20.1%) anchors the budget end of the frontier, but heavier scaffolding on a weak base model isn't automatically worth it — GPT-4o-mini + MedAgents costs more than SPO while scoring lower (18.3%).
